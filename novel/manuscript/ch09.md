## Chapter 9 — Apothecary Blackwing

The stairs behind House Blackwing's herbarium went down farther than the house stood tall. Soren counted them out of habit, the way he counted everything: forty-one to a landing, a landing to the next, the gas jets shrinking and going green as the dark rose around them like water. Ilse walked ahead with the lamp. Her left sleeve was pinned at the wrist, the bandage beneath had seeped through twice since the raid, and she carried the light in her right hand without once reaching for a railing that was no longer there.

"Rules of the deeps," she said without turning. "Touch nothing that grows. Drink nothing you are offered. If you smell honey, walk the other way."

"What if it smells like honey and offers to carry my kit?" Dax asked from behind Soren, breathing hard.

"Then it is lying twice and you are only the third lie. Keep up."

"Why school a weapon at all?" Dax said. "Point it at something."

"Because at Ninth you will brew soon, Merrow, and brewing badly is how apothecary houses burn with everyone asleep inside them."

"I sleep very lightly."

"You will sleep lighter."

The last landing opened into the receiving hall of the Concord deeps: a vault of black waxwood ribs, a table down its spine long enough to lay out three bodies on. Seven chairs stood behind it, five were filled, and the ceiling lamps were hooded to a brown dusk, so the Leaves sat in half-dark the way their letters were written. At the center sat a gray man with kind eyes folded into gentle wrinkles. Primate Aldous Crane did not look up until Soren stopped at the chalk line on the floor, and then he looked the way a physician looks, all the way through.

"Soren Vale. Registered this morning at Ninth Essence. Predator, sworn and sealed." Crane folded his hands. "Ten days from corpse-cart to Codex. The Concord thanks you for coming down quietly. Some of our guests arrive fighting."

"I was carried," Soren said. "It seemed polite."

A brooch like a copper leaf caught the light at the far chair. Its wearer, a wide man with vinegar in his face, spoke without being addressed. "Ninth in ten days is not a boy's pace. It is a designed pace. Write it in the record and read it back to ourselves until we are properly frightened."

"It is written, Leaf Ostler," Crane said mildly. "Everything is written. That is the Concord's disease and its cure." He inclined his head to Soren. "You will study in the scribing hall. You will brew on a licensed bench and nowhere else. You will keep tallyman Vann between yourself and your own enthusiasm. And you will bring this house one honesty a day. That last is not a rule. It is a favor I am asking."

"And if I run out?"

"Then you will be an entirely ordinary member of the Concord," Crane said, "and we will all be relieved."

Someone down the table laughed and disguised it as a cough.

* * *

The scribing hall held ninety desks under brass lamps, and the air had a taste to it: iron-gall ink, clove oil, hot chalk, and beneath all of it the sweet-rot smell of Hollow stock stoppered in jars along the north wall. Chalkboards covered the end wall with spirals of notation, loops nested in loops, like the growth rings of a tree that had never stood on land. Students bent to their desks and recited under their breath, and the recitation made a sound Soren felt behind his sternum, where the Lacuna drifted and listened.

"Vale. The fighter. Good." Preceptor Ansel was a small man with chalk in his eyebrows and a voice pitched for the back row. "Fight after you understand. Not before." He laid one finger on the nearest spiral. "A tincture is three things. The beast, the bench, and the words. The beast is Hollow stock, rendered and stilled. The bench is license, glass, and heat. The words are a gene-script, memorized and spoken while the brew turns. The script is an instruction. It tells your passenger what to make of the meal."

"And without the words?"

"Then the brew does not fail." Ansel tapped the board twice, and chalk dust went up like pollen. "It improvises."

"Improvises," Dax said.

"There is an older word. Amalgam. A generation ago it was spoken loudly, and what it built is still behind sealed wards. You will memorize your scripts and speak them exactly, or you will join the syllabus." Ansel looked at them the way schoolmasters look when they have buried students. "Begin."

The novice script was sixty-one words, written to be spoken at the speed of a slow pulse. Soren recited it with the board in front of him, and at the ninth word his forearm prickled like a limb remembering frost; at the thirtieth the prickling climbed to the shoulder; at the last word the Lacuna rolled over behind his sternum, slow and enormous, a whale turning under ice.

"Sealed at Ninth," Ansel observed, "and it still turns its head when it hears its own alphabet. That will keep you humble when rank makes you stupid. Again."

Dax, who could not afford the fees and paid for most things by being useful, stood in the doorway and recited the script once from memory, grinning. Ansel pretended not to notice, which was how Dax got to keep noticing things for free.

"Who writes the scripts?" Soren asked.

"Apothecaries who hate mistakes more than they hate sleep. Every licensed script in this house is signed at its foot. Turn up any folio's colophon and you will find the scribe's mark." Ansel wiped his fingers on his apron. "Scripts are the only honest paper in Greyharbor, Vale. They confess their authors."

At the hall's far end, past the last row of desks, a glazed gallery had been cut into the rock: sealed wards, six of them, lit low. In the third, a young man sat at a desk like theirs, except his knuckles had gone to bark and his palms had grown into the desk's edge, and he was reciting his script wrong, patiently, wrong again, the way a man reads letters from someone who will not answer. In the fifth, a girl with one side of her face smooth as blown eggshell pressed her good cheek to the glass and watched them study.

"They recited badly?" Soren asked.

"They recited short," Ansel said. "Four words missing between them. That is all it takes. That is all it has ever taken." He shut the board's folding cover over the spirals. "Word count is life count. You of all people should appreciate a tally."

Soren appreciated it. He counted the wards again on his way out.

Six.

* * *

At dusk Ilse sent him up with a misfiled folio to return to the herbarium gallery, a two-story rack of dried specimens and ledgers one floor below the street. He took the servant's stair by mistake, a narrow turn the chalk maps of the deeps did not bother to mark, and the stair ended in a corridor instead of a room.

The corridor was wrong before he could say why. It smelled of pressed flowers, down here where nothing had ever grown, and the smell went through him like a hand through a coat pocket. Brass lamps burned at intervals with their hoods turned low. At the corridor's end stood double doors of black waxwood, and every seam of them was flooded with amber sealing wax, and pressed into the wax at every seam, in triplicate, was a seal: a ring around a seven-lobed sprig.

He knew it. He had seen it blotted onto seed packets in a tin on the kitchen sill, stamped into the ends of her letters, pressed into jam lids with a thimble while she hummed. His mother had signed with it the way other people signed their names.

His feet had carried him close before he decided anything. The wax was warm through his glove, warm like a living thing, and the seal drank the lamplight and gave none of it back, and behind his sternum the Lacuna leaned toward the doors the way a plant leans at a window.

Behind him Ilse's brass counter clicked once and went still. She had counted the corridor twice. It was on neither count.

"That passage is on no map I hold," she said quietly. "Whose seal is it, Vale?"

The lamps hissed. The wax held its warmth like a held breath.

His mother's mark.
