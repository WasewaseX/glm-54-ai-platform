## Chapter 18 — The Hungry Canopy

The truce was signed on the back of a requisition form, which everyone agreed was fitting.

Four days after Ropewalk went into the purse, the harbor watch and what remained of the Concord met under the customs-house clock with militia clerks between them and signed terms while the fog leaned on the windows. No Concord blade inside the militia lines without a watcher. No militia patrol past the cordon without a tallyman. Rations shared at the checkpoint on Chandler's Bridge, cooked once, eaten twice, because Captain Serle of the harbor watch had a horror of waste and Ilse Vann had a horror of error and the two of them got along like twin auditors.

"Your people breed monsters," Serle said to Soren, across the chalk lines of the checkpoint, "and my people die of it. But you keep honest books, and I have not been able to say that of the Concord before. So: the truce holds until one of us makes it not hold. Sit down. Tell me what you need."

"Boats that don't creak," Soren said. "Lamps with shutters. And your worst swimmers, the ones you'd otherwise flog. I'll make them rich."

"Rich in what?"

"Alive."

Behind Serle, the morning queue came up from the Ropewalk edge: people who had slept a street away from the purse and woken outside it. A fish-porter with his hand still closed around a door key. Two children asleep on a barrow, holding between them a birdcage, empty, its little door swinging. Nobody in the queue said Ropewalk. They said the fog took the streets, or the tide came up wrong, or nothing at all, and Serle fed them from the shared pot, because arithmetic is arithmetic whoever does the sums.

The Hollow's expansion had a tide to it now, measured nightly by lantern-buoys and daring boys with chalk. The canopy had come down from the Ropewalk scar and spread along the harbor like grease along a pan, and where its shadow crossed, things bred: hush-hounds in the timber-yards, lantern-mites in the flooded basements, gallows-walkers striding the crane-lines at dusk, elegant and horrible, harvesting the drowned.

Soren ran the harvest-cells off a slate propped on a hogshead, and the slate looked, exactly, like the mortuary intake board he had kept for six years, because habits are the last thing the Fade takes and he intended to keep his.

"Cell One, the ropery cellar. Mites, three swarms, shallow water. Cell Two, the slipway, hounds, night work, quiet voices. Cell Three," and here he tapped the board, "with me. The crane-lines. There's a walker feeding on the pilings and it has eaten eleven sailors that I have counted, and I have counted twice."

He chose cell leaders the way a mortician chooses order of work, because his Organ let him. The kill-sense sat in him like a cold card-player's eye: any room, any crowd, and he knew which heartbeat would stop first. It was a terrible gift for parties and an excellent one for triage. The men whose hearts were not scheduled, he made leaders. The ones whose hearts were, he kept beside him, where he could spend himself before the Hollow could spend them. He never told them. He told Ilse, once, at two in the morning, and she had counted to four and said: "Then you will sleep less than any of us. Good. You owe the most."

Serle watched him post the board for a week before she said anything. "You run this like a receiving ward."

"I run it like a receiving ward because it is one," Soren said. "The wounded are only later in arriving, and the Hollow is faster than canal fever, and it never signs the book."

* * *

The crane-line harvest went wrong in the ordinary way, which is to say it went right until it very suddenly didn't.

The gallows-walker came off the crane-top with its limbs folding like a scaffolding deciding to be an animal, and it was fast in the way of things that have never once been prey. Soren let it. Predator Codex ran in him now as a grammar: read, feint, spend. He spent. The kill-sense put its death in his hands half a heartbeat before he arrived, and his hands, mortuary hands, careful hands, did what they were grown for. The walker came down into the black water in three pieces and its spilled shadow curled up off the surface like steam.

"Drink," he told his cells, hoarse. "The wounded first. Leaders last. I'm last."

He drank last and least, out of six years' habit and one year's principle, but the night was fat with essence, the whole harbor like a dropped purse, and what he took went down and down and found that there was further down.

The fever took him on the cots at the checkpoint, four days in, and it was the worst yet. He burned. His bones felt rewritten in someone else's handwriting. He dreamed in seams: every doorway in Greyharbor opening into every other, the whole city folded up like linen in a drawer, and something behind his sternum learning, patiently, the trick of the folds.

Ilse sat with him through it because her rule was that no gravid of hers went into the fire alone. She did not hold his hand, there being half of one and him being past knowing. She counted. "Eighty-four," she said, at some black hour. "Eighty-five. I knew a woman who climbed three tiers in a autumn and lived to be old and insufferable. She said the trick is to be too stubborn to be interesting." Click, went the counter. "You will have to work at being dull, Vale."

The burning was nothing beside the dreams. He lay in the drawer of the city, between folded streets, and something enormous and patient turned the pages of him, and stopped, and stopped again, at the thin ones, the ones a boy keeps under the floor of himself: a stove's warmth, soap, a laugh through the nose.

What the fever took, he discovered on the eighth day, when he reached, as he did every morning, for the small hoard he kept of his mother. Face: gone since the Slate Market debt. That hole he knew the shape of. But the voice, the voice had been his, held close, unspent.

He stood in the checkpoint kitchen with a tin cup going cold and tried to hear her say his name, and there was a room in his memory where the sound should be, furnished, warmed, lived in, and silent.

"Ilse," he said. "The fever last week. The Fade collects a tithe, doesn't it. Off every tier."

"It does."

"It took her voice. I have her letters. I have the watch. I know she laughed through her nose when I was being clever, because I wrote that down once, thank every salt. But I can't hear her." He turned the cup. "I've lost her face and now her voice. I'm running out of mother."

Ilse looked at him a long moment. Then she took her counter out and clicked it, once, gently, as if for the memory itself. "Then we will be your hearing, boy. Say her letters aloud to me on Sundays and I will hold them twice."

* * *

He tested the new Organ in the cellar under the customs house, where two Folds had been charted eight strides apart, one into the ropery dark, one into a flooded stair.

No door. No script. No tincture. He put his palm against the empty air and pushed, the way you push against a scab, and the seam split. Going through a Fold-step felt like being a page turned by someone else: one pressure, one flick of darkness, and the second cellar was around him, water-nipped and real, eight strides from where his heart still stood.

He did it four times, then sat down on a wet step and laughed until it turned into something else.

"Again," Ilse said, from the far cellar, having counted his absence. "Twice more. I want the number."

He gave her the number. Then, because the harbor never let a man keep a good hour, they climbed to the dockline before dawn to walk the pickets, and the fog was high, and the canopy had grown so low over the water that its fronds dragged the mastheads, shedding spores that glowed faintly as they drowned.

The whole picket line heard it first as a sound like the rigging in weather. Then it resolved.

It was the canopy, whispering along the dockline, a slow tide of it passing from mouth to mouth of the galls overhead, and it was not random. It had shape. It had a name in it, over and over, patient as rain.

Soren. Soren Vale.

Seven years of nightmare, and the thing above the docks had chosen a voice, and the voice it wore, soft, amused, saying his name the way a woman says it to a boy who has just been clever, was the voice the Fade had taken from him nine days before.

Down the picket line a militiaman took salt from his pocket and threw it over his shoulder, an old tide-superstition from before anyone had needed a new one. The sergeant of the watch whispered the noon prayer at midnight. Ilse clicked her counter twice and put it away, which was her version of both.

He stood on the dockline with his hands very still and counted his own heart, twice, and could not make the count come out even.
