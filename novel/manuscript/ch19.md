## Chapter 19 — Siege of Blackwing

The Court of Lanterns came for House Blackwing at dusk, and it came like a navy.

Soren saw the first carriages from the tar-house roof: chitin hulls the size of skiffs, drawn on six jointed legs, moving through the harbor fog without any sound that fog was allowed to keep. Behind them, rank on rank, came the thralls in their pearl-gray lacquered coats, and above the whole advance the lantern-mites flew in ribbons, and where the ribbons touched the house's ward-lamps the lamps went out one by one, eaten.

The alarm bells began, then stopped beginning. A pack of hush-hounds had come up the canal and crouched along the wall with their mouths open, and the sound of the bells was pouring into those mouths. Soren felt it go. He had lived three tiers now inside a body built for this and it still raised the hair on his arms: a city losing its own voice.

"Positions," Ilse called, and her counter clicked like a crocodile. "Count your blades, count your powder, count your exits. Twice."

The house was half empty because the house had cut itself in half eleven days before, and every Purist and Amalgamist who had marched out with a torn roster was one fewer body at these windows now. Soren ran the defense the only way he knew, off the intake board in his head. Kill-sense swept the courtyard and the quay and gave him the schedule of the night, and he spent the defending dead one hour ahead of dying.

The thralls came up the west wall in silence, because the hush-hounds had swallowed the shouting, and the defense had to be fought in hand-signs and whistle-codes. Soren called targets from the gallery by touching shoulders in order. The tall one with the lit gut, next. The pair on the granary roof, not yet. His Organ laid the night out like a duty roster, and he worked down it, and the working was calm, and the calm frightened him more than the thralls did.

The strangeness came at the third hour. The Court had numbers to crack the house flat and knew it, and did not crack it. The thralls pressed hard at the west dormers, at the granary wall, at the cellar mouths, and never at the archive, never at the vaults where the Seeds slept under his mother's seal. They fought, Soren thought, the way a crowd fights toward a ticket window.

"Count their presses," Ilse said beside him, reloading with her teeth and her one hand. "Nobody fights this badly on purpose unless they are not fighting to win."

"Then what are they fighting for?"

"Something under us, and I want very much to be wrong."

* * *

He found the answer at the sally-gate.

There was an old Fold-door in the granary wall, charted, chained, and warded, a postern the house's founders had cut through the world's skin for sorties into the Hollow. Soren came around the granary's blind side on a rumor of wrong weather and found the chain coiled on the cobbles and the Fold standing open, a seam of night in the wall with the fog bending into it like water into a drain.

Dax Merrow stood beside it with the gate-timber in both hands.

"Dax." Soren kept his voice level, kept his hands level, because the boy was a hairline from shattering and a crowd of thralls was already coming through the seam, soft-booted, patient, pouring into the under-croft. "Step away from the door."

"I owe them," Dax said. The words came out of him like teeth being pulled, one at a time. "A year of brews, Soren. Off my own bench, after hours, sold to a buyer who paid in perfume, and perfume is their paper, perfume is their coin, and every vial was a signature and every signature was a promise that some day they'd come to collect, and today they came." His thumb worked at his left wrist, where a mark showed now under the torn sleeve: a ring of pale script, written in the skin itself, sweet-smelling. "My mother's rent is paid in it. My mother's RENT, Soren. And the terms are one door, open, held. And if I don't hold it, they take her. Not her life. They don't want lives. They want her under the canopy, drinking, the way the seed-stock drinks."

"You opened a door into your own house."

"You think I don't know what I've done?" And Dax turned to look at the under-croft, where the thralls were working, and stopped looking, and looked anyway.

They were not killing. That was the thing. The wounded who had been dragged down from the west dormers lay netted in gauze that glowed from inside, and they were alive, all of them, breathing shallow and slow, and their faces, one by one, were becoming beautiful. Peaceful. The nets drank. The thralls hauled the nets on long poles, gentle as men moving beehives, toward the open Fold, and one thrall's chest-lantern was lit and in its glow Soren could see faces swimming, faces it had taken somewhere, smiling out at nothing.

Dax made a sound Soren had never heard from him, from anyone.

Then he ran, and Soren thought for one white moment that he was running for the Fold, for escape, and was already composing the arithmetic of his hatred. But Dax ran the other way. Dax ran for the still-room, where a year of unsanctioned tinctures and two hundred quarts of burning spirit stood racked and caged, and he came out the far door dragging the fire-cart with the lamps swinging, and he drove it up the ramp into the belly of the Court's boarding-line.

The boarding-lines were the Court's true assault: living spars of chitin, bent and grown from the carriage hulls to the dormer sills, bridges for the pearl-coats to cross dry and quick. Dax put the fire-cart under the thick of the nearest spar, pulled the lamp off its hook, and looked back along the ramp at Soren, and grinned, the real grin, the quick one, the one from before all of it.

"Tell her it's paid," he said, and threw the lamp.

The spirit went up the living spar like a shout down a rope. Chitin burns, it turns out, from the inside, where the animal still is; the line lit end to end and the fog went rose-colored and the pearl-coats on it dropped or burned or fell, and the second spar caught from the first, and the withdrawal horn was sounding before Dax even staggered.

He was burning. He stayed by the gate-timber anyway, holding the sally-gate's hinge-block wedged so the Fold could not be shut and the fire could keep working through the Court's lines, and he was burning when Soren got to him, and he pressed something into Soren's scorched hand and closed Soren's fingers over it with both of his own, and said, "Count it all, Soren. Count it all," in a voice mostly smoke.

Then he went back into the fire to cut the last spar free of the house, and the spar came down, and he did not.

Serle's watch came up at first light with tar-masks and hoses and found nothing left to fight, and stood among the burnt nets with their caps off. Ilse went down the under-croft with her counter clicking, low and even, over and over, and Soren did not ask what she was counting, because some counts are private, and hers had a brother in it already.

* * *

The Court sounded recall at dawn, in good order, with the house breached and bleeding and plainly theirs if they had wanted it. The carriages withdrew into the fog at parade step. The hush-hounds trotted after, fat with the city's stolen morning bells.

Soren sat in the burnt under-croft among the empty nets with Ilse, who had lost two fingers' worth of voice to a near miss and was being ferociously calm. In his palm lay a wedding ring, worn thin, warm from the boy's hand, and a page folded to the size of a coin. Dax's own ledger hand, neat under the bravado: all items, one year, MARKED PAID IN FULL.

On the charred breast of a dead thrall, Soren found a brand he half knew. He had seen it in the stolen Court ledger, a glyph his sleepless nights had only half translated, the phrase built around it like a scar around a nail. He found the page. The translation he had was crude and now it did not need to be better: ROOTGATE, and beneath it, in Court script: keel-anchor. Do not wake the keeper.

He laid the ledger beside the ring and made himself say the count out loud, because saying it made it true and true was the only thing left to hold.

"They never touched the vaults. They pressed for the deeps under the house. They recalled at the moment they'd won." Ilse's counter clicked once, twice, the way she counted anything she meant to carry. "They were never trying to take Blackwing," Soren said. "They were buying time. All night, for something at the Rootgate."
