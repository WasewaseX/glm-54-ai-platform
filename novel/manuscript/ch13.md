## Chapter 13 — Whispers in the Slate Market

He could not demand answers from men who owned the doors, so he went where answers were sold by weight. The choice was not brave. It was arithmetic. The sky had been brewed on a licensed bench by a scribe forty years dead or hiding, a corridor under the Concord smelled of his mother, and the Primate's kindness had no floor he could stand on. Soren was Ninth Essence with a single organ to his name and a Fade that had begun taking faces. Whatever came for Greyharbor next, he could not meet it at Ninth.

"If you must be a fool," Ilse said, when he told her, "be a polite one. The Slate Market opens when the fish exchange closes. And ask for Marrow. Marrow never lies." She clicked her counter once. "He also never finishes the truth. Count his sentences before you sign one."

The Brine Stair went down behind the fish exchange through forty years of scales ground into the steps, and at the bottom, where the tide used to reach, the old auction hall opened up under a bell that had not rung since the Sporefall. Stalls crowded the waterline. Memory vials hung in graded racks, amber to black, with prices chalked by the year of the feeling. Next door a vendor sold false tinctures, corked hope in pretty glass, warranted genuine by a wax stamp of nobody at all. Down the row a Mummer leaned against a pillar wearing a borrowed face two sizes too young for it, and looked at Soren with eyes that were shopping. Soren showed it a face worth keeping: the mortuary's flattest professional calm, a man here on unpleasant necessary business. The Mummer's borrowed smile widened by a fraction, filing him.

The market had rules chalked above the bell. Show no face you cannot lose. All debts are literal. No names but the ones you sell.

Marrow's stall was not the largest but it was the best lit, a counter of black slate with a jeweler's scales on it, and behind the scales stood a gray man with a gray coat of many pockets and the cheerful patience of a man who has heard every opening offer ever invented.

"The tallyman's new ninth," Marrow said. "Or should I say the widow Vale's boy. You have your mother's walk. She bargained like a subsidy and paid like a fine. Sit. Everything costs. Sitting costs nothing tonight, in honor of your grief."

"You know why I am here."

"You want to know who moves Moltwater in my city. Galls and honey and flint under it, the Court's own toilet water, sold in my market only to gentlemen who buy it for other gentlemen." Marrow turned the scales' brass weight over with one finger. "That answer is expensive, love. But I will discount it against something you carry, because I have been waiting years for you to walk in looking exactly like that. The sea. Whole and unspoiled. You were born on the water side of the harbor and I can hear it in you still."

"I do not follow."

"You carry a memory of the sea, boy. The real one. Storm mornings, the fleet going out, gull-clatter, your mother's hand around yours on the pier rail." Marrow smiled with real warmth, which was the worst thing about him. "It is the last unspoiled one in Greyharbor under the age of thirty. I have a client who collects exactly that. The trade is this: the sea, and in exchange, the answer you came for. Plus a bonus, because I like you."

The bonus was in his palm before Soren agreed to anything: a thumb of glass stoppered with wax, and inside it a liquid so dark it did not reflect the lamp so much as drink it. The vial was cold through his glove and heavier than glass had any right to be.

"First Essence," Marrow said. "Bottled before you were whelped. There are four phials of it in the world I cannot account for, and this is one, and I am giving it away, which should frighten you more than the price."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you keep the sea, the answer, and your tier, and one night soon the fog will ask you what you are prepared to be, and you will have nothing in your pocket to show it." Marrow set the scales square. "I never lie, love. I am only ever early."

"Why give me the discount?"

"Because debts like company," Marrow said. "And because a ninth who climbs angry does the market more business than a ninth who climbs careful."

* * *

The tap took place on a stool behind the stall, in a stall of red curtains that smelled of wax and camphor.

Marrow's rig was a thimble of glass on a silver thread, a needle no thicker than a whisker, and a brass cylinder that turned as it filled. The needle went in at the temple and there was no pain, only a sensation of being read. The bell filled with light the color of harbor water. Soren watched his own sea go up the thread: the storm morning, the fleet out, the gulls fighting over a dropped roll, and her hand, cold and ringed and certain, around his on the pier rail.

Then it was in the cylinder and the cylinder was corked, and Marrow was wrapping it in wool like a sweetmeat.

"Do you want to keep the facts?" Marrow asked, professional to the last. "The word, the dates, the names of boats? The client buys the feeling, I can leave you the ledger entry of it."

"No," Soren said. And then, because Marrow never lied and he wanted to hear the shape of it: "What will it be like?"

"Like every coat you ever gave away," Marrow said. "You will remember that you owned it."

He was right. Afterward Soren knew the word sea the way he knew the word chisel. Facts stood in a row and reported for duty: born waterside, pier rail, gulls. The feeling that had made the facts into a morning was gone, sold, corked, and wrapped. He touched his own temple and found no hole.

* * *

He burned the vial in Marrow's back room, on a slate counter, with Marrow supervising from a safe distance and a fire bucket to hand.

"You will scream," Marrow said. "Mind the crockery. It is not mine but I have grown fond of it."

First Essence did not go down like the Fields' stolen meal. It went down like a mine shaft. Soren's knees went first, then his sight, and behind his sternum the Lacuna opened so wide he heard his own blood find new channels, and the fever came and went in the time it takes a kettle to sing. He did scream. The crockery survived. When he stood up, the room's pipe-smoke had turned to glass.

He could see through it. Not past it, through it, every coil and ghost of it lit from within like paper at a window, and beyond the smoke the room's other air was legible too, the damp furred along the wall seams, the heat bleeding off the stove in slow banners. Greyharbor's fog, the fog that had hung for seven years over every street he loved, abruptly stopped being a wall. It had never been a wall. It had only ever been waiting for eyes that could read it. The new hunger arrived on schedule, a wanting with no object, an appetite for whatever was hidden. He caught himself staring at the seams of Marrow's coat, counting its pockets.

"Smoke-sight," Marrow said, and for once the gray cheer was subdued, almost respectful. "Eighth Essence. New organ, new hunger, and you will feel the hunger tonight, love. It wants the obscured. You will catch yourself watching people's closed hands." He held out the fire bucket, unnecessarily. "Congratulations. You are now worth kidnapping."

"What I came for," Soren said. His voice came out sanded. "Moltwater."

Marrow opened his ledger on the counter, a book bound in gray, and wrote the receipt in a hand as neat as money. The book lay open as he wrote, and the standing entries ran down the page in monthly rows, and Soren had stopped being able to look away before he finished deciding to.

Moltwater, one phial. Pale Row. Twelve entries, one to a month, twelve months unbroken, and against every line the same initials in the same unhurried hand.

"You sell to the Concord?" Soren said.

"I sell to gentlemen." Marrow blotted the page, gray and cheerful. "He does not wear it. Nobody wears Moltwater to bed. That is a man buying a conversation, love. He has been buying the same one for a year."

P.C. Twelve entries. Pale Row. The Primate's lodge.
