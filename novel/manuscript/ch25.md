## Chapter 25 — Betrayal at the Gate

They made camp on the rim of the nest and nobody slept much. Drill teams collared the outer root-crown with saltfire charges, ring after ring, and the engineers chalked their readings and redrew them and chalked them again, because the readings kept coming back curved. When the lanterns guttered, the woven walls showed faces. Every face asleep. Every face breathing. Soren stopped looking after the third one, which wore an expression he had last seen on bodies he had washed.

Ilse's counter ticked at his hip. He had stopped flinching at it. He suspected he would never stop listening.

Thirty-eight of them held the rim, and the number was a wound that walked with the column. Corwin, the Chirurgeon walker who had held a stranger's arm together at the third mile, worked down the charge-lines with his sleeves rolled, sealing each collar of saltfire to the root-crown, and between collars he talked, because the man had never met a silence he could not improve. "She held the door," he said, to no one, for the ninth time. "Thirty-eight of us owe her the rest of our lives, and I intend to be pedantic about collecting."

The tide came at what the engineers swore was dawn. Light came down the root-road the color of a struck match, and with it came the sound of many boots on root, and then the Court itself, in all its unbearable elegance: chitin carriages rolling like clockwork beetles, molted knights with limbs of smoke-dark glass, pikes of lantern-crystal held at an angle so disciplined it looked rehearsed for a century. The Court's elegance was the elegance of a thing that had never once been hungry in public, and the banners over it were threadbare in the way of flags that had lately stood at funerals.

Above them, on poles, flew the gray wing of House Blackwing.

The cheer started in the Concord lines and died there, unfinished, like a man walking into a door he had opened a thousand times. Those were Concord banners. Those were Concord banners above the Court of Lanterns, at the gate the Concord had sworn to burn.

Brack said, in the voice of a man reading out his own death warrant: "That's our wing. Why in the deep is our wing over their heads?"

Primate Aldous Crane rode at the head of the tide, travel-stained, kind-eyed, his primate's chain bright against a dust-gray coat. Beside his stirrup walked a figure a head taller than the knights, robed in lantern-glass panels, its face a lit window with a shadow passing behind it. The herald did not announce itself. The road announced it: every root the column crossed straightened slightly, the way a spine straightens before a judge.

The herald unrolled a letter sealed in black wax, and Soren knew the sigil before anyone said a word, because he had grown up under that sigil and seen it on his mother's vanished work. Blackwing's wing. Beneath it, Crane's own signet.

"By covenant," the herald said, and its voice was quiet and enormous, like a name read from a ledger. "The Ascension shall be managed. The Warden shall be fed by schedule. The city shall be fed to the Warden by order: the fog-touched first, then the debt-bound, then the remainder of the sixth district, thence by district and by density. The Concord shall administer the count. The Court shall guarantee the ration. Greyharbor shall continue as a garden. Indefinitely."

The word order went through the Concord camp like cold water through cloth. Soren found he had walked forward without deciding to, and that Crane had walked out to meet him, and that the two of them were alone in a moving island of silence.

"You'd feed them in order," Soren said. "The poor first."

"By exposure, not by purse." Crane's eyes were as kind as ever, which was the horror of them. "Soren. I have signed the burial orders of four thousand people whose names I made myself learn. What is coming otherwise is a house fire with the doors barred. I have chosen a hearth. A managed fire warms. An unmanaged fire eats a city in a night, and then eats the county, and then there is no one left to forgive me."

"And the poor are first because?"

"Because the fog took their district already. Because they are nearest. Because someone must be first, and I would sooner it were the people the city forgot than the people the city counts." Crane said it like a man laying a flower on a grave, and it was arithmetic, and both things were true at once, and Soren understood for the first time that this was how granite convictions worked: they did not feel like falling. They felt like standing still.

"And Dax Merrow?" Soren said. "Sixth district. Debt-bound. Nineteen. Which ledger did he go in?"

"The levy went out under my seal," Crane said, and did not look away, because granite does not look away. "I signed the paper that pressed his mother's rent into the Court's hands. If I had known what the paper was for, I would have signed it anyway, and slower, and hated myself in a more comfortable chair. That is the whole of my confession, Soren. I am not sorry in the way you want. I am sorry in the way that builds schedules."

The herald's voice rose again, and this time it said the thing that ended the world's usefulness.

"The Warden sets its crown aside. The Seventh Seed shall sit it. The first citizen of the canopy. Let the Concord bring its Seed to the gate, that the covenant may be completed."

Every face on the rim turned toward him. The Court's, because it was their covenant. The Concord's, because half of them had never been told what he was, and you could watch the arithmetic happen behind their eyes and go wrong. The molted knights came on in a slow crescent, pikes level, unhurried, courteous as undertakers. Crane's tallymen raised their count-irons. Half the expedition's own tallymen raised theirs in answer, and the two halves looked at each other across thirty feet of root and seven years of shared graves, and a knight, patiently, cut down a tallyman who hesitated, and after that nobody hesitated, they simply chose.

Corwin's count-irons came up and then, with a motion Soren would remember longer than most of his memories, went down again, and the Chirurgeon turned his back on his own code's hierarchy to stand over two burn-team men who had chosen nothing yet. The count of Soren's friends got very short and very bright.

"Take him gently," Crane said, to no one and everyone, his tears running freely now, his hands folded like a man at prayer. "He is to be wed to a god, not grieved."

"Now that," said a voice like perfume given a mind, "is the single vulgar sentence of your career."

Lady Wren had not left the first carriage. She leaned from its chitin door as from a box at the opera, fan furled, unmoving, and her court around her was very carefully not taking part in the crescent. "My court is not paid in you, little seed," she said, and behind the boredom something was awake and fast. "And I owe your mother's face one kindness, though she would deny it to my face and enjoy doing it. Run."

Soren counted what he had. The burn-teams pinned behind the knights. Corwin bleeding but standing. Ilse's counter ticking at his hip, her absent hand steadying his. The nest's walls behind him, and every face in the weave awake now, and every face turned to him, and beneath them all a pulse like the sea under the world.

He reached for the count of his allies and found it short by exactly one, and the one was permanent.

He spent his last look the way Ilse had taught him to spend everything: deliberately, in pairs, twice. Once for the charge-collars that would not be fired now. Once for Corwin's turned back. Then the second count, because certainty lived there: the nest behind him, awake, holding its thousand faces up toward him like lamps.

Soren opened a Fold. No door. No iron ring, no hymn-chalk, no permission from any banner or any god. That was the Seventh Essence's one free gift, and it had already cost him more than the Concord's whole treasury would admit to on paper. Somewhere behind him Crane was saying his name the way men say prices, and the herald's window-face was turning, and Wren was saying good, softly, in a language made of scent.

He stepped out of the battle, alone, toward the nest, and the war shut behind him like water over a coin.
