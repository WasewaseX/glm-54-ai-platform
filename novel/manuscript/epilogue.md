## Epilogue — The Overseas Letter

Vesperquay had not worn fog in a hundred and forty years. It was a warm-water port, all white stone and terraced gardens, where the harbor lay at night like a spilled field of stars and the sea came up the streets in a whisper instead of a roar. Apothecaries ran the place the way other ports ran navies, and the oldest of the apothecary houses was House Ondrey, keeper of the far signal, and on the top floor of the Ondrey signal-tower a vial of luminous water had sat dark on a velvet shelf for forty years.

Mirren Ondrey, nineteen, ink to the second knuckle, youngest of the Ondrey line and keeper of the signal by virtue of being the only one willing to climb the stairs at night, was re-trimming a lamp when the bloom-glass woke.

It did not blaze. It sang. A small gray-gold note, the color of rain deciding to be light, and the vial filled from the bottom up like a glass being poured by a patient hand, and the whole tower smelled suddenly of a garden after lightning. Mirren stood very still and counted her own heartbeats, twice, which was a habit the house taught its children along with letters.

Then she ran down four flights and woke her grandmother.

Grandmother Signe came up the tower stairs at two in the morning with a shawl over her nightdress and no surprise on her face at all, which frightened Mirren more than surprise would have. The old woman stood before the singing vial for the length of one long breath, listening to it the way one listens to a voice rather than a sound.

"There is a case," Signe said. "There has always been a case. It came to my grandmother's keeping with the founding correspondence, before your mother's mother was a rumor. Founder's instruction, in her own hand, sealed to the lid: open when the bloom sings. My grandmother's grandmother, and her grandmother before her, and every one of them told the next: the house does not own this. The house keeps it. Go and wake the cellar-warden, child. Tonight the keeping ends."

The case lay in the deep vault behind three locks and a law, a box of black walnut the length of a forearm, sealed all around in black wax. On the lid, pressed deep into the wax by a signet worn smooth with use, was a single glyph: a seven-lobed bloom drawn in one unbroken stroke, the kind of line a hand makes only after a lifetime of practice.

Mirren had seen it before, on the spine of the oldest ledger in the house, and had been told, as all the children were told, that it was nobody's sign and everybody's inheritance, and not to ask.

They broke the wax at the long worktable with the whole house standing around in nightclothes and candlelight, and nobody spoke, which in a house of apothecaries is the loudest thing that can happen. Inside, packed in wool a century old, was a lattice of gray root-glass curled tight as a fern furled for winter, and it was warm. Warm through the wool, warm against Mirren's palms, beating with a pulse so slow and small you had to trust it rather than feel it, and alive, unmistakably, gorgeously alive.

Beneath it lay a letter, the ink brown, the paper cold as the cellar stone and just as patient. Her grandmother held it to the lamp, and read the first lines aloud, and her voice, which had sworn in three languages and buried two husbands, went unsteady on the fourth word.

"To the house that kept the light. If the bloom has answered, then it learned to give instead of take, and everything I was afraid of was worth being afraid of, and everything I planted is worth what it cost. My children were made to be planted, not spent. Plant me gently. — M.V."

Nobody asked who M.V. had been. Every face around the table had understood, in the way of people who have spent their lives reading small signs in large darknesses, that the letter had traveled further than any of them would, and had been waiting longer than any of them had been alive, and that the waiting had just ended.

Mirren lifted the lattice into the lamplight. It turned. Slowly, the way a flower turns that has never seen a window before, it opened toward the warmth, unfurling one gray-gold coil, and then another, and out in the harbor of Vesperquay the water lay star-still under a sky with no fog in it anywhere, and had not had any for a hundred and forty years, and none, Mirren understood now, looking at the small warm thing unfolding in her hands, that a seed had not already promised to lift.

"Count twice," her grandmother said, with the last of the black wax still on her thumb, and there were tears on the old woman's face and she was laughing.

Mirren counted. The seed was warm. It opened like a hand.
