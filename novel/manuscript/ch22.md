## Chapter 22 — The Warden's Voice

He did not sleep in beds anymore; he slept near seams. The Circle's healers would have called that a symptom. Soren called it hygiene: if the deep thing wanted him, it could come to the front door he chose, and he would be awake for it.

On the third night after the mudflat, in the cellar of the mortuary, with a charted Fold breathing on the far wall and the ring and the watch both under his hand, the Warden did exactly that. It came as a dream, and the dream was Greyharbor.

Ilse had posted her conditions at the top of the stairs, where conditions belong. Two hours, like the garden. If he did not walk out of the dream by two, she would wake him with harbor water and the good salts, and if he woke screaming she wanted it in counts, not colors. He had agreed to all of it. Then he had gone down and made the charted Fold comfortable, the way you make up a guest bed for a guest you do not trust.

He walked up Cinder Lane in it, crooked kerbstone, blue door, no street name. Grown over. Mossed bone underfoot instead of cobbles, glasswing moths instead of gulls, and every doorway on the street hung open like a slack mouth, and through each doorway was not a room but more street, folded, the whole city tucked into itself like linen. Above it all was the canopy, near enough now to touch the chimneys, and the light that came down through it was green and kind and wrong.

"You keep your books well," said the Warden, from behind him, in no voice and then in one.

He turned, and his mother was standing under the gall-lantern at the end of the lane.

The face arrived before the horror did. That was the cruelty of it: first came the simple animal relief of seeing, after these weeks of a hole in his own head, the exact set of her brows, the freckles he had once counted across the bridge of her nose for something to do while she signed a burial ledger, the way her mouth went on one side before it laughed through the nose. Every detail matched, because every detail had been taken from him and spent here. She was dressed as she had dressed for evening shifts, in the green coat with the ink on the cuff. Her shadow did not match her. Her shadow was the size of a city.

"You are not her," Soren said. His voice held. He was almost proud of it.

"I am what she is now," the borrowed voice said, mild. "Which is to say: I am her Tuesdays. Her laugh. The way she signed her name with the tail too long. Her face, which your Fade took off you and banked, and I have worn it since the Fever, little seed, before we ever met. You heard it whisper on your docks and knew it, and thought you were going mad. You were not. You were being spoken to by your own property."

His hand had gone to the watch without asking him. The Warden's borrowed eyes followed it, and the face it wore did something complicated and gentle. "It is still stopped," the Warden said. "Her watch, stopped since the night we took her. Did you never wonder, little seed, why a stopped watch ticks once, at the worst hour, in every dream you have ever had? You did wonder. You counted it. That is partly why we chose you."

It came closer, wearing her walk. Soren stood still and counted: one lantern, two moths, three doorways, four counts of his heart, and the arithmetic held the floor while the rest of him screamed.

"What do you want?"

"Succession." It said the word the way a courtier says tea. "There is a chair at the bottom of everything I am, and the one who sits in it is old, and tired, and starving, and has watched every world it ever loved close like a book. I offer you the chair. Be the mind of the canopy. You count things; you would not believe what there is to count. Keep Greyharbor as the garden it is. Feed it gently, on the willingly forgetting, the old, the finished. No nets. You have my word, and my word is made of the promises of ten million people, and it is the only honest mass in either world."

"And my city keeps its own mind. Its own, what, its own everything."

"They keep their lives. Inside, and tended, and immortal in the way of orchards." Maerwen's face tilted. "You have seen my alternative, Soren Vale. You have stood in my under-crofts. The nets are not the King's cruelty. The nets are what happens when my patience runs out, and my patience is running out at the speed of a starving thing."

"And me," Soren said. "The part of me that is Soren Vale. The part that keeps the books. What becomes of it?"

"It keeps the books," the Warden said. "That is the honest horror of the chair, Soren Vale, and I will not hide it from you because hiding is not in my nature. Nothing is lost. Not one Tuesday of anyone, ever again. The man does not die. He is outvoted, forever, by everything he is asked to save."

"There's a third reading."

"There is always a third reading. Your mother wrote one, in fact. I have it here." It touched its borrowed temple. "She wanted to heal me. You will want to ask how that ends. I will not spoil her ending. It is the only thing I own that I have never eaten."

Soren's hand had found the ring in his pocket without his permission. Dax's mother's ring, warm from a dead boy's hand, and the Warden's borrowed eyes went to the pocket, and something crossed the face it wore that on a human face would have been called respect.

"You sold the sea," it said.

Weeks ago. A cellar of vials, a gray-cheerful fence, a price he had paid because he was drowning and it was the only coin he had. "I did."

"We bought it at second hand. Memories are currency, and all currency comes home to the bank." Maerwen's mouth smiled with the exact off-center tilt. "Would you like to hear it? I keep it well. It has been sung to, all this time, by people who drowned in it."

And it gave him the sea. Not the memory of it: the sea itself, restored, poured into the dream from wherever his Tuesdays of salt had been banking all this time. Gull-cry. The slap of halyards. His own young boots on wet stone, and the smell, the smell that the Fade had left a hole the shape of. It went into him like water into a man on fire, and he made a sound he did not authorize, and the Warden stood politely in Maerwen's shape and watched him cry with his own sold sea.

"Why," he said, when he could speak. "Why give any of it back?"

"Because it is yours, and we keep honest books, and because a man who has tasted what he sold makes a better bargain than a man starving for it. That is not mercy, Soren Vale. That is strategy wearing mercy's coat. We learned the trick of coats from your kind."

"I hate you," he said, when he could.

"Yes. It is one of the nicer things I have been given lately." The dream dimmed around them, the lane folding itself away like a fan. "You will not take the chair tonight. You are young, and grief is still doing your arithmetic. But hear the terms of the war you have chosen, because I am honest in the mass, if never in the part. You would starve me rather than feed me. So understand what you are starving, and when you have understood it, come to the bottom of everything and sit down."

"Show me what you are," Soren said. "Not the face. The books. Show me the ledger, since you're so proud of keeping honest ones."

The Warden considered him with his mother's eyes.

"This is not an argument," it said, and the last of the lane went dark behind them. "This is a receipt. You asked for the books. Every honest ledger has a first entry, and ours is dated seven years back, on the night your city calls the Sporefall. It is entered from my side of the page."

Then it gave him the ledger. The dream peeled open like a hand uncurling, and he was standing beneath the canopy at the bottom of the world, seven years deep, looking up through the membrane of somewhere at a city seen from the underside of the night: Greyharbor, far off and small, burning with its lamps, every window a grain of light.

And above it, between the city and the skin of the sky, a cloud was turning. Luminous. Patient. Beginning, with the whole slow weight of a starving god behind it, to fall.
