## Chapter 11 — Dax's Bargain

What spilled from the spliced when the burn took them, Soren drank. He had been starving, the Fields had rung his hunger like a struck goblet, and the Circle ordered the findings rendered anyway, so he knelt in the incinerator room and did what the Lacuna made him and hated it efficiently, in counts, the way he hated everything he could not put down. The fever arrived two nights later, riding in on the tail of that stolen meal. Between its waves, he went down to the recruits' boiler room, because Dax had proctor's rounds to dodge and Soren owed him a cover.

The boiler room belonged to no roster. Its pipes had been condemned a decade ago and its warmth had been annexed by every recruit with a secret: letters, gin, one memorable ferret. Tonight it held a still assembled from a tea urn, a church window's worth of salvaged glass, and copper tubing bent with more hope than skill, and it held Dax Merrow, sleeves rolled, tongue between his teeth, feeding a jar of rendered Hollow stock into the turn with the tenderness of a man feeding his grandmother.

"Proctor's round in four minutes," Soren said.

"Four. Good." Dax did not look up. "Nine little bottles, Vale. Nine. If he finds one, I lose the bench, the fees, and the skin off my knuckles."

"What are they?"

"Courage for the harvest-cells, steadiness for the watch rota, sleep for the archivists. All of it harmless. All of it brewed on an unlicensed bench, which makes it a crime the way breathing through the wrong window is a crime." Dax wiped the condensation from a gauge with his shirt hem. "Sanctioned tinctures cost what my mother makes in a month. Unsanctioned cost me scruple, and I have budgeted for that."

Above them the corridor door shuffled, and keys sang, and Soren was already moving. He met Proctor Gedd at the head of the stair with a mortuary tray held out level, on it a lamp gone to black and a jar of something gray and pickled, and his face arranged to the precise temperature of bad news.

"Seepage from the herbarium stack," Soren said. "Condensate into the wick-store. I need your signature on a contamination docket before I can vent this floor, proctor, and if the venting goes in the log unsigned, it is your name the Circle reads at quarter review."

Gedd's face did the arithmetic of a man weighing a smell against paperwork. Paperwork won. He signed against the wall, fled the corridor, and Soren was back down the stairs before the keys stopped singing.

"Contamination docket," Dax said, admiring.

"There is no docket. There is no wick-store. He was too afraid of quarter review to check." Soren set the tray on the boiler. "You were saying nine."

"Nine." Dax pulled one small bottle from the rack and turned it to the lamp. "It is not the crime I mind. It is the reason. Eleven crowns a quarter for our rooms on Coil Street, and rising since the Sporefall, because the landlord says the fog eats his slates. My mother takes in mending. Her hands are going; she drops the small needles now and laughs about it." He put the bottle back as gently as an egg. "Rent is not a number, Vale. It is a countdown."

"Who buys?"

"A collector."

"Collectors of what?"

"Steadiness, courage, sleep." Dax grinned, and the grin did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "You cover me, I cut you in. Ten percent."

"I do not want your ten percent."

"Everyone says that the first time."

* * *

The fever took him that night in the little room over the mortuary cart-shed, and it was not like the growth fevers the others had described, fire and climbing. It was cold. It was a tide going out through him, wave by wave, and each wave went out along a different shore.

The Lacuna stood wide open behind his sternum, gorge-lit, drinking, and the Fade came to collect in its patient order.

It had already taken smells. He had learned that after the last fever without noticing the lesson: he could no longer find her soap in memory, the oilcloth smell of the kitchen, the green bite of stems under her thumb-nail. Rooms he could keep. Dates he could keep. The smell of her had gone to the Hollow months before he knew there was a war for it.

Tonight the tide went out along her face.

He stood in the shallow dark of the fever and reached for it the way you reach for a stair in a house you have lived in all your life, and the stair was not there. He had the brow. He had the laugh-lines, the particular way one corner of her mouth went up before the rest decided to. He had flour once on her cheekbone in the yellow kitchen light. The parts were all in his hands and the center would not close over them, and he stood there holding pieces of her while the fever breathed.

He lit the lamp. He took charcoal and paper like a man taking tools to a wound, and he drew her, and the drawing was of a stranger. Kind mouth, maybe. Tired eyes, maybe. Maybe was all that was left, and maybe is not a face.

The watch was in his palm without his remembering taking it out, her stopped pocket-watch, brass gone soft at the edges from her pocket and then his. He opened it. The hands stood at a quarter to midnight where they had stood for seven years. He still had the watch. He could describe the watch to a jeweler across a table, the worn crest, the chain's kink at the second link. The watch was a face he could keep, and her face was not, and somewhere out under the city the Hollow now wore her at its leisure, because the Hollow was built of exactly this, taken by exactly this tide.

"Maerwen Vale," he said aloud, to the little room.

The name still held weight. The name still held warmth, a hearth-radiation, a gravity. The name was a door with nothing behind it that he could see.

He sat on the floor with his back against the bed until the fever's tide turned, and he did not sleep, and in the morning Dax found the drawing and looked at it and looked at him and put it face down with a gentleness that was worse than any comment.

"Fade took the kindest first," Soren said. "She warned me it would."

Dax said nothing clever. He went out and came back with bread and butter and a hard-boiled egg and put them in front of Soren and said, "Eat. The Fade eats you, you eat back. That is the whole of my philosophy and it has kept me beautifully alive."

* * *

Two nights later the buyer came.

Soren heard the soft boots on the stair, a man walking as if the stair were owed courtesy, and stepped behind the water tank with his heart going like a trapped bird. The boiler light laid stripes across the stranger: storm-gray coat, gloves, a collar that had never known fog. He counted the little bottles without touching them, nodded once, and paid Dax in crowns counted out onto the boiler's warm brick, nine of them, each set down with a click like Ilse's counter.

"For the inconvenience," the buyer said, and added a tenth object, not a coin.

It was a crystal phial the length of a thumb, sealed with a drop of black wax, and pressed into the wax was the imprint of a lantern. Even sealed, it breathed. Galls and honey, and under that something like struck flint, a scent Soren had smelled exactly once before, drifting down from a rooftop during curfew, folded into a calling card addressed to the Seventh.

Dax took it the way people take tips from their betters, pleased and careful, not looking at it too hard.

The buyer turned to go. In passing, gloved fingers rested a moment on Soren's shoulder, light as a toll paid, and the soft boots went up the stairs and away, and the boiler room was just a boiler room again.

Soren stood in the warm dark and did his sums, and the sums came out wrong in a way he did not have numbers for yet.

Court perfume. The Court was already paying Dax.
