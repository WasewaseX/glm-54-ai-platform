## Chapter 20 — Lady Wren's Garden

The invitation came the evening after the siege, which was either an insult or an apology, and with Lady Wren the two were the same currency.

It arrived at the checkpoint under militia seal, because everything crossed the truce lines under militia seal now: a gall the size of a plum, grown on a twig of black wood, and when Soren took it in his palm it opened like a lantern and spoke in a voice made of perfume, which was a thing he had hoped never to hear again.

"The garden on the drowned bell-tower, midnight. No debts are called in my garden, little seed. Come alone. Wear something you can burn."

"You will not go alone," Ilse said.

"She'll expect me alone."

"Then she has already won the first exchange, and I want you to hear that from me before you go, so that after, when you are counting, you count it too." Ilse clicked the counter, once for the order, once for the fear she was not going to admit to. "Midnight. Two hours. At one minute past two hours I come in after you, and I will burn her garden, and it will start a war, and that will be your fault for being late."

"You'd never make it through her door."

"No," Ilse agreed, placid as a stone. "But she would know I tried, and knowing it will cost her something. Everything costs them something. That is the whole of their religion."

Soren said nothing. He went and got his coat, and into the left pocket he put the stopped watch, and into the right the ring that had come out of a dead boy's hand. At the checkpoint door he stopped and told Ilse where his books were kept. Cellar, third flagstone, under the tarpaulin. Ilse said that was morbid. Then she wrote it down.

* * *

The drowned bell-tower stood off the harbor's mouth, a stump at low tide, a rumor at high, and its Fold was not hidden at all. Wren had hung it with lantern-galls, dozens of them, so that the entrance looked like a doorway into a wedding. Soren went through with smoke-sight banked and his hands empty and found a garden where a bell-room should have been.

The bell was still there, or had been grown into. It hung in its frame the way a heart hangs in a chest, webbed over with pale wood, and when the wind came off the harbor the whole tower hummed through it, one note, enormous and patient, more felt than heard.

Terraces spiraled up the inside of the tower's ghost, all of it grown: galls in tiers like fruit, pale vines with leaves of thin chitin, glasswing moths drifting between the lanterns, drinking, he supposed, whatever dreams the moths found there. Wren sat at a table of bone-smooth wood pouring something amber into two glasses, and she was beautiful the way a knife is beautiful, which is to say the matter was settled and only the use remained.

"You kept my card," she said, nodding at his pocket, where the calling-card from the anniversary night still lived because he kept his enemies' debts the way Ilse kept counts.

"I keep evidence."

"You keep grief in an organized way. It's your mother in you. Sit."

He sat, because the chair was there and the truce was real and the amber, when he finally drank, tasted of quinine and pears and made the Lacuna behind his sternum lie back down like a growling dog. Poisoned, no. Drugged, she would consider rude.

"You saw my court's work last night," Wren said. "The nets. You are doing arithmetic about it, and I am going to save you the labor, because I am honest, and being honest is the most frightening thing about me. The Hollow is starving, Soren. It has been starving since before you were born."

"Greyharbor begs to differ."

"Greyharbor is a meal. Meals do not testify about the diner's health." She turned her glass. "Consider the diet. The Hollow eats memory, and only memory, and it must eat or die, the same as you. Once, in the world it came from, there were cities beyond counting, centuries deep, whole civilizations of remembering. Then that world closed, the way a book closes. Your Sporefall was not an attack. It was a lifeboat. The Warden was drowning, and it poured a measure of itself across the skin of the world into the richest tide-pool it could see, and the tide-pool was Greyharbor, and we have been feeding ever since."

"Seven years of feasting."

"Seven years of famine with garnish." Her voice stayed level, pleasant, terrible. "Do the sums. The dead take their memories with them into us, but the dead of a small city are a small harvest. The living forget slowly, and hoard what they don't forget, and fear has made them thrifty. Every year there is less in Greyharbor to eat. Every year the canopy goes hungrier. Ropewalk was not a victory, whatever my colleagues' carriages said about it. Ropewalk was a man eating his own boot."

"And the King?" Soren said. "The one nobody sees. Where does the Lantern King eat?"

Wren was quiet for a moment, and in the quiet the tower hummed its one note. "My king does not eat. My king is, if anything, eaten. No. I will not say that tonight, on a full stomach of your distrust. He was the first of us and he will be the last, and his silence is not absence, whatever the Grand Canopy pretends. Ask me again when you have met him. Or when he has met you."

"So the nets."

"So the nets. My court's plan, the King's plan, is the garden. Bring humanity under the canopy, whole and managed and bred for yield. Not slaughtered, no. Potted. Every soul a permanent, renewable little spring of memory, dreaming its little life over and over under our leaves, forever." She smiled, and it was the worst thing in the garden. "We do not want your city broken, Soren. We want it planted."

"One more item for the audit, then," Soren said. "You told me once my mother is inventory. I have had weeks to wonder what that word does in your ledgers."

"Held," Wren said. "Not spent. In a starving house, little seed, inventory is the most guarded thing there is. Ask me the rest of that question in a different season, when you can afford the answer."

The fog moved in him, anger, old and cold. He put it on the scale where he put everything. "Ilse's people netted in your under-croft were not volunteering for the orchard."

"No. And that is why my court is noble and my court is damned, and I have stopped trying to resolve it. But hear the last sum, because you will not sleep without it." Wren set down her glass, and for the first time since he had known her she looked, briefly, like someone else's clerk. "There is a faction in your Concord that finds the garden plan reasonable. On a schedule. Gently. With the poor going first, because the poor, the arithmetic says, will be missed least."

"You're lying."

"I never lie; it ruins the flavor. A Primate of your Veridian Concord has been in negotiation with my court for a controlled ascension. Corrals, quotas, a tithe of souls a season, and in exchange, the canopy holds still and the city keeps breathing. The terms are drafted. The perfumes have been flowing to him for a year." She watched him arrive at it, watched him arrive one year back, to a ledger page in a market cellar, and she nodded slowly, as one does to a student.

"And a word of trade, since you are a buyer of truths now. You do not starve a canopy by pruning its branches. File that where you file things you do not yet understand."

"You'll tell me his name."

"I will not." She rose, and the galls dimmed around her as if dimming were polite. "Names are how your kind hunts, and I have need of both your hunters. You will want it to be a monster, little seed. It is worse. It is a man doing arithmetic, and his convictions are granite, and granite cracks along its own grain. That is the last honest thing I will give you tonight, and it is the one that will cost you the most sleep."
