## Chapter 3 — Greyharbor Below

The lamp lost its third shade, and Soren stopped being a naturalist.

He took the fire-iron from the hearth in both hands and put his back to the wall, and when he tried to shout for Mrs. Ferrow through the floor, the shout left his mouth and did not come back, and he felt it go the way a man feels a coin leave his hand in a crowd. The dark in the corner stood up. It stood up all at once, the way a cat stands, with no intermediate stages, and it was taller than the wardrobe and patient as arithmetic, and where its eyes were, two coals that had been quenched in something colder than water looked at him with professional interest. It had no face that his eyes agreed to record. Looking straight at it gave him an impression of long limbs folded like a carpenter's rule, and a head that was mostly hinge.

The wallpaper split. Not torn: split, along a seam that had never been printed, the pattern peeling back from a line of cold, and behind the plaster there was no lath and no brick. There was a gape, breathing cellar-air and crushed flowers, and the hound poured itself through it the way night pours under a door, and the split stayed open, exhaling.

Through the floor, from somewhere below, Mrs. Ferrow's cat began to shriek.

Soren did his counting, one to ten, twice. Then he took the storm lantern off its hook, turned the wick low, and stepped through the wall of his own room, because a house with a hound in it does not stay a house, and because the hunger behind his sternum had risen up like a dog that has heard its lead.

* * *

Passage was one cold stride, like stepping through the skin of a window from the warm side of the world to the other. Then he was outside, under a sky, and the sky was a canopy.

Grennel Street went down and down before him, and it was Grennel Street, door for door, and it was not Grennel Street at all. Overhead, tiers of shelf-fungus stacked themselves into a vault higher than any cathedral, rust-brown and bone-pale, sifting a slow snow of spores that fell without hurry, as if the whole roof were yawning. The eleven doors of his street were still eleven, and every one of them was a mouth now, grown shut with nacre and veined at the hinges. The chandler's shop had become a heap of pale fruiting bodies stacked in the shape of a shop, honestly shaped, mockingly shaped. The cobbles were bone. Vertebrae and skulls and long anonymous bones, all of it mossed green and worn smooth, laid in courses like a street some enormous mason had loved once.

His lantern was the only light, and the light was wrong. It arrived everywhere a half-second late. Sound was worse: when his boot pressed the bone cobbles there was no click, only a soft arrival of click afterward, like a servant following a master at a distance.

Lantern-mites came out of the canopy to graze around the glass, a drifting constellation of green sparks, each one the size of a grain of barley, and they did not touch the lantern. They hovered at the edge of its light, feeding on what leaked, and he understood, with his new organ's cold opinion, that they were livestock, and the light was pasture.

He counted the doors as he moved, eleven, and it steadied him the way it always steadied him, right up until the count became eleven mouths, and every mouth opened slightly as he passed, in greeting or in appetite.

The hound hunted him by echo. He learned its method over three streets of mossed bone: it sent a sound ahead of itself, a stolen sound, and watched what the sound did to him. Once it threw his own voice down an alley, saying, in his voice, with his fear in it, "Who's there." Once it let him hear Mrs. Ferrow's kettle, at boiling, in a street where there had never been a kitchen. It wanted him running. Running things are loud.

So he stopped running, and counted, one to ten, twice, and made his fear small, because it hunted fear the way his new hunger hunted light, and a small fear is harder to smell. Then he backed to the end of the street, where the drowned chapel had stood before the Sporefall, its yard grown through the fence like a green wave, and he took the spade that was leaning there, moss to the shoulder, blade pitted but true.

The hound came for him at the gate of the dead.

It came low and wrong, joints unfolding in the wrong order, and it stole his shout again, mid-throat, and that was its mistake, because a man who cannot scream does not need to. Soren side-stepped behind the gatepost, let the weight of it pass, and swung the spade not at the body but at the space the body was about to agree to occupy. The heel of the blade caught the skull where the ear would have been on anything honest. He dropped his knee onto the neck and drove down with everything the trade had built into his back, and the sound the hound made was the sound of a wet book closing.

Then the world got its voice back. Every stolen sound came home at once, out of the dying thing like air out of a lung: his shout, whole and furious, and Mrs. Ferrow's kettle, and the cat, and a night's worth of small noises, a wash of them rushing outward across the mossed bone. Soren knelt on the carcass, breathing, counting his own heartbeats, and the carcass went on dying in a way he had never seen anything die. It deflated. Not bled out: leaked out, as if the body had only ever been a coat hung on something else, and the something else was escaping through the seams.

The escaped thing was black. It pooled around his knees like mercury, and it did not spread. It gathered, flowing gently uphill, against the tilt of the street, toward him.

He was so hungry.

The hunger was not like appetite. It was like standing in a drought looking at a well. He told himself, in the ledger voice, that he did not know what it would do, and the black shadow-stuff reached his boot and climbed the leather like a kind tide, and the well said: yes.

He put his hands in it and drank.

It tasted of burnt hair and frost. Under those, clear as a watermark, it tasted of fear, the hound's own, the flavor of a thing that had hunted ten thousand years and been counted at the end, and the taste went into him and he knew it, and knew that knowing it was going to be the shape of his life now. The world rearranged itself around the meal. The spore-snow stopped falling: it hung. The mites' grazing became audible, a field of small green teeth. The dark everywhere acquired depth, became a country with districts, and in that country he could feel his own small light the way a lit window feels to a street.

If anything down here kept books, and something did, a new line had been written under his name. He had no way to read it yet. The line said Tenth.

He went to find the way back and could not. The split in the air where he had entered had healed like a mouth after a scream, without a scar. He walked the street end to end and counted the doors, and the count was twelve, and one of the twelve was swollen in exactly the shape of his own room's window, down to the bubble in the pane where Mrs. Ferrow's cat liked to sit. He stood in front of his own room wearing another street's architecture and made himself use the ledger voice. Subject's location: not negotiable. Subject's condition: fed. Subject's options: count them. He counted. The options came to one: walk until the street remembered he was a paying tenant somewhere else.

The hunger did not die. It rose from the meal the way a diner rises from a first course, and looked down the length of the table, and found it good.

Soren got up off the dead hound with the spade in one hand and its shadow drying black on his knuckles, and turned to look for the split in the world he had come through, and that was when the applause began. Slow. Dry. Courteous. Each clap arriving a half-second after its hands met, which meant the clapper was very tall, and very far off the ground, and did not mind that he knew it.

On the balcony of the swollen townhouse opposite, among knotted woody galls the size of hams, something folded itself upright, joint by elegant joint, and kept clapping for him.
