## Chapter 8 — First Essence Burned

The safehouse was under the mortuary, which had been Greech's idea, or Greech's price, depending which Concord clerk you asked. The old plague laying-out hall beneath Salt Row had tables of bolted iron and a history of receiving things the city preferred not to mention, and for seven years the Veridian Concord had stored its Greyharbor archive there: crates of sealed glass, ledgers bound in fish-skin, a rack of tinctures graded by tier, and one long crate with no label that Ilse would not let him open.

"Catalogue, don't pry," she said that night, chalk in hand, ticking the rack. "Curious gravid die of curiosity and the house buries the question with them. Tinctures, four through nine: read them off."

He read. It was past second bell, the fog was close on the street above, and the undercroft smelled of camphor, glass, and his own family's trade. He had been at it an hour when his kill-sense, which had been resting quietly all evening like a cat in a laundry basket, opened all the way up.

Every heartbeat in the room was owed.

Ilse's. His own. It had never said his before.

"Vann," he said, and she was already turning, chalk still in her hand, and the counter in her pocket clicked, and clicked, and went to a run.

The iron-filing wards around the door guttered out one ring at a time, and the ramp down from the coal chute filled with footsteps that arrived a half-second late, and they came down into the lamplight: five of them, in nice coats, in dockside broadcloth, in one case in a bishop's grey, and every one of them was somebody. That was the horror of them. They had faces Soren knew from the parish ledger. They had pupils like lit wicks, and at every throat, under the jaw, a soft gold light beat like a lamp behind silk.

"Good evening," said the one in front, in the voice of a dockhand Soren had laid out in the spring, drowned off Ropewalk, twenty-six years old, no next of kin. The voice was kind. The mouth that made it was a stranger's mouth, and it smiled with great courtesy. "Forgive the hour. Her ladyship asks after the Seventh."

"The house," Ilse said, level, moving to put herself between the ramp and Soren, "keeps no Seventh."

"It does," the thrall said politely. "It has kept one since before he was born. But her ladyship is patient, and so are we, and we are only asked to deliver a greeting tonight. Stand aside, tallyman. This is the smallest of favors."

Ilse did not stand aside. Ilse did anything but stand aside.

Soren had seen her fight once, in the yard, drills with the porter, and had thought: quick, tidy. What she was under the lamp was something else, a counting of distances so fast it looked like prophecy. She broke the first thrall's knee with the chalk-holder's edge, took its wire-hand and used it, put a second one into the wall hard enough to crack the plaster through to brick. The third and fourth circled. The thrall-master watched from the foot of the ramp with its borrowed voice and its courteous hands, and between those hands, as the bishop-grey one came at her, it shook out a length of light.

The lantern-wire went through her guard like a bill through a shop window. It took her left hand at the wrist, clean, without a sound worth remembering.

The hand fell among the tincture crates. Ilse looked at it, one full second, the way she looked at everything, and then she was fighting one-handed, and winning the exchange, and screaming was something she had evidently decided against on principle. Her counter lay on the floor by his boot where it had fallen from her pocket, still clicking, faithful as a heart.

The thrall-master stepped past her toward Soren.

He had time to be afraid. Then the fear went through him and came out the other side organized, and the world slowed down the way the world does in dreams, and he understood what was happening to him because a part of him had been counting toward it since the canal. The essence of weeks of harvest rose in him like a tide finding its mark. His heartbeat and the second, smaller heartbeat behind his sternum, out of step since the night they met, came together on one count, and locked.

The provisional oath came back to him whole, finished by something older than his memory of it. Iron in his teeth. Iron filings and copper and rain. The Ninth.

And the Fade, prompt as any clerk, took its fee on the spot. He reached for something to steady himself with and found a hole with clean edges where a smell should be: the bakery on the harbor quay, morning, the smell of bread coming off the ovens across the water. He knew what was gone the way you know a lifted coin. He would never smell that bakery again. He filed the loss and kept his feet, because the kill-sense had not stopped, and the kill-sense was looking at the thrall-master.

That one is owed, it said, with the calm of arithmetic. Now.

The thrall-master was fast. It was not faster than a man with nothing left to lose standing between two old lifting tables. Soren went down before it, the way bodies go down in his trade, under its reach, and his hand found the lift pawl on the iron frame, the ratchet that holds a table's counterweight for the living and lets it go for the dead. The thrall-master bent to take him by the collar, courteous to the last, and Soren Vale, mortuary assistant, third-generation counter of other people's endings, released the brake.

The brass column came up like a verdict. It caught the thrall under the jaw with the whole stored weight of the counterbalance behind it, and the borrowed voice stopped being borrowed, stopped being anything, and the body went up and back and broke across the head-board with a sound like the wet book closing that Soren had heard once before, on Grennel Street, with a spade in his hands.

The light at its throat guttered and died. The wire of lantern-light fell from its hands and was only a length of pale cord.

In the sudden stillness, the remaining thralls stopped. They turned, all four, with the smooth simultaneity of weathervanes, and looked at him kneeling among the tables with his hands still on the iron, and their wick-lit eyes did something he would spend a long time failing to describe: they lowered. The way eyes lower to a rank.

"How good," said one, in a voice like a bell heard through a wall, "that her ladyship's letter found its house."

They went up the ramp together, unhurried, and the dark took the sound of them a half-second late, and the perfume of crushed flowers and cold wax hung in the undercroft like a signature.

* * *

Soren tied off what was left of Ilse's wrist with the tourniquet cord she told him how to tie, her voice level the whole time, correcting his grip once. He carried her up the stair to the parish kitchen, set her down by the stove, and gave her the brandy Greech kept for witnesses. Her left sleeve was a weight of blood. Her face was gray as the fog and perfectly arranged.

"Report," she said, before he could speak. "Thirteen minutes ago you were Tenth Essence. You are not Tenth now. Say what you are."

"Ninth," Soren said. His voice worked. That seemed like a lot to ask of it. "The Codex sealed. Predator. I felt it set like a bone."

"Then you are the fastest climb this house has logged in forty years, and every old man on the Circle of Leaves will now be glad of you and afraid of you in exactly that order." She looked at her own bound wrist and clicked the counter once against it with her right hand. "Ilse Vann, third circle. I am down one hand and up one novice. The house pays its debts. It will owe you a surgeon, a season of tincture, and more answers than it has ever given a novice, and I will be in the room when it gives them, because the Circle does not like to say the word sowed in front of witnesses, and I have just heard what your wallpaper asked you."

"You heard about that too."

"I count things, boy." For the first time since the wire, something moved in her face that was not bookkeeping. "Concord owes you answers. Take them. But hear the arithmetic underneath: the Court sent five and spent one to deliver a greeting. That is not an attack. That is a calling card laid on a grave, and graves mean courtship with her ladyship. She knows your name's number, Soren. She has known it longer than you have."

He sat with that, and with the brandy, and with the warm weight of the watch against his chest, ticking where it had never ticked.

He thought of the wall of the missing, and the seer's vine-script, and a tall thing applauding in the dark of his own street, and a lady who signed her letters in perfume. The Concord owed him answers, and he meant to collect them, every one, with a bailiff's interest.

But first, he owed the Court a visit.
