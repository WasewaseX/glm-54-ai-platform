## Chapter 29 — The New Codex

Nine days after the stilling, Greyharbor began handing things back, and the handing-back was stranger than the taking had been. An old woman on Sallow Court woke remembering the name of the street she had lived on for fifty years, which the fog had taken, and wept into her tea. A musician got back a tune his sister used to hum, all but the last bar, and walked the canal for a day trying doors in it. A docker remembered his daughter's face. A docker's wife remembered leaving. The re-seeded Hollow returned what it had hoarded, down the lines along which the Fade had collected, and the city spent the week flinching from its own gifts.

A lamplighter on Chain Row remembered his son's laugh, which he had buried without noticing the burial, and stood under his own lamps for an hour with one hand on the pole. The Circle posted a table in the fish exchange: what the Hollow had taken, what the re-seeding had returned, and a third column, honest and mostly empty, headed still out. Every morning there was a queue to read it like a tide table.

Soren went up for the arrest of Primate Aldous Crane because he had the veto now, and the veto meant being summoned to things.

They did it in the re-roofed hall of House Blackwing, in front of the re-formed Circle of Leaves, and they did it with Crane's own tallymen binding his wrists in count-cord, which was the Circle's idea of mercy or of poetry. Crane watched the knots being tied with the interest of a man checking another man's stitches. His kind eyes found Soren across the hall.

"I would have fed them in order," Crane said. "Order was the only mercy left in my hands. God forgive the arithmetic." He raised his bound hands, both of them, like a man asking a question in school. "One thing. When the ledgers are opened, count the ones I would have spent. Put the number in my cell where I can hear it. It will be lower than the number that would have been, and I would rather live with the smaller of my two evils than the larger of the world's."

No one answered him, which was an answer.

Soren stopped at the door, because there was one thing owed. "The number will be counted properly," he said. "By someone who counts twice." Crane nodded the way men nod at sentences that will keep them company in a small room.

Soren left before the chains came out, because he had counted enough, and some counting is only a way of watching.

He went up again, past the stilling canopy, to the crown, where the Molted Court was leaving. They went in a long glittering line of chitin carriages, lanterns dimmed to coals, up and out through Folds that led away from any city, to soil that was promised to nobody. Lady Wren waited for him at the last carriage with her fan furled and her face arranged into something almost like candor.

"The canopy will feed on memory given now, not flesh taken," she said. "My court was built on the old diet, little seed. We would waste here. Slowly, gorgeously, and to no purpose, and Wren does not waste. So: exile, above and away, by our own choice, before the New Codex makes us beggars with manners."

She looked out over the city from the crown, and the look was long, and she permitted it to be seen. "It is smaller than it believes and lovelier than it deserves," she said. "Keep both facts true, little seed, and the Concord will outlive my court's whole future reputation for generosity."

"Will you starve out there?"

"Wonderfully. But on our own accounts." She looked at him for a long moment, and the knife-honesty came out, as it always did, wound in courtesy. "Twice I offered you apprenticeship. I have never been gladder of a refusal. And since I am leaving: your mother court-spied for the Concord and paid her gin debts in bad verse, and I liked her enormously. This farewell is the only honest inheritance I have. Spend it well. Starving things become simple. Fed things become interesting. Keep your Concord interesting, or I will be back, and I will not be alone."

She stepped up onto the carriage's running-board, then turned, because Wren never left a debt uncalled even to remit it. "Give the god my regards," she said. "Tell it the Court is sorry. It will know what for. It keeps our oldest accounts."

The carriages took the light with them. The crown of the Hollow, for the first time since the Sporefall, was only a roof over a city, and it rained.

The Circle of Leaves re-formed around the New Codex in the third week: every ledger open, every corridor unsealed, every brew licensed twice, the giving-script chalked above the teaching benches where the first rule used to hang.

Students filed past the unsealed corridor all that week, touching the doorframe the way pilgrims touch the shoulder of a saint. Somewhere in the house a seer's forty-year-old message grew one new line of wallpaper that nobody could account for and nobody dared remove. The veto went into the founding ink in Soren's own hand, and he scribed it twice, and the registrar notarized both copies, because certainty lives in the second count. They offered Soren the primate's chain, because cities are grateful in the laziest possible way, with the heaviest object they own.

"I count things," Soren said. "Chains get counted around necks, and I've fitted enough of those in the mortuary. Take it back."

"Then what will you take?" asked the Leafmaster who had signed Dax's harvest warrants, and could not look at him while asking.

"A veto. No feeding, no sealed corridor, no Seed conscripted, no brew burned into any body without my consent. And the ledgers open to anyone who can read them."

"That is a small throne."

"It's a leash," Soren said, "with me on the far end. Take it or leave it."

They took it. Then they asked him, gently, whether he had noticed anything coming back, and he said he had, and the Circle had the good manners to pretend that was paperwork.

It came back at the mortuary sink, of all places, on an evening of no importance. Not from the outside in, the way memories return when a smell unbolts a door. From the inside, up through the sternum, through the Lacuna, along the re-seeded lines, from the Hollow's gift. Tired eyes. A mole under the left eye, like a period at the end of a sentence. Hair escaping its pin at the right temple, and the frown of a woman counting.

In his vest pocket, the stopped watch ticked. Once. Then again, and then went on, in the smallest voice in the world, keeping time for the first time in seven years.

"Hello, Mum," Soren said, and his voice did something embarrassing, and he let it.

The face was not her. It was the memory of her face, returned, and it was everything, and it was not enough, and he held it anyway, the way you hold a cup of something hot in winter: carefully, greedily, knowing it cools.

And under the face, like a foot pushed under a door, came the way she had said his name. Soren, with the o gone soft, as if it were being counted too. He kept the watch wound from that night on, out of a superstition he did not examine, and it never stopped again.

The Circle's registrar brought the Seed ledger to the mortuary two days later, because the veto meant the paperwork, and paperwork finds the man with the veto. Seven designed lineages, made before the Sporefall. Three burned in the Sporefall, names and dates and the registrar's small tidy notation. Two never bloomed: dormant lines, quiet, accounted. The Seventh, Soren Vale: present, accounted, obstructive. And the Sixth.

"File burned in the schism," the registrar said. "No harvest record, no bloom record. Whereabouts unknown."

The registrar dipped her pen and wrote the sixth line as it stood, and the ink dried in the lamplight like a door closing politely.

The registrar blew on the ink, packed her ledger, and paused at the mortuary door with her hat in her hands. "For what it is worth, Vale," she said, "the sixth line is the only one in that book that keeps me up at night. Dead lines rest. Unknown lines wait." She put her hat on and left him the silence, and the silence ticked.

Of the seven Seeds, six were accounted for.
