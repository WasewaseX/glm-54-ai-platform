## Chapter 28 — Greyharbor Rises

The Warden carried him up its own body like a thought, and for a quarter of an hour Soren saw Greyharbor the way a root sees it, from below and everywhere.

The dockline held. He watched it through the god's veins: the militia and the truce-armies in their oyster-shell sandbags, pikes and tally-irons and the old harbor guns hauled around to face the water as well as the sky, and above them the canopy, which had leaned on the city for a year like a collector leaning on a display case, going still. A root as thick as a barge sighed backward out of a warehouse wall it had pinned since spring, trailing moss, minding its manners. A soldier touched the withdrawing wood with one finger, snatched the finger back, and then, when the wood did nothing at all, touched it again and left it there. All along the dockline the pressure let go at once, like a fist unclenching, and the truce-bell rang half-notes, because the ringer was crying and could not manage the whole ones.

A militiawoman with a harbor-gun crew stood her post through all of it, because standing posts was what she had, and when the root finally let the warehouse go she marked the wall where it had been with a smudge of chalk, the way tallymen mark a debt paid, and the whole crew pretended not to see her do it. The tram wires, dead for a season, began to sing in the changed wind. Gulls came back over the docks in singles, then pairs, then in scandalous quantities, arguing about the return of the fishing.

Below, in the warm gray-gold core, the throne waited with the patience of furniture.

Soren put his hand on it once more, because a refusal should be made on the thing refused, and took it away.

"Kings get counted," he said. "I do the counting. Grow me a bench instead."

The Warden considered this for the space of a tide. "The city will make you its king anyway," it said. "Cities are lazy with their gratitude. They will put your face where the fog used to be."

"Then the fog is coming back," Soren said, "because that face is staying right here, behind a sink."

The Warden grew him a bench. It came up out of the floor through the night like a tooth through a gum: bone of the god's own rib, polished, slate-surfaced, with burners that burned blue spore-flame and racks that held glass at exactly the angle a scribing hand needs. The first bench the Hollow had ever licensed, and the licence was the god's own consent, witnessed by a cathedral of the dead. Soren ran his thumb along the slate and thought of House Blackwing's teaching benches, and of the first rule chalked above every one of them: a brew is a promise made in a body.

"Then we'll keep it," he said.

Three ingredients, and the first was the one no apothecary in history had ever used, because no apothecary had ever been given it. The Warden opened a vein of pale gold along its wall and gave. Not spilled, not harvested, not milked from some screaming thing in the dark. Gave, the way a host pours. Essence ran into the copper bowl and lay there glowing, and the Lacuna behind Soren's sternum went silent for the first time since the Sporefall, stunned, perhaps, or perhaps merely polite.

The second was the script. He scribed Maerwen's giving-script out of the sealed memory onto glass, in the old alphabet, and his hand cramped, and then the last third of it wrote itself while he watched, the way a word finishes when you finally stop interfering. Seven lobes. One stroke. A promise that the mouth is a bridge.

The bench wanted reagents, because benches do: a measure of harbor brine, boiled and cooled; the ash of spent saltfire, which burns root to a root that will not sprout, asked now, gently, to be a mineral and nothing angrier; and a grain of the Lantern King's perfumed debt out of a phial Marrow had sold him in a different life, surrendered at last to the only creditor who could call it in. He fed the desk the way the old teachers fed their desks, and the old teachers had never once known what it felt like to have the desk feed back.

The third was human memory, and this was the tithe that mattered, and he chose it like a man choosing which fingers to give.

Rain on slate. The exact sequence of the quarantine bells, rung wrong for seven years and now, maybe, rung wrong forever. Bread from the canal ovens at four in the morning. The first snow on the abattoir roof, and the specific silence of a city that has stopped to look at it. He poured them through the scribing desk into the brew, and each one went through him like a coin through a grate and came out the other side lighter. Ilse's tick and Dax's ring he kept out, and told the bench so, out loud. Some things are not currency.

The first New Codex tincture came off the bench gray-gold and luminous, smelling of rain and salt, and under that, faintly, of a struck match in a garden. Half of it he drank. It tasted like the smell of a childhood kitchen you cannot place. The other half the bench drank, root rising through slate, the god receiving from a human hand for the first time in its long and starved existence.

Something opened in him that had no name yet, and he felt the canopy's appetite the way skin feels weather: pressure dropping, hunger fronting over the city like a storm that had nowhere left to make landfall. Then the storm felt the new thing growing in the roots below it, and turned, and began to rain on itself. Down on the dockline, in the changed air, the first man to notice would only report, afterward, that the sky had stopped leaning.

The re-seeding began as a sound, or the memory of one: a chord resolving. Light ran up the roots of the world in slow rings, out along the miles of the Warden's body, into the canopy over Greyharbor, and the Hollow turned over like something waking and began, at last, to eat its own hunger. It had spent an age consuming outward. Now it consumed the appetite itself, courses of it, years of it, the whole groaning banquet of want that the Court had taught it and the Concord had starved, and the expansion did not die with a scream. It died with a sigh, the sigh of a held thing being put down.

In the walls, the faces changed their singing. Some of them, here and there among the thousands, stopped singing altogether. Not silenced. Released.

Far up the body of the god, at the crown of the nest, the throne he had refused put out one pale shoot toward the space where he had stood, found nothing to hold, and stopped. The Warden let it stand there a day, and then grew it, patiently, into a shelf for the brew-glass he had left behind, which was as close as anything in the Hollow had ever come to taking a hint.

And then, in the turning of the tide of memory, the Hollow began to give things back, down the lines along which they had been taken, the way a bank that has caught fire returns its deposits. Somewhere above, he knew, an old woman was getting her street back. Somewhere a musician a tune. The god had hoarded an age of takings and now it spent, and among the first of the coins it pressed into his hands, unasked, came one he recognized by weight alone.

Salt. Kelp. Gull-cry. The cold green light under gray water, and tar and rope and the slap of a hull against a breakwater he had stood on at fourteen with his trousers rolled, and could not, until this moment, have remembered to miss. He had sold the sea to Marrow for a vial of First Essence and thought the price fair, because a man dying of hunger does not haggle over scent.

The Warden had let him sip it once before, in a dream, the way a lender lets a debtor smell the coin, and the sip had faded by morning, as dreams do. That had been demonstration. Proof of holdings. This was the deed, signed over in full, principal and interest, and there was a difference, and the difference was the rest of his life.

The sea came home to him all at once, the whole of it, wind and all. His hands, hanging at his sides, made small motions of tying off a line he had not tied in nine years. He laughed once, which in the circumstances was the noise his crying had decided to make.

It smelled like the sea.
