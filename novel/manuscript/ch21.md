## Chapter 21 — The Seventh Seed Blooms

It began with the doors, two days after the garden.

Every door in the mortuary started to hum when Soren passed it, a low glass note like a wet finger on a rim. Then the doorways of the checkpoint. Then, embarrassingly, the doorway of Ilse's office, which had a beaded curtain, and which announced him at all hours like a shop bell. He put chalk in his pockets the way other men put tobacco and found himself standing in frames, head tilted, listening to the shape of the air.

"The books are very sure," Ilse said, watching him do it in the cellar. She had the page open in front of her, the Concord's own primer, printed in five inks. "No gravid opens a Fold. The Lacuna feeds, it grows Organs, it climbs the tiers, but the doors are the Hollow's. Even First Essence walks through doors someone else made. It is the one courtesy the enemy extends us, and you appear to be declining it."

"I haven't done it yet."

"You are standing in a doorframe at midnight counting its pulse. You have done it. You simply have not confessed."

The maturing had no tier to it; that was what was wrong with it. He was Seventh Essence and had been since the crane-line harvest, and the tiers came by ladder, one rung of hunger and one new Organ at a time, paid for in essence and fever. This was not a rung. This was the ladder itself flexing under him, the Lacuna behind his sternum gone from a seed to a thing with intentions, and its intention, every time he stood in a frame, was out.

He confessed on the fourth night, in the flooded stair cellar under the customs house, with Ilse counting and two charted Folds eight strides apart and a fire-axe leaned against the wall, because Ilse believed in arithmetic and in axes.

He put his palm flat to the empty brick between the two doorways and pushed, the way he had pushed once before, and this time there was no borrowed seam, no charted scar, no door at all. The world split. It parted the way old scar tissue parts, grudgingly, with a sensation like a held breath let go, and the dark beyond the split was the ropery cellar, eight strides and one world away, and his hand had made it.

"Well," said Ilse, who had gone very still. "Click twice if you can still hear me."

"Once for yes, twice for no," Soren said. "I invented that system. I know how to use it."

"Then yes, once," she said, "and once again, from me, for every textbook in this city that is now firewood," and she clicked her counter, and her face, for one unguarded second, was the face of a woman watching a new star get named after nobody she liked.

* * *

The overreach came at the Ropewalk cordon, five nights later, and it came because he was showing off, which Ilse predicted, in writing, beforehand.

They were moving a harvest cell through the fog wall, the old way, walking them out to the scar and through the Fold there, and the cell was green and the water crossing was deep and cold, and Soren thought: eight strides saved is eight strides safer. He opened his seam mid-shoal, took his cell's four hands in four of the cell's other four hands, and stepped.

The scar under Ropewalk was not like other Folds. It was not a door into the Hollow. It was a wound where the Hollow had come out, and a wound, he understood one heartbeat too late, runs both directions, and the thing at the bottom of that wound was not local.

He came out of the step alone. The cell stayed home; the Fold declined them; only he was taken, and where he was taken was deep.

The deep Hollow has no canopy, or the canopy is all there is. He stood on a root-road the width of a quay, grown of mossed bone and pale wood, and it ran out ahead of him until running out was the wrong idea for it. The road bent with the curvature of somewhere. On every side, to the edge of sight and past it, were faces.

They were set into the world the way plaques are set into walls. Thousands. Tens of thousands. Old men with harbor beards. Girls in mourning bonnets. A sailor he had prepped on his very first night shift, drowned and decent, recognizable because Soren's memory, unlike everything else in him, still kept its books. The whole harvest-team from the Mycelium Fields, all nine, ranked like a class portrait. A small face he knew from Ashlane, unspliced, whole, exactly as a child is before the world gets its hands in, and Soren's legs nearly went at that one.

And they were singing.

Each face its own song, low, and all the songs braided into one slow tide of sound that was not music the way rain is not weather but contains it. The sailor sang fishing. The team sang out numbers, a tally, the last work of their lives. The child sang a skipping rhyme, over and over, unhurried, certain of the rope.

They were the eaten. Not the dead, which Soren could have borne. The eaten: every memory the Fade had ever taken off this city and grown into the walls of its new owner's house. The Hollow was not built on their bones. It was built on their Tuesdays.

The Lacuna behind his sternum leaned toward the singing like a sunflower at noon, and Soren felt, with a clarity he would have paid to lose, how easy it would be to open his mouth and add his verse. The hunger for thresholds was in him now, and he was standing on the largest threshold in the world, and it was harmonized, and it had room.

He gripped the watch in his pocket and the ring beside it, one hard shape each, and counted the faces he could name, because counting was the rail he had left. One. Two. Nine. Eleven.

He got to forty-one.

The song stopped.

Mid-verse. The child's rope never hit the ground. Tens of thousands of throats, if they had throats, closed on the same breath, and the silence that followed was not empty, it was attentive, it was a silence with an ear in it, and then every face on the horizon of forgotten people turned to him at once, the way a courtroom turns, the way a pack turns, ten thousand pairs of eyes that did not need eyes arriving at him together.

He did not remember opening the seam home. His hands did it, the way a sleeper's hand finds an itch. The last thing he carried back was the very beginning of a second song, one new verse, sung by all of them in unison, in a voice that had forty-one names in it.

His name.

* * *

He came out on the mudflat under the cordon with Ilse's fist in his collar, hauling him out of a half-open seam like a gaffer landing an eel, and the world was dawn-colored and ordinary and smelled of tar.

"Talk," she said. "In counts."

"Deep Hollow. The Warden. I saw it. Or the edge of it. Ilse, they were all in the walls. Everyone we've lost, everyone the Fade ever took. Singing."

She went down on her heels in the mud in front of him, gripping his jaw with her whole hand, a field test, eyes, pupils, the lift of the lid. "And you are back. Good. What did it take off you? There is always a fare."

He checked, the way you probe a socket with your tongue. His childhood was all there: the stove's warmth, the soap-smell of the mortuary, his mother's letters. He walked the street of it in his head, the crooked one, the one with the kerbstone he had tripped on at six years old, outside the house with the blue door, on the street called

The street called.

"There's a hole," he said slowly. "Small one. I grew up somewhere, and I can walk every yard of it, and Ilse, it has no name. The street has no name. That's the fare? It took a street name?"

"Names come last," Ilse said quietly. "Scents first, then faces, then names. It is the order of the Fade, and it is also the order of how much a thing is loved." She stood, and pulled him up, and did not let go of his sleeve. "Something with ten thousand faces looked at my boy last night and paid for the view. Whatever it is growing toward, Vale, it has started wanting you by name."
