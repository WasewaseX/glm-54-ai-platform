## Chapter 10 — The Ledger of Flesh

The Fold under the render-yards opened out of a cold-storage wall, a seam of bruised light between hanging carcasses of frost. Ilse went through first with her lamp up. The joiner had fitted her walnut hand overnight, brass pins at the knuckles, and it carried the weight of her kit without complaint; she had already learned to crack its fingers like a man cracking a safe.

"Six tallymen," she said, "three days overdue. Sent down to mark essence-veins for the quarter survey. Names in the report, boots in my memory. We catalogue what we find and we touch nothing we cannot count."

"And if we can't count it?" Dax asked. He had the brewer's kit on his back and a miner's lamp hooked to his belt, and he had already charged Soren two crowns for the oil Soren would inevitably spill.

"Then we run," Ilse said, "and count while running."

The Fold folded them out under the Mycelium Fields. Soren had seen the Hollow's streets, mossed bone and roofless rooms, but the Fields were another order of country. Caps the size of cathedral vaults shouldered up out of the dark, their gills lit from beneath in slow pulses, and the light fell as a pale snow that drifted upward, not down. The ground gave like proofed dough. Somewhere far off, the wet clicked, a sound like many patient mouths.

His kill-sense opened as they walked, that pressure behind the breastbone that pointed at the heartbeat that would stop first. It pointed at nothing. He had learned the feel of that nothing in the deeps: here nothing would die first, because nothing here died. Things in the Fields accumulated.

Ilse worked the route the way she worked everything, in counts. Two hundred paces between way-marks, lamp raised, thumb resting on the brass wheel of her counter. The essence-veins the lost cell had been sent to chart showed for Soren as a slow brightness under the ground, rivers of the stuff the Hollow made instead of blood, and he marked their bends on the survey sheet while Dax held the light and complained about the damp in strict alphabetical order. It was, for twenty minutes, almost boring. He was learning to distrust boredom in the Hollow the way sailors distrust a flat sea.

They found the harvest-cell's gear before they found the cell. Six lamp-racks, racked neatly. Six rope coils, coiled neatly. Six brass tally badges laid out in a row on a shelf of fungus, polished, aligned, as though someone had tidied them after.

"Nobody flees and stores their ropes," Soren said.

"Nobody flees at all," Ilse said, and clicked her counter once. "Two hundred and eleven paces. The lean-to should be there."

The lean-to was grown, not built: ribs of mossed bone bent and lashed with mycelial cord into a low shelter around a cold hearth of fungus. Inside, in the gill-light, lay nine shapes.

Soren had laid out four hundred bodies in the mortuary and he had a word for each way a body stops being a person. There was no word for this. The first shape kept a human plan, a man's shoulders and a man's jaw, but his ribs were lacquered with gill-structure that opened and closed in a slow rhyme, breathing for him. The second was a woman whose back had grown a shelf of bracket-fungus in folded tiers, like wings pressed flat and dried. The third and fourth had been large men; something had spent them as material, lengthening the arms, splitting the hands into root-fingers that gripped the ground. The rest had gone further, plan dissolving, anatomy remembered only as a rumor. Brass tally badges glinted on all nine.

Polished, he saw. He rubbed one with his thumb and it came up bright, and the bright line ran in an arc, as if the badges had been worn in file and laid down in order, left to right, the way Ilse laid out instruments before a report.

Half of every face was serene. That was the part Soren's hands understood and his stomach refused.

"Six went down," Ilse said. Her voice was the flat one she used for reports. "I count nine shapes."

"Spliced," Dax said quietly. He was gray around the mouth. "Human stock and Hollow stock in one vessel. That is the Heresy, that is exactly the Heresy, and the Heresy is a generation dead."

"It is a week old," Ilse said. She knelt without touching. "Look at the gills. Dew still on them. Blood still wet under the lacquer on that one's arm. The Heresy is not dead, gentlemen. It has been paying its debts elsewhere and moved to town."

They worked the way the Concord had taught them to work a field find, and because the find was flesh, Soren led. The ledger of flesh: he went shape to shape with tags and a stylus, the mortuary in his hands even out here, noting what had been human, what had been taken, what had been added, and where the seams ran. The seams did not run. That was the horror he could not put a number to. Mortuary flesh parted along its injuries; this flesh had agreed with itself.

He wrote gauge and span, seam and join, in his careful ledger hand, because the hand was a rail and the rail was all there was. At the fourth shape he had to set the stylus down and count his own pulse twice, because the fourth shape wore a Concord signet ring gone green on a root-finger, and he had begun, without noticing, to number the shapes the way you number guests at a table you are setting.

"Nine bodies," he said at last, and stopped, because his kill-sense had finally found purchase.

It pointed, faintly, five times.

"Five heartbeats," he said. "Nine shapes. Five of them are alive."

Dax took two steps backward before his spine caught up with his manners. Ilse did not move at all. "Say the count again."

"Five."

They gathered at the lean-to's mouth with lamps shielded, and Soren felt for each heartbeat the way he had felt for the drowned man's pulse on his first shift, two fingers, one count. The first lived behind the gill-ribs, slow as a bell in sand. The second ticked in the wrong place, low right, where a liver ought to be. The third and fourth ran together, a two-beat rhythm in the twin shapes, like partners keeping time. The fifth was a flutter, far in, under the bracket-wings, a bird-heart's rhythm in a body built for ballast.

Five alive. Nine shapes. Six badges.

"Which six went down?" Dax whispered. "Which three came from somewhere else? What were the three?"

"Catalogue first," Ilse said. "Questions after. That is the discipline that keeps the questions answerable."

So Soren catalogued, and his stylus scratched, and the Fields clicked far away, and the upward snow fell on the lean-to's ribs and did not melt. He tagged, he noted, he measured gill-span and seam-width, and he did the work because the work was a rail to hold in the dark. The ledger of flesh filled page after page in his careful hand.

Somewhere in the fifth shape a drip fell, metronome-steady, into a pool that had no business being so regular. Dax counted it without meaning to, under his breath, one, two, three, and the count steadied him the way counts steadied Soren. "It is nothing," Dax said. "It is drainage." He said it twice, which in this house counted as a prayer.

He was writing the word badge for the sixth time when the nearest shape's mouth moved.

The jaw cracked open along a seam no jaw should have, a hinge of pale gristle, and what came out was not a moan, not a groan, none of the sounds the mortuary had taught him flesh makes when it is spent. What came out was numbers.

"Forty-one," it said. "Forty-two."

The voice was a man's voice, ordinary, worn, a little rough at the edges from smoke. It counted the way a workman counts, absent, even, a tally kept aloud to keep the hands honest. Soren's stylus stopped. Across the lean-to, Ilse had gone from living color to paper.

"Forty-three," the shape said. "Forty-four."

And Ilse's brass tally-counter clicked.

It clicked in her hand the way it clicked at every report, at every muster, at every body she had ever laid out, her double-count, her habit, her iron rule. Except she was not touching it. It sat on her belt, and it clicked forty-three, and it clicked forty-four, and the voice in the mouth grown mostly shut matched it beat for beat, the way a tune matches the instrument it was written for.

"Forty-five," said the shape.

The second twin spoke from the floor, lips Root-split, the same voice with the smile still in it, and the third, and under the bracket-wings the flutter-heart's shape moved its half serene face, and they were all of them counting, in chorus, patient as rain, in a voice Ilse Vann had buried with the first tide seven years ago.

Soren knew it too, then, from the way she stood. Tomas Vann. He had seen the name once, chalked under a photograph in her tally-room, a young man laughing with his collar up.

The counting paused. The whole lean-to paused with it, the gills, the pulses, the upward snow.

"Forty-six," said Tomas Vann's voice, gently, out of five mouths. "You are one behind, little sister."
