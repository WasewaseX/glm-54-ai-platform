## Chapter 23 — Gathering of Codices

The war-council met in the harbor watch's drill-hall because it was the only room in Greyharbor big enough for both armies' pride. Blades were peace-bonded at the door with militia wire, and Ilse Vann stood at the wire with her counter and counted the bondings, both ways, out loud, which took eleven minutes and set the tone of the evening exactly as she intended.

Primate Aldous Crane presided from a militia chair that sat lower than the Concord's own, a choice of his, Soren was fairly sure, made for the look of it. Around the long tables: four Leaves of the rump Circle, Captain Serle with her officer-cadre, the harvest-cell leaders with their slates, and two sanctioned Chirurgeons who between them had most of the city's remaining calm.

Soren reported first, because Ilse volunteered him and volunteering was her way of checking his spine.

He gave them the garden. The galls, the terraces, the amber glass. He gave them the sums as Wren had dealt them: a starving biosphere, the Sporefall as a lifeboat, the plan to bring humanity under the canopy as seed-stock, potted and permanent. He gave them the last of it flat and unlovely: a Concord Primate had been in negotiation with the Court for a controlled ascension, a tithe of souls on a schedule, the poor first. He did not name Crane, because he had no name to give.

The hall did what halls do. Serle's officers went quiet in the way of people recalculating their own lists. A Leaf named Hollis demanded to know, red-faced, how Soren had come by enemy intelligence, and whether enemy hospitality came with obligations, and Soren let him finish.

"It came with a bill, Councillor, like everything. I paid it in sitting still while she insulted my city with perfect honesty. If you'd rather I'd taken notes badly and escaped with less, I'll do that next time."

"He reports true," Ilse said into the murmur. She did not raise her voice; she never had to. "I have cross-checked the arithmetic against three years of harvest-yields and the Ropewalk intake. The Hollow eats memory; the yields fall every year; the fall steepens. A starving thing gets either brave or reckless, and this one has a court full of aristocrats, so it will get brave. Her sums are true. As for the Primate in her story, I have been counting the Concord's perfume-buys through the Slate Market brokers since spring, and I have the count, and I will give it to this council when this council is ready to hear it, and not one day before, because numbers released early are spent early."

Soren felt the hall look at Crane without turning. Crane looked back at the table with kind eyes and granite stillness, and did not sweat, and did not blink, and Soren, who could read a room's dying heartbeats, noted that the Primate's heart was not the first in the hall scheduled to stop and was not the second.

Then he laid the rest on the table: the stolen Court ledger, open to the crude half-translation. ROOTGATE. Keel-anchor. Do not wake the keeper. Beside it, the charred brand cut from a thrall's breastplate, matching. Beside that, Ilse's siege arithmetic, chalked on a slate: presses toward the granary, toward the cellar mouths, toward the under-croft stairs that ran down into the old drowned streets under the harbor; never one soldier's weight of pressure on the vaults; recall sounded at the hour of victory.

"The siege was a clock," Soren said. "They kept us looking at the house while something was done under the harbor. The ledger calls it the Rootgate. It is what the Warden's body roots through: the anchor. The taproot of the whole canopy, under the water, in the deep root-roads that grown things run down there. And this morning the choir-buoys all report the same thing. The canopy is drawing down. Pulling in from the edges. Toward the water."

"Toward the tide," Serle said, "that comes the day after tomorrow. Spring tide. The highest water in three years."

"The ascension tide," said Ilse, and clicked her counter once, and the name sat down in the room and stayed.

* * *

The plan, when it was finally spoken plainly, took four words: burn the Rootgate down.

The sappers took over, the two Chirurgeons and Serle's chief gunner, and the arithmetic grew teeth. Fire-tinctures enough to boil a canal, brewed under guard through the coming day. Divers from the fishing fleet to run the drowned streets under the bell-tower shoal. A fighting column to hold the root-roads open behind the burn-line, because the deep Hollow would come for its own taproot, and come in numbers, and come singing.

"The roads themselves are the difficulty," Crane said, into the pause, and his voice was gentle, a schoolmaster's. "The root-roads are grown of the Warden's oldest flesh. Everything that walks them is read. My tallymen who have entered have come back broken or not at all; the roads know a stranger and digest a stranger. They are keyed, of a kind. Old script, old seal. Botanical work, forty years old." He looked up, and along the table, and the whole room looked with him, and every eye arrived where it had been arriving for an hour. "They are keyed to a Seed."

"I move it formally," Serle said, "before anyone can dress this up as anything nobler than it is. The Seventh Seed walks point. All in favor, and none of you look at me like that, he volunteered in the garden already, by all accounts."

The vote was not close. It never is, when the choice has already been made by the shape of the world, and everyone simply attends the counting. Soren raised his own hand first, which Ilse said afterward was the first time in her life she had seen a man vote for his own funeral and manage to look like he was balancing a ledger.

Assignments went up on Serle's slate in her brutal clerical hand. Gunner's division to the fire-barges. Chirurgeons to the diving-line. Ilse took the rearguard before it was offered, and when Soren opened his mouth she informed him, politely, that he had two hands and she one, and that the rearguard's work was counting, and that she had counted everything twice for thirty years and did not intend to start missing now.

There was an empty chair at the third table with a slate on it, cell rosters in a neat hand with the bravado showing underneath. Nobody had moved it. Nobody would. Soren put his fingers once around the ring in his pocket, on his way past, the way you touch a doorpost leaving a house.

They stood for the blessing at the end. Crane did not make it long. He came around the table to Soren, and took his face in both old hands, and his eyes were wet, and it was the terrible thing about the man, that the tears were real. Every coin in him was real. "Whatever you find down there, Soren Vale," he said, "you are Greyharbor's, and Greyharbor's God, and mine, go with you. I would walk it in your place if the roads would have me. Remember that of me, whatever else is remembered."

"I'll remember it," Soren said, and meant it, and hated meaning it.

* * *

He came back past the drill-hall near midnight for his satchel, because he had forgotten the ledger, and the ledger had become the only set of books he trusted from other people.

The hall was dark except for one lamp at the clerk's desk, and Crane was alone at it, sealing a letter. Wax, black, from a stick Soren did not recognize. The Primate pressed his seal-matrix into it with care, the whole weight of his shoulder, the way a man who has decided something finally does it, and Soren, in the dark of the doorway, caught the scent that came off the melted wax.

Perfume. A specific perfume. One he had smelled once across a masque gallery, and once in a cellar of vials, and once on the wrist of a dead boy who had paid a year of debts in it.

A runner waited in the yard, river-boots, oilskins, no livery Crane's office had any business keeping. Crane put the letter into the runner's hand with his own, said something too low to carry, and watched from the step as the man went down toward the water.

Soren stood in the dark and counted it, the way he counted everything now: one letter, black wax, one scent, one runner, one tide due the day after tomorrow. Then he counted Crane's tears, which had also been real.

The Primate closed the door softly behind his kindness, and the letter went down to the harbor through the fog, toward the Court of Lanterns.
