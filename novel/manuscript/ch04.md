## Chapter 4 — The Tallyman

The Hollow let him go at dawn, the way a creditor lets a debtor leave town.

A split opened for him at the drowned chapel's gate, sighing cellar-air, and he stepped through it into Grennel Street under a real sky, dish-colored, drizzling. The spore-snow on his sleeves turned to ordinary water as he watched. The eleven doors were eleven doors. Mrs. Ferrow's cat sat in the window with the bubble in the pane, spat at him, and went back to sleep, and Soren leaned on the chandler's wall for a while with the spade still in his hand, shaking in the ordinary cold, alive.

The applause had followed him for three streets in the dark. It had never hurried him. That was the worst of it, he thought, walking home to leave the spade by Mrs. Ferrow's scrubbing step and wash the black off his knuckles in the horse trough: whatever had clapped had wanted him alive, and fed, and amused.

"You reek," said a woman's voice from the corner.

She was standing under the gutter outflow where nobody sensible stands, and she did not look like she had been there long, and she looked like she had always been there. Forties. Iron-gray hair in a braid thick as a ship's cable. A coat that smelled of camphor and iron filings, and in her right hand a small brass instrument like a pocket watch with a thumb-lever, which clicked twice while he watched, then clicked twice again, the second time as if checking its own work.

"Morning to you too," Soren said.

"Tenth Essence." She said it the way a clerk reads a charge sheet. "Fresh-taken, last night, in the vicinity of your own street, off a hush-hound you killed with a garden tool. You've been tracking the smell across my rounds since the anniversary. I found a sailor's shirt in a quarantine incinerator with a pale growth stitched into it, and I found a woman on Salt Row whose lodger stopped sleeping at the right hours, and then I found you." She clicked the brass thing again, twice, and looked at his chest, not his face. "Gravid. New-made. And here is my professional problem, young man: you are walking around loose."

Soren put his hands in his pockets, where his knuckles could pretend to be calm. "And your name?"

"Ilse Vann. Tallyman, third circle, the Veridian Concord, out of House Blackwing. You will not have heard of us. That was expensive to arrange."

"Was it you I heard on the stairs the other night? Mortuary on Salt Row. Somebody closed a door and stopped existing."

Her eyes came up to his face for the first time, and there was something in them like approval trying not to hatch. "I counted five organs in that body through the floor. Unlicensed tissue, matured in a dead host, light-fed. I reported it. The report takes eleven hours to walk through the board, and the board takes another eleven to not act." She turned the brass counter over in her palm. "You cut it open and it did not die, and now it lives behind your sternum. So we are going to have a conversation about doors, and you are going to stand still for it."

They had the conversation walking, because she said standing still was for church.

The word for him, she said, was gravid. The organ was a Lacuna: a parasite grown from exposure to the Sporefall's leavings, some in one in ten thousand, sitting behind the breastbone, drinking light, hungry for essence, which was the shadow-stuff of the Hollow's creatures. A gravid who fed it climbed a ladder called the tiers, Tenth Essence at the bottom, First at the top, near-mythic and mostly graveyard. Each tier brought a new organ and a new hunger. The organs grew glands the way apprentices grew opinions: taste of fear, sight through smoke, a voice that could still a hound. The ladder was climbed on essence and survived through fever, and it was made survivable by tinctures, brewed on a licensed bench from Hollow reagents and a memorized gene-script, and if you brewed without a license you did not climb, you spliced. The Amalgam Heresy, the old books called it. There were reasons the word heresy had survived in a city that had burned its churches.

"And the price?" Soren asked. "Nothing climbs free. I weigh bodies. I know the toll schedule of the universe."

Vann stopped walking for a moment. "The Fade," she said. "Every tier burns memories into the Hollow. Scents go first. Then faces. Then names. The Hollow is built out of what it takes. Memory is the one coin both worlds accept, and the exchange rate is set by the Hollow." She clicked her counter, twice. "I have buried friends who could not remember why they had buried friends."

"And the Hollow. The place with the bone cobbles."

"The dark mirror of this city, wearing its streets. Reached through Folds. Inhabited. Governed. The Court of Lanterns sits at the top of it, and it considers you seed-stock." She said the word without decoration. "There is a tide that comes off it when the Court is hungry. My brother went out to count a tide once. The count came back. My brother did not."

They had crossed half the town by then, through streets going about their frightened morning business, and she had never once raised her voice, and he had understood two things. First, that every word could be checked, because she gave him numbers, and liars round their numbers. Second, that the Concord was not the only party interested in a loose gravid.

"And the sweep," Soren said. They had come down through Salt Cross by then, past the weigh-house with its chained scale. "Seven years back. Gray coats, lists. People came back from some of those wagons."

"Some," Vann said. "The board calls the rest relocations. The board's own archive burns annually. Accidents with lamps." She clicked her counter, twice, slowly. "The Concord began counting the morning after the Sporefall and has not stopped. Counting is the whole of my trade, boy. It is the only prayer I know."

The quarantine board paid eleven marks for a gravid, delivered intact. She had watched the wagon men measure a boy in a fish cellar with calipers, and she described the sounds, briefly, so that he would know she was not frightening him for pleasure.

"So there are two doors," Soren said. "The one you're holding open, and the one with the calipers."

"There is always a second door. The Court holds a third, for exceptional stock." She did not smile. "I am offering you a bench, a teacher, and a leash you can see. I will not lie to you about the leash. The Concord wants its gravid counted, kept, and spent carefully. So does everything else in this city. The difference is that we write down what we spend."

He thought of the pale organ drinking the lamp, of the applause in the dark, of his mother's name on a gray coat's list. He thought about doors.

"The leash I can see," Soren said. "Show me the office. Show me the ledger. I don't sign what I haven't read."

Vann clicked her counter twice, and this time the second click sounded almost like a laugh. "You'll do," she said.

* * *

The tally-house was a mission hall behind the salt stores, and the courtyard at the back was walled in brick the color of dried blood, and the wall had been painted white once. It was covered now, from gate to gutter, in small glazed tiles, each with a face fired into the glaze, and beneath each face a name in chalk, and beneath many of the names a second line: SPOREFALL, or SWEEP, or TIDE, and a season.

"The Wall of the Counted," Vann said. "Everyone the city lost and cannot bury. Eleven thousand, four hundred and six as of this morning. The Concord keeps it. Somebody has to." She watched him, not the wall. "Read it or don't. But know that it is the honest wall. Nothing on it was collected. Everything on it was missed."

He read it, because he had never once in his life walked past a thing that could be counted, and the tiles went back seven years, alphabetized by some patient hand. Rain beaded on the glaze and would not run. Here and there the chalk beneath a face had been refreshed, the same letters in the same neat cursive, year over year, the way people tend graves they cannot visit. He was two-thirds down the V's when he stopped counting.

Her face was small under the glaze, a little thinner than he kept it in memory, a little younger around the eyes. She was looking out of the tile at him with the expression she wore when a body told on itself: level, kind, unsurprised. Beneath the face, in chalk that someone had refreshed recently, the name said MAERWEN VALE. Beneath the name: SWEEP. SPRING AFTER SPOREFALL. UNRESOLVED.

"That's my mother," Soren said.

The counter in Ilse Vann's hand clicked once, and went quiet.
