## Chapter 2 — Lacuna

Greech did not come down. Soren called again, once, and then stopped calling, because the lamps came back up shade by shade on their own, and the eye closed. It closed the way a net closes, all the fine veins drawing in together, and then it was only a pale organ in a dead man's chest, and the room smelled of formalin and wet slate, and nothing in the world had changed except everything.

He sewed the sailor closed with the smallest stitches of his career, washed the table twice, logged the body in the parish book as cause of death: immersion, and then, in his own cheap notebook, in a hand he barely recognized as his, wrote: One (1) organ. Site: where the heart should be. It drank the light. It looked back. He read the line three times, decided it was true, and slept three hours in the back room with the lamps burning, which was two more than he meant to.

The quarantine wagon came at dawn. Two men in gray coats took the shutter without a signature, and one of them looked at Soren a moment too long, the way a man looks at a door he intends to come back to. Greech watched from the office window with his tea.

"You look like you slept in a drawer," Greech said.

"Long shift."

"Anniversary shifts are long. It's the city's way of reminding us who keeps the books." He drank his tea. Soren waited to feel the pull of the lie he was keeping, the pale thing under the stitches, and the pull never came, and that frightened him more than the eye had. A secret with no weight in it was a secret that had already chosen its owner.

* * *

He slept at noon in his room over the chandler's shop on Grennel Street, curtains drawn against a sky the color of dishwater, and dreamed the hunt.

He ran on four limbs that bent in too many places, through a fog that was a whole country. Around him ran the pack, and the pack made no sound at all, which was not strange in the dream, because sound was a kind of meat and they were saving it. Ahead of them something small and bright fell out of the sky like a coin knocked off a table, and the pack turned as one body, and he turned with them, and the chase was joy, pure and single, the first unbroken feeling he could remember since he was sixteen. They ran down stairways that grew as they descended. They crossed a canal that hung in the air like a curtain of cold. The bright thing led them through eleven doorways, and behind each doorway was the same room, and in the room a lamp was burning, and they were not allowed to touch the lamp.

They caught the bright thing at the bottom of a garden where the flowers were teeth. The pack set it into his chest, under the bone, careful as sextons laying a coin in a dead man's hand, and said, in no voice at all, in pressure and intention: Keep it. We were only carrying it. It was always addressed to you.

Soren woke on the floor beside the bed with his hand over his sternum and two heartbeats under his palm.

His own he knew: fifty-eight at rest, a clerk's heart, a counter's heart. The other was small and quick and sat a half-inch behind the breastbone, and it came in late, like an echo that had decided to be a voice. There was a taste in his mouth of smoke he had never smoked, burnt hair and frost and under it something sweet, and when he got to the mirror he found a seam on his chest, thin and pink and exact as a surgeon's line, though nothing in this world had cut him.

The watch on its chain was warm. It had not told the time in seven years, and it was warm as a held coin.

The window was fogged from the inside, and the fog on the glass was crawling. He watched it come, bead by bead, toward the side of the room where he stood, and his breath showed in the noon air of his own bedroom, a small ghost that would not leave.

* * *

Mrs. Ferrow knocked the way she did everything, twice and firmly. "Rent," she said through the door. "And don't think I didn't hear you pacing half the night. On the anniversary too. Some of us keep the customs."

He counted out the coins with steady fingers and opened the door on the chain, because even with two heartbeats he was not going to explain his face to a landlady at noon. She was sixty and built like a dock bollard, chalking her doorstep behind her, and she looked at the money, then at him, then at the window behind him where the glass had gone clear again.

"You look like death's clerk," she said. "I've let rooms to death's clerks before. They pay prompt."

"You flatter me. Death keeps a better class of office."

"Mind you keep the hearth swept. Damp comes in on nights like this and takes the weak ones first." She said it lightly, and then did not move from the doorway for a moment, looking at him with the flat professional eyes of a woman who had survived three landlords, two wars of the wharf unions, and one Sporefall. "You've a smell on you," she said at last. "New. Like the cellar after rain. You want to get that seen to."

She went down the stairs. The chalk on her doorstep was a ward his mother could have drawn from memory, and Soren stood in the door of his own room, in his own rent, and understood that he was hungry in a way that food would not answer.

He tested it, because that was what he did with things. Downstairs the chandler sold him a loaf and strong tea and asked no questions, and he ate at the counter like a man who had read about eating somewhere. The bread went down like paper. The tea went down like warm rain. The hunger stayed exactly the size it was, and when he carried the lamp back up the stairs, the hunger turned its attention toward the flame with a slow, loving interest, and the flame bowed in its glass, and Soren climbed the rest of the way in the dark rather than feed it again in front of a witness.

* * *

He examined himself that evening the way he would examine any body that came in over the threshold, because method was the only family he had left. Lamp close, eyes clear, hands steady. The seam did not hurt. Under it, when the lamp came near, something moved, and the lamp dimmed, and the something brightened, and the transaction was so smooth and so greedy that he laughed once, a bad sound in an empty room.

He tried the ledger voice on himself. Subject: Soren Vale, twenty-three. Prior condition: poor. Present condition: two hearts, one appetite, no name for any of it. Whatever he was now, nothing had told him its rank. No mark, no paper, no bell. Only hunger, and fog that leaned toward him like a plant toward a window.

He locked the door. Three bolts, and the chair under the knob for a fourth opinion, and he sat up with the lamp turned high and the notebook on his knee, because he had decided, in the daylight, that whatever had been in the sailor's chest had chosen him in some formal sense, and things that chose you formally could be studied.

By midnight the lamp had lost two shades with the wick untouched.

By one the sounds of the room had begun to arrive late. The tick of the cooling stove came a half-breath after the stove had ticked. The creak of the chair arrived after his weight had already shifted, an echo standing in front of its own voice. He said, "Who's there," and heard the words leave him and not come back, stolen somewhere between his mouth and the wall, and the silence that swallowed them had edges to it, like glass.

The dark under the bed had stopped behaving like dark. It had a grain to it, like fur stroked the wrong way, and it was pooling toward the corner by the wardrobe where the light was worst, and from inside that pool came breathing. It arrived a half-second late, patient, and it was the wrong size for the space it was in.

Dockmen told a tale to frighten apprentices, and the tale had a name in it, and like most of the tales told in Greyharbor lately, the name had stopped being folklore the way a door stops being a door when it is kicked in.

Hush-hounds, the tale called them. They steal the sound of you before they take the rest.

There was a hush-hound in his locked room, and by the way the dark sat in the corner, it had been there long enough to get comfortable.
