## Chapter 16 — The Mother Hypothesis

He walked into the Circle's session at the ninth bell without knocking, because knocking was how one asked to be counted.

Five Leaves behind the waxwood table, the Primate at the center, the lamps hooded to their brown dusk, and Ilse at the wall with her counter silent and her weight even. Soren laid his evidence down the length of the table like a mortuary tray: the genome-ledger from the Grand Canopy, its SEEDS column face up; the Chirurgeon's list, seven names, the seventh fresh; Hollis's sworn finding on the wallpaper script, forty years old, Concord notation; and a page copied from Marrow's ledger, twelve monthly entries of Moltwater to Pale Row.

"Greyharbor's sky was brewed," he said. "On a licensed script. The mark at the foot of that script belongs to Maerwen Vale, who is on your missing-persons wall, whose corridor is sealed behind your herbarium, and whose son you are counting as an asset. The Court has been paying a rogue chirurgeon to practice grafts on children, and its perfume has been standing on this table's credit for a year. I am done asking politely. Open the corridor."

The dusk sat with him for a moment. Leaf Bracken's fan had stopped. Leaf Ostler leaned forward into the lamplight with the face of a man pleased to finally be frightened on the record.

"The corridor is sealed by the order of a Circle gone to dust," Ostler said. "Sealed with the full rite. Sealed for reasons this table inherited and does not question, which is what a table is for. The boy is fevered. Ninth Essence breeds grandiosity, it is in the literature."

"He is Eighth," Ilse said. "He is also the only person in this house whose hand the seal answers, and you have known that since the night he found it, because I filed it and you read what you file." She stepped from the wall, and her voice went to the register she used on the Fields, flat and countable. "I count three sealed doors, one dead brother counting in the dark, a boy's name on a Court list, and a ledger with your Primate's initials buying the Court's voice by the month. Open it, or stand here while I count you the ways it has already been opened."

"Eighth in a fortnight," Ostler said. "Faster and faster. Gentlemen, the boy is a ladder someone else is climbing. Every argument he makes, hear it in that register."

"I do," Ilse said. "I also hear the register of a house that let a dead woman's son find her work by blundering into a corridor its own maps were ashamed of."

"Leaf Vann overreaches," Ostler began.

"Soren Vale overreaches," Crane said quietly.

The Primate had not touched any of the evidence. His kind eyes had rested on the genome-ledger's open column the way physicians rest on a wound they have been dreading. He folded his hands and let the dusk have a long moment.

"Mr. Vale. You believe this table buried your mother's work and let her vanish into the ledger of it." His voice was gentle and it did not bend. "You may be right. The house will go down together to the corridor, because the truth of a seal should be witnessed by those who keep it. But hear the whole of me, boy. If that door holds what you think it holds, the Concord does not survive it opening the way you have opened it. Nothing does. That is not an argument. It is a forecast."

* * *

They went down in a body, seven Leaves and their Primate and the tallyman and the boy, past the scribing hall's shuttered boards, past the wards where the bark-knuckled boy recited wrong into the dark, to the servant's stair and the corridor of pressed flowers.

The lamps leaned in as Soren approached. The amber seals had kept their warmth all autumn, waiting with the patience of wax. He set his bare right hand against the largest of them, the one at the meeting of the doors, and the Lacuna opened behind his sternum, and the seal drank.

It drank his light the way the Canopy's lantern-fields drank, without malice, without hurry, a rooted thing receiving weather it had been planted for. The amber went from honey to water. Something in the doors turned over, ancient and oiled, a mechanism answered rather than forced, and the seals parted along a seam that had existed since before he was born, and the corridor breathed out pressed flowers and cold stone and, faintly, the ghost of a kitchen.

The Leaves made sounds behind him. He did not turn to catalogue them.

The corridor held a herbarium. Pressed specimens under glass, forty years of dust lying on forty years of order, chalkboards of quadrant notation in his mother's quick slanting hand. Seven folios stood in cradles down the left wall, numbered with Roman strokes, I through VII, spines stamped with the ringed sprig. Against the right wall, behind glass, waited cribs of seed-glass like reliquaries, each holding a suspension that still moved, slow, patient, alive. Folio VII lay open on a lectern at the corridor's end, and on its first page, in her hand, a title.

The Mother Hypothesis.

Crane came to stand beside him, and for a while the Primate read the chalkboards with his hands behind his back, the way men stand in the houses of the dead. "She was the finest scribe of her generation," he said at last, to the boards and not to Soren. "I signed her commission. I countersigned her seclusion. Both signatures were given with the same conscience, and that is the most frightening sentence I know."

He read the way he counted, and the Circle read over his shoulders, and the corridor held its breath. The hypothesis ran to pages, but its spine was one claim: the Hollow was not a predator. The Hollow was a mother, starving, and a starving mother will eat the household; the cure was not fire, was not feeding, was re-seeding. Seven lineages, designed, grown not chosen, so that what was given to the Hollow would be given by consent. Gravid blood carrying human memory into the canopy the way seed carries a garden into a field gone fallow.

And in the margins, in a second hand, fair and looping and patient, the argument ran the other way. The same seven lineages, read as harvest instead of sowing. Seed-stock, selected and rationed. Ascension, managed. The two readings shared every letter of the data and disagreed about what a person was for.

Folio VII's register page carried the lineages' names and states, and the last entry was the newest ink in the room: Vale, S. Grown, not chosen.

Six of the seven glass cribs stood empty behind their panes, cultures spent or transferred long ago, and their labels were written in a schoolmaster's neatness: cultivated, walked, counted. The seventh label was newer than the rest, and its final line was not a cultivation date at all. It was a delivery date, and it matched, to the week, the night a stopped pocket-watch had ticked once in a mortuary.

"Two readings," Soren said. His voice came out sanded and steady. "Heal it or feed it. She wrote the first. Someone has been editing the second for forty years, and the editing hand bought a year of Court perfume from Marrow, and the Court's list has my name on it, and this table knew."

"The table knew nothing," Crane said, but his hands, folded, had gone bone-white at the knuckle, and no one in the corridor believed him, least of all the table.

They voted at dusk. The corridor would be re-sealed, the folios impounded, the matter held for the whole Circle of Leaves, a phrase which that afternoon acquired the exact smell of a coffin lid. Ostler argued for burning the cribs. Bracken argued for moving them to the deeps. Crane argued, in the kind granite voice that had carried every vote for twenty years, for patience, and signed the sealing order first, with the complaint of a man signing a mercy.

By evening, pages of the genome-ledger had been copied in three different hands and were moving through the houses like weather. Nobody claimed the copying. Everybody read the copies. Two of the houses wanted Ostler censured over the Fields report. Three wanted Crane's ledger page read aloud at muster. Nobody wanted the thing the pages actually said, which was that every faction in Greyharbor had been bidding on the same boy.

Soren stood in the herbarium corridor while the wax went back over the seal, warm and amber and reeking of pressed flowers, and watched a Circle of Leaves wall up his mother's voice for the second time in forty years.

He had been counted all autumn. By tiers, by lists, by ledgers, by votes, by perfume. Eighth Essence, grown not chosen, and the Court and the Concord both wanted the Seventh, and both counted him the way Marrow's scales weighed memory: as price.

He stopped trusting institutions that night. Let them keep their books. Anyone who counted him as an asset could count without him.
