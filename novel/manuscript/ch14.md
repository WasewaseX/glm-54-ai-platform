## Chapter 14 — The Chirurgeon of Ashlane

Ashlane asked for help the way Ashlane asked for everything, in silence. The note came up from a washerwoman through three hands to Ilse's tally-box: children going into the cellar under the chandler's on Tannery Row, and coming out quieter, and coming out luminous.

The Circle spent an afternoon on it. Leaf Ostler wanted it watched, in case the clinic was Amalgamist work worth documenting from a distance. Leaf Bracken wanted it raided with a full cell and a witness from the militia. Primate Crane, kind eyes down, said that the poor of Ashlane were owed the same protection as the ledger's entries and no more delay than caution allowed, and managed to mean both halves. It was Ilse who cut it short.

"I have counted what rogue grafting does," she said. "I heard it count back. We go tonight, and we go small."

Leaf Ostler's order had already benched Dax, pending review of the Fields report, so it was the two of them who went down through Ashlane's fog an hour after curfew bell, past tannery vats exhaling their last chemical breath, to the chandler's dark window and the cellar door that smelled, wrong for a chandler, of lavender wax and under it copper.

The washerwoman's note had said luminous, and Ashlane had believed it the way Ashlane believed everything quiet, privately. Lights in children, after the Sporefall, were not a miracle in this district. They were a landlord.

The cellar was warm, which was the first obscenity. Whitewashed brick, rows of pallets, a stove, a kettle, orderly jars with labels in a fair round hand. Six patients were in residence. A haulier sat up on his pallet, proud as a groom, showing the gill-crease in his throat that let him breathe the fog without coughing. A woman held her own wrists and watched light travel under the skin of her forearms in slow dim pulses, and she looked at the light the way people look at a debt collector. A lad of fifteen sat the far pallet with his knuckles gone to bark, reciting under his breath, patient and wrong, the way the wards in the deeps recited, and the sight of it went into Soren like a hook. The boy was not a ward. The boy was a practice piece. Nobody screamed. That was the second obscenity: this was a ward, and it was tended, and somewhere in Greyharbor the Concord's licensed benches had empty chairs.

"You are Concord," said the man from the inner room.

He came out wiping his hands on a mud-green apron, a big soft-bodied man with an undertaker's gentleness and a case of instruments that Soren's eye catalogued before his stomach could object: chitin scalpels, glass cannulas, bone-saws ground from Hollow teeth.

"Chirurgeon Gorst," he said. "You are up past your licenses, tallyman, and you have brought me a gravid. I can smell the bench on him. Ninth, sealed, Predator, and not yet sworn to anything useful." He smiled with real warmth. "The Concord seals its benches and lets the poor die licensed. I charge less than heaven and I answer letters."

"You are splicing civilians," Ilse said.

"I am grafting civilians. Splicing is what happens when the words go short." Gorst set down his hands. "Every graft in this room takes, tallyman. Ask them. The haulier works double shifts in fog now. Mrs. Anser's arms will carry her to work when her lungs quit, next winter, the way they are going. I refine the craft as I go."

"As you are paid to refine it," Soren said.

Gorst's smile stayed on and stopped being warm.

Then Soren's kill-sense, which had been open since the stair, pointed for the first time all night, and it pointed through the inner doorway, and his stomach dropped through the cellar floor. There was a small bed behind the curtain, and in it a girl of seven or eight with a doll and a blanket, and under her sternum something was blooming, a lantern-organ grafted in half-grown, pulsing light through her nightgown in rhythm with a heart that was losing the argument. The graft was drinking her. It would finish within the day.

He could see the craft in it now, with the organ-man's eye he had never asked for: the pulse of the thing was mite-rhythm, swarm-time, a colony where a gland should be, fed and coaxed and half-taught. The Concord's own novice fevers were gentler than this by orders of mercy.

"Tansy," Gorst said, and for the first time his voice had weight. "Foundling. Board paid by the parish. The graft ran ahead of my notes. It cannot be undone. Grown things can only be ended."

The girl looked past all of them at Soren, because children always found the dangerous one in the room.

"Make the light stop," she said. "It hums."

He had laid out four hundred bodies and he knew every way a body stops being a person, and none of the ways had ever been his to choose. Ilse did not move and did not look away, because she was Ilse. Gorst watched him with the terrible interest of a colleague.

Soren took off his right glove. He crossed the room. He was aware of the kettle, and the doll's enamel face, and the count, his own count, the one he kept to keep his hands honest: her pulse at the throat, quick and small; the graft's glow between his fingers, humming against his palm like a struck bell that would not finish. He did the work the way the mortuary had taught him, in one motion, with economy, with his hand over hers so that the last thing the count held was company.

Ilse's tally-counter clicked once. She counted the dead, always, and this time so did he.

Gorst was already through the coal hatch.

* * *

The fog above Ashlane was a wall to everyone alive. To Soren it was glass.

He came out of the hatch with smoke-sight wide and saw the cellar district lit from within: coal smoke in the yards, steam off the vats, and one warm man-shape galloping between the drying racks with his apron flapping. He did not run. He walked the straight line the fog had stopped being able to hide and arrived at the tannery gate two seconds before Gorst did, and what followed was not a duel. Gorst's left hand became something with too many options; Soren's kill-sense told him which of the options would die first, and Predator had been waiting all autumn for a man whose body believed its own advertisements. By the time Ilse arrived with the lamp, the Chirurgeon of Ashlane was face down in the mud with his wrist bent at the exact angle of a confession.

They interrogated him in the cellar, among his patients, because Ilse wanted witnesses.

"Count with me, chirurgeon," she said, walnut hand flat on his chest. "You will find I never stop."

He counted. He was glad to, in the end, the way spent men are glad of any rail. Payment had come monthly for eight months in phials of perfume he was ordered to wear to certain meetings in Ashlane chapel, a patron in gray gloves whose face he never saw, and instructions in a fair round hand.

"I was told to practice," Gorst said, gray-lipped, on the pallet where Tansy's doll sat looking at him. "Refine the graft. Make it take quickly. Make it take in the young. The tide will need hosts that walk."

He said the last like a prescription. Soren heard the boiler room, the crowns on warm brick, gloved fingers resting on his shoulder light as a toll.

"Whose hand wrote your instructions?" Ilse asked.

"A patron's. The same hand wrote my delivery notes and my wages and my silences." Gorst looked at the ceiling of the cellar as though reading a fair round hand on it. "I was hired for craft, tallyman, not for politics. I practiced. I was paid in perfume and left alone, and in Greyharbor that is the whole of what mercy looks like from below."

Soren filed the hand the way he filed everything now: seven names, seven folios he had not yet seen, a corridor that answered him by touch. The counts were converging. He did not like the shape of the shape they were making.

The chandler's tin box stood open on the bench while Ilse bound the writs in evidence cord: reagents, a Court-stamped writ with a lantern pressed in black wax, and a folded paper of names in a fine looping hand.

Seven names.

The first six the damp of the cellar had made into ghosts, letters gone to lace, recoverable by scholarship and not by sight. The seventh sat black and clean and recent, ink still proud of the paper, as if the list had been written in a hurry from the bottom up.

Soren Vale.
