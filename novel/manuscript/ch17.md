## Chapter 17 — Schism

The wards of House Blackwing began to burn an hour before dawn, and they burned in the order they had been written.

Soren Vale came awake in the novices' dormitory with light crawling up the wall: a soft green flame following the brushed lines of script that had guarded that room for forty years, eating each word backward from its ending. The flame made no heat and less sound. It did not destroy so much as unwrite. He watched a ward that should have boiled the blood of anything Hollow cross the doorframe and go out letter by letter, and he understood the only way script burned like this. Someone inside the house had revoked the counterglyphs. The fire had been invited.

In the corridor the whole household stood with lamps held high, watching their protections die politely.

People watched their protections die the way relatives watch a bier. Nobody spoke above a murmur. An apprentice wept without drama, two fingers pressed to her mouth, and Soren knew the posture from the mortuary: the stance of people waiting to be told what they are allowed to feel.

"The ledger," Ilse Vann said at his shoulder. Her iron-gray braid was half-pinned, put up in haste, and her left sleeve was folded and looped where a hand used to be. She counted the onlookers with one flick of her eyes, the way she counted everything. "Two hundred and eleven souls in this house last night. By breakfast there will be two factions and one fire."

"You're cheerful," Soren said.

"I'm awake. It passes for cheerful."

The morning's second insult was nailed to the refectory door. Twelve pages, copied fair and fast, the ink still smelling of the scribing desks: his mother's sealed corridor, itemized. Her gene-scripts, transcribed. Seven lineages, named by number. The seventh circled twice in a steady hand.

"Nailed up at the turn of the watch," Ilse said. "Before anyone could be shamed into privacy."

Dax Merrow came late to the refectory, which was unlike him, and did not meet Soren's eyes, which was more unlike him. His right thumb moved over the inside of his left wrist, back and forth, the way a man worries a scab. When Soren glanced at the wrist, Dax turned it over and gave his quick grin to the middle distance. "Wild, eh? Who'd even have a Court ledger?"

The answer, which Soren did not say aloud, was: I did. He had stolen it out of the Grand Canopy with lampblack on his face and handed it to the Circle of Leaves, and one of the Leaves had evidently decided the house deserved to know what grew in its cellar.

* * *

They convened in the brewing hall, because it was the largest room with benches bolted down, and because both sides wanted the stills.

The Purists stood beneath the great ward-script with Primate Aldous Crane among them, wax-white leaf badges at every collar. The Amalgamists stood by the stills under Preceptor Roswald Vane, who had rolled back both sleeves to show his forearms: the left was plated in pale chitin, grown in careful squares like a faithful copy of armor. Behind him were the ones the Concord called the grafted. A woman with a film of gill-slit at her throat. A boy whose eyes blinked sideways once in a while, nictitating, patient.

"The wards are burning," Vane said, "because we have spent forty years writing locks in a language our enemy speaks better than we do. The Hollow reads our scripts the way a farmer reads weather. It is not a monster at the gate, ladies and gentlemen. It is a kinsman at the table, and it is starving, and we are pretending not to hear its stomach." He spread his plated arm. "I grafted this to learn it. I have never once been lied to by my own arm."

"Kindly do not say it that way," said the woman with the gills, and someone laughed, badly, because the room wanted so much to laugh.

Vane let the laugh gutter out before he turned to the benches where Soren sat with Ilse and Dax, and he put on the gentle voice, which was how he did his worst work. "And here is the man of the hour. Your mother sowed seven fields, boy. I have read the pages; we all have. The answer of the Circle is to plough them under and salt the earth and call the salting mercy. I am offering you harvest instead. A farmer's son should hear that as a kindness."

"I am a mortuary man," Soren said. "We do not harvest. We dispose. Different trade, and the difference is the whole difference."

Crane's voice was quiet, which in him was worse than shouting. "We are not them. That is the whole of my argument, and it is granite, and granite does not care that it is unfashionable. The night we splice ourselves to be like them is the night we deserve to lose." His kind eyes found Soren across the benches. "Soren. Stand with the Concord. Be counted."

And Vane, smiling: "Counted. Yes. Do you notice how it is always counting with these people? The Seeds were designed, boy. Designed things have purposes. The Circle will seal your mother's work and call it mercy. We will finish it and call it war. Choose the banner with your blood in it."

"You will have your quay shifts sanctioned by tonight," Crane said quietly, before the murmur could spread. "And my blessing, such as it is. Granite does not chase anyone, Vale. But it remembers."

Every face turned to him. He felt the room like a held breath, felt his Lacuna sit up behind his sternum and taste the air the way a tongue finds salt. Fear, and under the fear something worse: hope. Two hundred people hoping he would take their decision off their hands and carry it where it could not be argued with.

"I've been counted my whole life," Soren said. "Mortuary roster first, then the wall of the missing, and now by two banners in one morning. So here is my count, and it comes to three. Vann. Merrow. Me. We take the quay shifts, we run sanctioned harvests, we brew what the bench allows. When either of you wants the city defended more than you want to be right, you know where the dead are laid out. We'll be the ones keeping the books straight."

Silence. Then Vane inclined his head like a fencer conceding a touch. "A fourth faction of one cell. Greyharbor will miss you when it burns."

"The city will miss your forearms," Soren said. "They were very educational."

By noon the Circle of Leaves had split down its long table like a dropped loaf. Three Leaves walked out with Vane and took half the field rosters with them, names read aloud and struck through in ink before the door had shut. All that day and night the wards burned on both sides, each faction revoking its own contributions, the house unwriting itself in green fire, until the old keepers went through with salt and chalk and made the place honest again: four walls, some doors, no promises.

Soren stood in the courtyard at midnight with Ilse and Dax and watched a ward his mother had probably walked beneath come apart letter by letter.

"We held the line," Dax said, too lightly.

"We held a hall," Ilse said. She clicked her brass counter twice, once for each thing she intended to remember. "The line is the harbor."

* * *

The bell that woke them at dawn was the wrong bell. Not the curfew bell, not the fire bell. The all's-well bell at the customs house, rung ragged, rung by someone who did not believe it.

The word came up from the mudlarks and then from a militia runner, breathless, disbelieving of his own mouth: Ropewalk had not fallen in the night. Ropewalk had not burned, had not flooded, had not been taken. Ropewalk was gone.

A woman's single shoe stood on the bridge parapet, placed neatly, laces tucked in, the way you leave something you mean to come back for. A militia sergeant knelt at the cordon rope being quietly sick. The customs bell was still ringing its all's-well, and the sound went out over the water and came back with nothing under it.

Soren stood at the cordon on Chandler's Bridge with smoke-sight banked low and looked at where nine streets and a ropery and two thousand souls should have been. The fog there was not fog. It was a wall with a grain to it, and the grain ran vertical, and his sight went through it the way a hand goes through smoke. Behind the grain: mossed bone in the shape of streets. Lamplight, if lamplight grew on trees, hanging in rows where windows used to be. Canopy, miles of it, dark and gentle as a held breath. The district had not been destroyed. It had been taken, whole, into the body of the Hollow, like a coin into a purse, and the purse had closed.

And somewhere in there, muffled to a thread, faint as a voice under water, a ropery bell was ringing. Someone in the taken streets was ringing the alarm, and the alarm was ringing inside the thing that had eaten it.

Ilse clicked her counter. Once, twice. "Nine streets," she said. "Say it, so it stays said."

"Nine streets," Soren said, and his voice came out level, which cost him something. "The Folds were doors before. It knocked, it reached, it stole through keyholes." He watched the vertical grain of the world's new wall flex and settle, flex and settle, patient as breathing. "This morning it stopped knocking. It has begun to eat the plate we serve it on."
