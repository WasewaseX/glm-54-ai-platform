## Chapter 6 — Curfew Hunters

The tide came off the canals on the ninth night after the anniversary, and it came in through Tarlock Stairs, where the water runs black under the gasworks and the militia keep a brazier and a bell that nobody has ever rung in time.

Soren heard it first as weather. Then the kill-sense opened in the back of his mind like a fan, and the night rearranged itself into debts: forty-two militiamen along the quay, none owed; a squad at the stairs, none owed; and down in the dark under the bridge arches, a crowd of small cold heartbeats, all of them owed, all of them coming up the stairs together.

It was his first night out with a harvest cell, and the cell's orders were the orders the Concord gave on every tide night since the Sporefall: walk the cordon's blind side, take what the tide threw off, be home by second bell. Gravid walked curfews because the tide hunted gravid blood, and it was better for everyone that it found the Concord's gravid first, in a place of the Concord's choosing, wearing the Concord's iron. Soren carried the house spike now, a broad short blade with a mortuary knife's balance, because a man could not take a borrowed spade to every war. His hands knew its weight in three days. His hands were quick to know weights.

"Breach," he said. "Below the arches. Twenty-odd. Small things."

"Tide's early," said Brack, who was the cell's fourth and oldest member and had spoken, by Soren's count, eleven words in two weeks. He began loading his fowling piece with the unhurried economy of a man buttering toast.

They went over the quay rail one at a time, because the cell worked the fish-market side where the militia line did not look, and Ilse had already paid the duty of pretending not to be paid. The curfew bell was tolling its long complaint overhead. Below the arches, the water was folding. Not waving: folding, like cloth, and out of the folds the hush-hounds came up the wet steps in a silence so complete that Soren's ears rang with it.

The next ten minutes were work, and the work was ugly, and afterward Soren never told anyone the part where it felt like a trade he had been apprenticed to all his life. You killed a hound with iron and salt and standing ground. You did not let it steal your voice. You counted your companions' heartbeats the way the harbor counted ships.

His first kill of the field came at the third stair, a hound that flowed at him out of the bell-shadow of the arch. The spike did what Ondrey had promised the bench-steel would do: it went in on the bias, it came out on the bias, and the thing came apart around it like a coat giving up its lining. He had time to be sick later. The tide did not schedule time to be sick.

He was counting his own, at the top of the stairs, when the wall behind him unfolded.

The gallows-walker had been standing against the bridge abutment for a full minute, and he had walked past it twice, because it looked like rigging and shadow and a bad job of lamplight. It was fifteen feet of folded blade-limbs, a carrion giant, patient as a debt, and when it chose to move it moved like a falling chimney, and its two forelimbs came down where he was standing, and he was still deciding to duck.

A shot took the thing in the second joint of its left forelimb and knocked the whole stroke sideways into the stone. Salt and iron shot, close enough to smell. Then a hand had his collar and the world did a half-turn, and Soren found himself sitting in a puddle while a boy about nineteen went past him at a run, reloading as he ran, whooping in a way that was going to get someone killed, quite possibly himself.

"Vann!" the boy yelled. "Left leg, it pivots on the left leg!"

They killed it in ninety seconds, on the count, the way the Concord kills everything: Brack to the knee, Ilse to the neck-joint, the boy putting his last shell into the lantern-organ at its throat when it reared. The light went out of it like a tide leaving a gutter. The silence it left behind was enormous.

The boy turned around, walked back to Soren, and offered a hand up. He had a quick grin, a chipped front tooth, a coat two sizes too big with chalk dust in the seams, and eyes that were already doing arithmetic.

"Dax Merrow," he said. "Tallyman, ninth circle, which is the circle they put you in when the alternative is debtor's gaol. You're the new tenth, then. Soren, right? Vann talks about you the way sailors talk about weather."

"You saved my life," Soren said.

"Two seconds ago, yes." Dax produced, from an inside pocket, a small printed card, filled out in pencil. "I also want you to know that I keep accounts. One life, saved. One shell, expended, number nine shot, saltload. One coat, ruined, yours, from the puddle, not my fault but adjacent to me. I do a bit of brewing on the side to keep the ledger honest, so we can settle in installments. Most clients prefer installments."

"He's joking about the installments," Ilse said, coming up the stairs with her blade still out. "He's not joking about the card. He has filed one against every member of this house, including the Primate. Harvest now, comedy later. We have maybe a quarter bell before the militia finds its nerve."

* * *

The harvest was performed the way the Concord harvested: on the count, with knives on a bias, taking only what the ledger allowed. "Galls at the neck," Ilse said, kneeling over the first hound. "Cut on the bias, never squeeze. What you bruise, you burn. The Hollow keeps no refunds."

Dax worked three knives at once, talking the whole time in a low running line that Soren realized was not nerves but ballast. "Fourth one's got a two-gallon gall, Vann, we're rich, I'm buying my mother a hat. She'll hate it. It'll be wonderful." His hands never once matched his mouth. They were fast, exact, and gentle with the carcasses, and Soren filed that away, because he filed everything.

The hounds gave up galls at the neck, small cold purses of shadow. The walker's throat-lantern, split, gave up a measure of pale essence that smoked even in the rain. Soren knelt on wet stone with the canal breathing under him and fed for the first time in the field, and it was nothing like the desperate gulp off a dead hound's shadow. This was a meal with a tablecloth on it. The essence went down cold and bright, and every bite of it said: soon, and further, and more.

The kill-sense sorted the night for him while he worked. The militiamen up on the quay: none owed, though one was owed soon, a lung he could hear failing in the rain. The dead things: paid in full. It was in the middle of that comfortable arithmetic that he caught the perfume.

Crushed flowers and cold wax. Anniversary fog, bottled and worn as clothing.

He looked up. Across the canal, on the roof of the salt warehouse, stood a figure that had not been there and was there, tall, holding perfectly still, with a soft light at the throat like a lamp behind silk. It stood the way courtiers stand in engravings, one hand resting on a rail that no other human hand could have reached. It did not move. It did not need to move. The perfume came across the water to him alone, and settled on him like a hand on the shoulder, and when he blinked, the roof was empty, and the night smelled only of salt and powder and dead walker.

"What is it?" Ilse said, sharp.

"Perfume," Soren said. "On the warehouse roof. Gone now."

Brack and Ilse exchanged the look of professionals sharing an entire report in one glance. "The Court of Lanterns does not send perfume to spice its weather," Ilse said slowly. "The Court sends perfume to open negotiations, or to close them. Which was it?"

"It stood still the whole time."

"Then negotiations are open." She said it flat, but her counter clicked twice in her pocket, and Soren had begun to learn that the counter never once told him something she hadn't already counted.

Dax, banking his piece, said, too lightly, "Rooftop perfume. Grand. I bill extra for Court work," and then was quiet, which was the loudest thing Soren had seen him do.

They packed the harvest, took the fish-market stairs two at a time, and were through the cordon before the militia finished arguing about its bell. At the door of the tally-house, going in, Soren put his hand in his coat pocket for his keys and found paper.

The card was thick as bark and dry as a file. It smelled of crushed flowers and cold wax, and it had been in his pocket through all of it, through the tide and the harvest and the quarters of a bell when his heart had tried to leave through his back, and he had never once felt it arrive.

The handwriting on it was old-fashioned, upright, and very beautiful, and there was nothing on the card but an address.

For the Seventh.
