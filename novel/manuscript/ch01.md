## Chapter 1 — The Body in the Fog

Seven bells for seven years. The curfew bell of Greyharbor rang at dusk the way it had every night since the Sporefall, but tonight the ringers added seven strokes at the end, and the fog took them one at a time, so that the last note arrived at the mortuary on Salt Row soft and worn, like a coin rubbed too long between fingers.

Soren Vale counted them anyway. He counted everything. The trade had grown the habit into him: the difference between six trays and seven was somebody's missing aunt, and a body logged with nine fingers was a story the coroner's man would want told twice. Counting kept the world where he had put it.

Salt Row was quiet at bell. Below the hill, the harbor lay under wool, masts black as burnt matches, the gaslight along the quay diffused to a yellow smear. On nights like this the fog carried a faint sweetness, crushed flowers and cold wax, and no one in Greyharbor could explain it and no one talked about it. Seven years since the luminous cloud had come down over the city for a single night, and half the Sixth District had walked up into it and not walked down. The anniversary came back every autumn like a debt, and the whole city paid it by staying indoors after dark.

Chalked notices on every corner said the same thing in the same tired hand: KEEP IN AFTER BELL. KEEP DOORS SHUT. DO NOT ANSWER TO VOICES YOU KNOW.

Soren had been sixteen the night the sky came down. He remembered it in pieces, the way he remembered most useful things: the light through the shutters lying on the kitchen floor in pale rings; his mother chalking the doorframe with salt and marks she would not explain; her hand flat on his chest, pressing him down onto the boards, saying his name once in the voice she saved for the mortuary. In the morning the light was gone, the fog had moved in like a lodger who paid no rent, and men in the quarantine board's gray coats were walking the streets with lists. His mother's name had gone onto one of those lists by spring, and nothing had come off any list since.

Upstairs, the mortuary's owner was arguing with the ledger. "Soren." Old Greech's voice came down the stairwell, gravel over a cough. "Boat's at the side door. You'll want the good apron."

"What's the cargo?"

"Sailor. Out of the Breadwin Channel, an hour before bell." Greech appeared at the foot of the stairs, sleeves rolled to the elbow, gray stubble, ink on his thumb. "Third this week. A man could found a religion on drowned sailors in this city."

"Somebody has," Soren said. "It meets on anniversary nights and complains about the fees."

Greech snorted and went to sign the watch's docket. The harbor watchman delivered the body on a shutter. On his way out he paused with his hat in both hands. "Third one," he said, to nobody in particular. "Other two went to Saint Ellery's. The father there charged the parish a blessing fee and put them in one grave, side by side, like spoons." He said it the way men say things they don't want repeated, and left.

"One grave, like spoons," Greech repeated, disgusted. "That's a fee I'd have waived." He signed the docket with a flourish that bordered on vandalism. "This one sits with us tonight. The quarantine wagon comes at dawn, anniversary or no. What the parish does after that is between the parish and its God." Soren had stopped minding the arrangements two years ago, around the time he understood he would never be permitted to call himself anything grander than assistant.

The sailor was young, thirty at the outside, rigger's hands, no boots. They laid him on the second table, the one with the raised lip, and Soren lit both lamps, because anniversary nights deserved the courtesy of light.

"Anniversary work," Greech muttered, stamping the docket. "Bad luck, washing a body tonight. My mother said so."

"Your mother also said eels were the souls of tax collectors."

"And has an eel ever wronged you, boy? No. There's a woman who knew the world." He shuffled off to the office with the docket, keys jangling, and left Soren alone with the dead man and the fog.

Soren worked the way he always worked. Coat folded, pockets inventoried: a bent tin of matches, a lead token for the ferry, nothing written. Shirt next, buttons counted into the dish. Seven, one missing, replaced at some poor mouth with cheaper bone. Boots gone, sold or lost before the pull. A rope-burn across one palm, weeks old and healed wrong, the kind a man gets from a line under strain. He logged the hands, the teeth, the old break in the left forearm. A life, itemized, filed under a number until somebody claimed it or the parish paid. The skin was cold, but not canal-cold, not after an hour in November water, and that was the first thing that sat wrong. He said his observations aloud, a low running tally, because a thing said out loud was harder for a quiet room to change.

"Time of death doesn't match the pull," he told the sailor. "You've been out of the water longer than they think. Somebody kept you somewhere warm."

The dead man held still and let himself be read, which was what Soren liked about the dead. They never lied. They only stopped.

* * *

The lungs were where the night stopped being a night like other nights.

He had opened a hundred drowned chests, or near enough, and drowned lungs had a look he could have drawn from memory: heavy, wet, liver-dark, weeping froth at the knife. He made the short incision under the ribs the way he always did, and stopped with the blade still in his hand.

The lungs were dry. Dry as pressed flowers, pale as the pages of an old book, folded down at the corners. No water in them at all.

"Well," Soren said. "You didn't drown."

Against his breastbone, under his shirt, something ticked. Once. His mother's pocket-watch, dead these seven years, stopped at six minutes past four on a morning he did not let himself think about. He had worn it every day since he was sixteen, through the quarantine sweep that took her, through the year of letters that got no answers, through the six years of this. It had never once made a sound. He pressed his palm flat over it and stood very still, and the watch lay against his skin like a small cold stone, keeping its silence, pretending it had always been obedient.

The lamps guttered. Not flickered: guttered, drawn down, the way a candle dims under a snuffer, and both of them dimmed together, toward the table.

Soren leaned over the opened chest. Where the heart should have sat, nested in its sac like a pearl in a shell, was something else. Pale. Smooth as the inside of an eggshell, webbed with veins so fine they might have been inked with a pen, and the veins moved. Not the way a heart moves. They turned, all of them together, slowly, like compass needles finding north, and every needle pointed at the nearest lamp.

At the light.

As he watched, the gaslight lost another shade, and the thing in the chest brightened by exactly as much as the room went dark. He could see it drinking. His hands stayed flat on the table's edge, careful hands, mortuary hands, and his mind did the only thing it knew to do with a world gone wrong.

It counted. One lamp. One body. One organ where no organ had any business being, grown where a heart had been, and the heart gone as clean as if it had been paid for and collected. Somewhere beneath the fear, an older voice of his took over, the mortuary voice, the one that catalogued: unknown organ, one unit, site pericardial, previous owner unavailable for interview. It was a ridiculous comfort. Numbers were a floor to stand on, and for now the floor was holding.

"Greech," he said, and his voice came out level, which surprised him. "Come look at this."

Upstairs a door closed. Then there were no footsteps at all, and the fog against the windows went silent, and the silence had edges to it, like glass.

Soren should have stepped back. He knew that the way he knew the fee schedule. Instead he took up the small bone saw and touched its flat side to the pale thing, as if it were any unknown and he any examiner. Cold, colder than the room. And beneath the cold, a warmth with a rhythm to it, small and quick and patient, nothing like a man's heart.

The veins turned. All of them, together, away from the lamps.

Toward him.

The light lost another shade. In the opened chest, the smooth surface gathered itself into fine folds, the way skin does around a waking mouth, and parted with a sound like a wet page turning.

The organ opened an eye.
