## Chapter 5 — Tincture of the Novice

House Blackwing kept its front door behind a fishmonger's cold room on Candlecourt, down a flight of steps that smelled of salt, sawdust, and something older than either, and the first thing the house did to Soren was take his coat.

"The house takes coats," said the woman at the bench. "It takes names too, eventually, but that's a senior privilege." She was fifty and sharp as a bone folder, sleeves pinned to the elbow, forearms freckled with old scald-marks, a chalk stub riding behind her ear like a quartermaster's pencil. "Apothecary Ondrey. I brew. You are the fresh Tenth that Vann pulled off the street, which means for the next month you are the most flammable object in this building. Walk carefully, breathe carefully, and never brew alone."

The brewing hall ran the length of the house: benches bolted to the floor, fume cupboards of brass and glass, and along the far wall a chalkboard the size of a ship's sail, dense with looping close-written script that hurt to read, the way a stair hurts when you look down it. The script moved. Not obviously. The way weather moves.

"Gene-scripts," Ondrey said, watching him not watch the wall. "A tincture is a recipe and a contract, and the contract is written in that. Hollow biology reads it the way we read a face. You will memorize three before you are permitted to recite one, and if you ever recite one you do not understand, I will personally assist the splice in finding you. Sit."

She sat him at the junior bench, where the glassware was arranged like a surgery, and brewed his first tincture with him the way surgeons teach stitches: hands over his hands.

Reagents came out of the cold room one at a time, each named and logged. Fold-rain, which had to be caught in the Hollow within a minute of falling, or it turned to spite. Mite-ink, green-black, taken from lantern-mites at the graze. A pinch of hearth-salt from a house that had stood through the Sporefall. And last, from a numbered vial with his own mark on the ledger beside it, the ichor of the hush-hound he had killed on Grennel Street.

"That's yours," Ondrey said. "You harvested it, you banked it. First lesson of the trade, boy: the Hollow takes payment in what you were willing to kill. The tincture remembers."

The brewing itself was mostly waiting, and the waiting was the dangerous part, because while the copper distilled, the novice recited. The gene-script went into him by rote, two hours a night, chalk in hand at a scribing desk with a monitor's hand on his shoulder. When at last he stood at the bench and recited it through, from the first glyph to the last, the script did not feel like words. It felt like swallowing a rope that was being pulled gently from the other end. The alembic above the copper ticked. The vapor came down the coil the color of dusk, and filled the receiving flask with something that moved when the lamp moved, and Ilse Vann, who had come to watch with her arms folded and her counter clicking its quiet double-checks, said: "Good color. Don't get attached."

"What does it actually do?" Soren asked, watching the dusk in the flask turn to face the lamp.

"Binds the Lacuna to your ledger," Ondrey said. "A raw gravid burns essence like a drunkard burns wages, up the ladder with nothing to show but appetite. The tincture puts a hand on the ladder. Steadies the organ, signs the growth-fever into a form your body can pay, and tells the Lacuna who its host thinks it is. Brewed, you climb. Unbrewed, you are consumed by an enthusiastic employee." She stoppered the flask. "And tomorrow you choose your Codex, and then comes the fever. Eat now. Sleep now. The bench will still be here. The bench is the only thing in this city that is always still here."

* * *

The Codex plates were brought out on a tray, because the formality mattered, Ondrey said. Four of them, each a hand's width of pale slate fired with a glyph, each resting in a cradle of moss that was faintly, warmly alive.

"Predator," Ondrey said. "Hunt, speed, and the kill-sense: you will feel, in any room, which heartbeat is already owed. The Hollow's oldest trade. It will make you quick and it will make you certain, and certainty is the most expensive thing on this tray."

"Chirurgeon. Flesh-shaping, mending, unmaking. You will never lose a patient again. You will also know, always, exactly how they would come apart. Some of our kindest graduates stopped being able to look at their families."

"Botanist. Spore-craft, growth, dominion of a sort over Hollow flora. The land listens to a Botanist. The land also argues."

"Symbiont. Pacts. You would carry passengers, and some of them would carry you." She set the tray down. "Provisional now. You choose with ink; at Ninth Essence the choice seals, and the Codex writes itself into your genome, where no clerk can reach it to correct it. Most gravid choose what they were already like. It saves the house time."

"Which did you choose?" Soren asked Ilse.

"Predator. Thirty years ago, at that bench, for worse reasons than yours." She did not look away from him. "My brother signed Botanist, and the tide took him out counting flowers. Choose with your eyes open, not with mine."

Soren looked at the four plates a long moment. He thought of the sailor's dry lungs, of the wall of the missing, of a thing applauding politely in the dark of his own street.

"I intend to find out who sowed my city," he said. "That's hunting. Give me the Predator plate."

Nobody tried to talk him out of it, which he noticed, and which told him something about the house. He signed the roll with his blood and his name, Ondrey pressed his pricked thumb to the glyph, and the slate drank the drop the way chalk drinks water, and somewhere behind his sternum the Lacuna turned over like a sleeping man being told good news.

The fever came at moonrise.

* * *

They strapped him to a plank bed in the novices' ward, because a body growing its first organ thrashes, and packed him in ice, because the Lacuna drinks light and heat and would drink him if it could. The fever was a tide. It ran up his spine in rings, and in the rings he dreamed of running, and for the first time the dream had no pack in it. He ran alone, through a Greyharbor made of bone and moss, wearing his own legs, counting streets, and the count came out even, and in the dream that frightened him more than anything with teeth.

He woke at dawn with a nurse unlacing the straps and the taste of smoke gone from his mouth at last, replaced by every other taste in the ward.

There were nine beds. He could hear nine heartbeats, distinctly, the way a man in a dark room knows where the lamps are. And under the far window, in the seventh bed, one heartbeat sounded different. Not louder. Earlier. It sounded like a bell that had been struck a half-second before anyone pulled the rope, and his new sense, quiet and factual, laid the knowledge in him the way a mortuary ledger takes an entry: that one is owed. Hours from now, not days. The old man in it was asleep, breathing the careful breaths of the recently dosed, and he had a visitor's posy on the sill, and Soren stood in the middle of the ward with his new organ opening like an eye and understood that he would now always know this, first thing, in every room he entered, for the rest of his life.

The old man died at noon, easy, in his sleep, a stroke the physician had called kind. Soren told no one about the bell. He wrote it in the back of his notebook instead, under the entry about the organ that had looked back at him, and the book was getting to be a strange book.

Ilse found him there in the afternoon, sitting on the ward steps with his watch warm in his fist.

"It works, then," she said. "The kill-sense. I could tell by how you walked past the porter. Predators walk like debt collectors."

"It told me who was going to die. It didn't ask me if I wanted to know."

"It never asks. Listen to me now, novice, because this is the speech I give once." She sat down beside him, and the counter in her hand was still. "You will climb. You're built for it, you're angry enough for it, and the Concord needs you at the higher tiers before the tide season. But every rung is paid for, and the currency is memory, and the Fade is not a thief. A thief takes the gold. The Fade takes the most valuable thing you carry, every time, because the Hollow builds itself out of what people cannot bear to lose." She looked at the watch in his hand. "It takes the kindest memory first."
