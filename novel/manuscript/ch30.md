## Chapter 30 — First Light in Greyharbor

The fog lifted on a Tuesday.

It went the way weather goes when it has finally run out of arguments: thinned from the top down, then from the edges in, and by mid-morning Greyharbor could see itself for the first time in seven years. Slate roofs steamed in the sun. The canals came up green-gold and embarrassed, like rooms opened after a funeral. And out past the harbor mouth the sea lay flat to a horizon of struck tin, so bright that men on the dockside took off their hats to it, and not one of them could have said why.

The churches rang their bells out of order, because none of them had heard a sunrise in seven years and every one of them had something to say. Nobody minded. Out-of-order bells were the correct music for a city learning to look up.

The fish exchange opened with its shutters all the way up for the first time since anyone could remember, and the honest smell of it drifted over the canal. Market stalls came up like mushrooms after the rain they had finally seen. Children who had been taught the fog the way other children are taught religion stood in the middle of streets pointing at clouds, and their parents let them stand there and did not count minutes to curfew, because there was, as of that morning, a motion on the Circle's books to strike the word curfew from the city code, and everyone knew it would pass, and everyone went out anyway, on credit, so to speak.

Soren kept the mortuary.

It had changed less than the city had. The sixth floor was still a Fold, though the garden behind it had gone quiet, and its keeper, whatever she had been, had stopped leaving calling cards. The slab-room smelled of carbolic and honest work. He had hired Wick back in the hungry month for the only qualification that mattered, which was that the boy had asked, at the interview, what happened to the ones nobody claimed, and had not liked the answer, and had stayed anyway.

Old Nesta Fenn came in on the Wednesday, dock-widow, eighty-one, dead of age and nothing else, which was its own kind of festival, and he prepped her with the care he had been taught and had taught onward: line, grain, one motion where three would do. The new assistant, Wick, sixteen, all elbows and questions, held the tray and watched the counting.

"Why twice?" Wick asked, when Soren ran the instruments a second time. "You always do it twice."

"Because the dead are owed exactness," Soren said. "And because a woman named Vann once counted me twice, and it kept me alive. Count, Wick. Twice. She held a door for three dozen of us so the habit could survive her."

Corwin came by at midweek with the last of her kit in a canvas roll: a whetstone worn into a smile, a folding knife with a Chirurgeon's mark, and her spare counter, the backup she had carried for twenty years and never needed, until now, in the sense that she would never need it again. "She'd want it counted on," Corwin said, and could not finish the sentence, and did not have to. Soren put the spare in the ledger drawer with Marrow's note, and the drawer by then was becoming, without his noticing, a reliquary.

On the sill by the sink, Ilse's brass tally-counter ticked. It had ticked since the Choir's Crossing, small and regular and unhurried, and he had stopped asking what it counted. He liked to think it counted what came back. In his vest pocket his mother's watch kept time now, ticking properly for the first time in seven years, and the wedding ring hung at his neck with its worn engraving against his skin, and between the three of them the room felt guarded, the way a city feels guarded when the fog goes and everyone can finally see the gates.

A crate arrived by the noon cart, spendthrift-heavy, packed in straw: sea-salt caramels, two stone of them at least, and a note in gray ink on gray paper.

For the man who ended the market's only famine. No charge. (Truth: everyone owes someone. Today it is my pleasure to owe you.) — M.

Marrow never lied and never told the whole truth. Soren ate a caramel, decided the caramels were the whole truth, and put the note in the ledger drawer with everything else he intended to keep.

He still walked the docks at dusk and counted the lights, because the habit was the man and the man had promised.

Two hundred and eleven lights the first week of the sun, and he counted them twice each evening from the harbor steps, and liked the two hundred and twelfth best because it was new: a lamp hung by some unknown hand on the pilings under the fish exchange, burning for nobody in particular, which is the definition of a vigil and also of a decoration, depending on the town. The Circle had offered him a seat three times by post, each letter more embarrassingly deferential, and he had declined three times by the same post, each reply shorter, and on the third occasion Wick had asked him why he kept refusing power that everyone agreed was his.

"Because I've laid out what power leaves on the slab," Soren said. "It comes in here looking exactly like an accident."

Wick considered this with his whole face, which at sixteen is still under construction. "Then why keep the veto?" he said.

"Because I'm cheaper than a king," Soren said, "and easier to bury."

The courier came at the turn of the tide, in Concord gray, boots salt-white to the knee from a ship that had sailed farther than any ship Soren had ever seen in the harbor. The letter was in a house hand, formal, waxed, and heavy with distance: House Ondrey, of Vesperquay, apothecaries, keepers of the far signal, the Concord's oldest cousin across the water. Their bloom-glass, dark for forty years, had sung in the night, the letter said. The same gray-gold note, the letter said, that had run through the roots of Greyharbor when the canopy re-seeded, and the house's signal-keepers had stood a whole watch around it with their hats off, the way men do at a birth.

The courier had the look of a man who had been briefed to expect a god and had found, instead, a sink and a ledger drawer, and was visibly uncertain which was the disappointment.

The paper was good rag, the wax a deep green-black, and the seal was a seven-lobed bloom drawn in one unbroken stroke. He sat down on the stool without deciding to, because he had last seen that sign in a sealed corridor, in a wall of the Hollow, and in a memory that had cost a god forty years of waiting to deliver.

The closing lines had come loose from their formality, the way writing does when a hand stops being an office's and starts being a person's:

We are given to understand the count stands at six of seven. The signal was answered here too. The house would correspond with the Seventh Seed, and begs him to forgive the impropriety of a first letter sent at dawn.

Soren read it twice at the window, in sunlight, with the sea visible for the first time in seven years and Ilse's counter ticking on the sill behind him like a small determined heart. Somewhere south or east or past the edge of the charts, another house had watched a dark vial wake up and sing, and had thought of a seed.

He folded the letter along its creases and put it in the ledger drawer with Marrow's note and the memory of a rearguard, then stood a while at the window, doing the thing he did instead of praying, which was counting: the gulls, the masts, the bells still ringing wrong, the seven years, the six of seven. The count came out the same every time, which is the property of true counts, and he found he did not mind the open line in it. Open lines are just work that has not started. He had been a mortuary man long enough to know the difference between an ending and a sending-off.

Somewhere across the water, a seed-signal had been answered there too.
