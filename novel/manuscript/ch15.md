## Chapter 15 — Court of Lanterns

Marrow sold him the masque the way Marrow sold everything, at a fair price and a foul angle. The Court held its Molt-masque at dark of moon in the Grand Canopy, guests admitted by scent, singly, no retinues, and the phial Dax's buyer had left on the boiler brick would open the way for exactly one mortal nose.

"The Court speaks in perfume and debt," Marrow said, wrapping the phial back in its wool as though it were fragile and Soren was not. "Somewhere up there the debts are written down, love. Ledgers. The Court adores a ledger. Now buy a mask you can afford to lose."

"If the Court smells a Concord gravid among them," Ilse said that night, "they will not escort him out."

"They will not smell one," Soren said. "They will smell one of their own, on me, worn as a guest wears a house's colors. And whatever is written in those ledgers, my name is in it now. Ashlane taught me that. I would rather read my page than wait to be read."

He went up alone, because scent-guests came singly and because he counted what he would owe anyone who covered him, and he was tired of spending other people.

"Wear the phial at the throat," Marrow had said, closing the deal with a stamp of wax he did not explain. "And mind whose house scent you are wearing. Moltwater is the Molted Court's own blend. Wren's blend, if we are naming names, and at a masque names are the one currency never spent." He had smiled at whatever his customer's face did with that. "You will be announced as a guest of her house whether she likes it or not. Try to survive the introduction."

The Fold over the Opera Stairs opened in the theater district's oldest ghost light, and folded him out onto a ledge of living wood above the Grand Canopy, and the Canopy did what the Hollow always did to him: it made the city above feel like the sketch and this the finished engraving. Lantern-mites hung in tame galaxies over the walkways, and their light did not flicker so much as breathe. Gall-balconies ringed a hall of vaulted branches. Chitin carriages drew up along a spine of grown road, their harness bells pitched for the aching ear, and when a black carriage rolled past with its windows waxed opaque, the whole gangway bowed to the wax without being asked, a field of shoulders dipping like wheat. Music came from a reed-organ grown into the wall, breathing chords with the patience of a tide. Courtiers poured up the gangways in masks and borrowed faces, and the borrowed faces were the thing that stopped his feet: he watched a Mummer hand a smile across like a hat, and a lady put it on over her own expression the way one wears another's coat.

His kill-sense opened out of habit and pointed at nothing, and this nothing was different from the Fields' nothing. Nothing in the Canopy would die first because the Court did not die. It moulted. He felt that in his sternum like standing in a room with no floor and being told it was a dance.

He went down the gangway wearing a slate mask with a chipped brow, and the Moltwater phial cracked open under his collar, and the Court admitted him the way a body admits its own blood. Nobody looked at him twice. Twice was what he got.

The masque orbited an empty dais. That was the trick of it, the thing that made his skin crawl: every dancer, every servant, every lantern-bearing child kept one shoulder angled toward the dais and never, ever turned a full back to it, and the dais held nothing but a standing lantern that no one looked at directly. The Lantern King's court orbited his silence like water finds a drain. Once, passing the dais, an old courtier bowed to the emptiness and breathed into another courtier's cupped palm a mouthful of scent, and the other bowed lower, a debt paid, and the ledger of it lived in both their bodies.

He kept to the edges and did his trade's work on the hall: counted the exits, counted the guards, counted the carriages still drawing up. The masks made every face a rumor. A lady with a heron's beak drank one sip from a vial of memory and wept borrowed tears down a borrowed cheek, and the courtier beside her accepted the weeping with a bow, a debt settled, and moved on hungry for the next.

The Ledger Gallery stood off the hall through an arch of fused antlers: standing cases of pale hide bindings, rack after rack, curators of none. The Court wrote everything. That was the indecency of it, Soren thought, working the first case's lantern-wax seal with a mortician's blade. The Court counted souls as stock and then had the patience to keep books about it.

He found the genome-ledger on the third rack, its spine lettered in the fair round hand he had seen in Ashlane, and inside, columns of lineages, rents paid in memories and in children, notes on graft-rates. A lineage of harbor ferriers rented out by the quarter-century and renewed by lot. A breeding of Choir-voices with the venues listed like concert programs. Margins annotated in two hands, one patient, one young and furious, and one column headed with a pressed sprig and the word SEEDS. Seven rows. The seventh row was the newest ink in the book.

He cased it like a folio and turned to leave, and the gallery's lamp-light leaned toward him, and a voice like poured wine said, "At last."

She stood between the racks in layered green-gray silk that moved like moth wings, masked in birch-white, and behind her the two gallery sentries were already walking away, dismissed by a breath.

"You smell of mortuary soap and mortal brine," Lady Wren said, "and beneath that, seed. Seventh of seven, come up out of the fog to rob my library. Do you know what I admire about the Concord's tailoring? The seams."

He put the blade's tip between them, an old habit of his trade, and she looked at it the way one looks at a child's drawing on a court wall.

"You are going to offer me something," Soren said. "Thieves do not get waited for. Patrons get waited for."

"Apprentice yourself to me." She took his mask off with two fingers, the way one takes a lid off a jar, and studied his face without surprise, without appetite, with a horrible kindness. "I will teach you her alphabet. The one the Concord burns books to forget. I knew Maerwen when she was young and wrong about everything except the important thing."

"What thing?"

"That a starving thing can be fed differently." Wren let the mask drop to his chest, still in his fingers. "Your Concord writes its hypotheses on her work and seals her corridors. My Court writes its appetite on everything it touches. And you, little seed, are being counted by both, and I am the only party at this masque offering you a salary."

"The Concord pays in purpose," Soren said.

"The Concord pays in purpose and collects in everything else. Ask its fourth-year recruits what their scripts cost them. Ask Ilse Vann what her hand bought, and who signed the order that sent her somewhere a hand was the price." The birch mask tilted. "I made this offer once before, to a botanist with your jaw and better boots. She chose the leash and called it duty. Go and look at the shelf her duty sleeps on."

She glanced at the case he had emptied, unhurried, and gestured at it with two fingers, a host waving a guest's oversight through.

"Take the genome-ledger you have already taken. Debts disclosed are debts I no longer owe, and that page has been owed to daylight for a long time. Deliver it where it will do the most damage. You have a talent for exits; I have watched you practice them."

"What do you want for it?"

"For now, only this. Ask me about your mother. You came all this way. It would be a waste of a good nose not to."

The gallery's lanterns leaned in. Somewhere beyond the antler arch the reed-organ changed its tide. Soren counted his pulse twice and made himself say it.

He thought of the drawing lying face down on the floor by his bed, the stranger with the kind mouth. He had come to the Canopy to read his own page in the Court's books. It had not once occurred to him that the books might hold her current address.

"Is she alive?"

Lady Wren leaned close, kind as a knife going in.

"Your mother is not dead, little seed. She is inventory."
