## Chapter 24 — The Rootgate March

They went down at slack water, through a Fold pinned open behind the fish exchange with iron rings and hymn-chalk, and Soren counted them through the way he counted everything: out loud, in pairs, twice.

Forty-one souls. Nine burn-teams with saltfire satchels waxed tight against their spines. Six codex-walkers under Predator and Chirurgeon codes, their coat-collars chalked with their licences. Ilse Vann at his shoulder, iron-gray braid coiled close to her skull, ruined left hand tucked into her belt like a tool she had finished with, brass tally-counter clicking in her right fist as she counted the same forty-one from the far end of the line.

"Forty-one," she said.

"Forty-one," Soren said. "You're a beat behind me."

"I'm a beat certain. There is a difference, and one day it will keep you alive."

The Fold let them through and shut like water over a dropped coin, and the root-roads took them.

The roads ran beneath the harbor like the ribs of a drowned cathedral. Root vaults arched away overhead into a dark that ate lantern-light a few feet past its edge, and between the ribs hung mossed bone, furred gray-green, older than the city above it and patient in the way of things that had never needed to hurry. Somewhere far overhead the quarantine bells struck the half hour, and the sound came down through forty feet of stone and root thinned to almost nothing, a bell ringing inside a wool coat. Brackish rain fell where the harbor leaked through. It tasted of ship's bottom and rust.

"Saltfire," said Brack, burn-master of the third team, a cannonball of a man with singed eyebrows and a voice that ground like a millstone. "Burns wet. Burns in the dark. Burns root down to an ash that won't sprout. And don't ask me what's in it. The recipe is a Concord grave, and I am the last man alive who can recite it, so if I die down here, mourn me properly."

"We'll bury you twice," Soren said. "Count on it."

Brack laughed like gravel going down a chute, and the column moved.

The gallows-walkers found them at the third mile, or the walkers' gallows did: cocoons hanging from the vault ribs like pale fruit, swaying in a draft that had no business existing underground. Soren felt them wake through the kill-sense, a cold unfolding behind his sternum, and the sense rang like a struck glass: six heartbeats in the dark above, and every one of them already dying.

"Vault," he said, and the column stopped breathing.

They came down on leg-joints like scythes, gallows-walkers, carrion giants, dry chitin and moss with faces pressed into the undersides of their heads like faces pushed into wax. The first took a burn-team man off the road and up into the dark before his satchel straps had finished sliding from his shoulders. His scream went up with him and came down shorter.

Soren did not fight like a soldier. He arranged, the way he arranged bodies on a slab: find the line, respect the grain, make one motion that would have taken three. The kill-sense showed him which heartbeat would stop first and his body simply went there, Fold-step carrying him between one blink and the next, chalk-grey limbs parting under the long knife he kept for exactly this weather. Brack's teams poured saltfire at the roots of the largest walker and the reagent burned green-white and hungry, hissing where the brackish rain hit it. A Chirurgeon walker named Corwin held a crushed arm together with bare hands and a hissed gene-fragment, sealing the wound like wax on a letter.

Four walkers down. One man up and gone. Forty remained.

"Forty," Ilse said, clicking her counter.

"Forty," Soren said, and found he could not say anything else for a while.

* * *

The glasswings came at a gallery where the road widened into a hall, and the hall was full of them, though at first there seemed to be nothing there at all. That was the point of glasswings. Wings like window-glass, bodies like a held breath. They did not drink blood. They drank dreams, and they were drinking as the column walked.

The men began to smile. Soren saw it happen: a burn-team man slowing, head tilting, lantern drooping, the deep unguarded smile of a bridegroom. Another left the line entirely and walked toward a wall with his arms out, saying a woman's name twice, tenderly, like a prayer that had already been answered.

Smoke-sight saved the rest of them. Through the Eighth Essence gland behind his eyes the hall stopped being empty: the air stitched with translucent wings, hundreds of them, each moth a glass knife with soft edges, each one fastened to a dreaming man's forehead with its mouth. He could see exactly where the dream went in.

"Lamps down. Oil out. Burn a line across the road and walk it behind me," Soren said. "Nobody follows a nice thought tonight. If it feels like a good idea, it's a glasswing."

They burned the trench and walked it, and the swarm came apart along the fire like a window coming out of its frame, wings catching, moths dropping and shattering with small bright sounds. Most of the smiling woke at the heat and wept, which Soren considered the correct response to being robbed.

One did not. A young tallyman named Pim walked into a side gallery with his lantern held before him like an offering, still smiling, still saying the woman's name, and the dark folded itself around him with a courtesy that was worse than any scream. Corwin started after him. Soren caught the man's shoulder and shook his head, because the gallery had gone quiet in the particular way that means a room has already eaten.

"Thirty-nine," Ilse said. Her counter clicked twice, for him and for Pim both.

* * *

They heard the Choir's Crossing long before they saw it. The road ended at a span of woven root, two cables braided into a walkway, stretched across black water where a drowned street still showed its chimney pots. And from the far dark, moving along the cables like weather wearing voices, the Choir came singing.

It sang in a child's voice first, a tuneless skipping song. Then it sang in Dax's voice.

"You owe me for the rope, Vale," the dark sang, cheerful, precise, dead. "You owe me for the rope."

Soren's stomach turned to ballast. Beside him, Ilse had gone the color of the bone in the vaults, because the Choir had found a voice she knew better: her brother's, counting a harvest-team out loud, one, two, three, the count that had never come home from the first tide.

"It steals what it hears the Fade take," she said. Her voice was level. Her counter was not; it clicked and clicked in her fist.

The plan was the plan. The cell crossed the span, the rearguard held its head, and saltfire cut the cables behind them before the Choir reached the crossing. What the plan had not specified, because plans are written by people who count everyone home, was who would stand at the span-head while three dozen souls crossed a singing dark.

"You're the point," Ilse said. "Points go through. Doors get held. I have been holding doors since before you could shave."

"Vann."

"Vale." She said his name twice, because she counted everything twice, and because once would not have been enough for either of them. She pressed the brass tally-counter into his hand and closed his fingers over it with her maimed left hand, the ruined one, so that he would know the gift was the whole of her. "Hold my count. When it ticks, that's me being certain. And Soren. Count the rest for me."

He crossed with the cell, and the Choir sang around them in the voices of everyone either of them had ever buried, and Ilse Vann stood at the span-head with a saltfire satchel in her good hand like a woman waiting for a coach. He looked back exactly once, at the last of the planks, because looking twice would have been more than either of them could spend.

The light when she fired it was green-white and total. The span fell. The Choir's song went into the black water and kept singing anyway, muffled, patient, counting.

Ilse did not come back.

In Soren's fist, for the first time since the fish exchange, the tally-counter began to tick on its own. Small. Regular. Unhurried. Like a clock that had been waiting forty years for permission.

He counted the ticks while he walked. They did not stop.

* * *

An hour past the crossing, the road widened into a dark that their lanterns could not put a shape to, and the engineers' chalk began to lie. Every line they drew across the stone came back curved. The walls had stopped being walls; they had begun, gently, to be the inside of something. The air warmed. It smelled of milk and iron, of a thing newborn and a thing midwinter, and under the smell ran a pulse so broad and slow that at first they mistook it for the sea they were marching under.

"Where's the gate?" Brack said, finally saying what all of them were not saying. "Root-anchor, warden-seal, a door, a seam. Where's the blasted gate?"

Soren put his palm flat against the warm woven dark. Far beneath it, something enormous turned over in its sleep and settled again, and every hair on his arms rose to attention, and the counter ticked against his hip like a small determined heart.

It was not a gate. It was a nest.
