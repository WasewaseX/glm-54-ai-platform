## Chapter 26 — Court of One

The root-roads behind him filled with lantern-light and the tick of count-irons, and Soren ran down into the dark with his blood keeping a bad rhythm and his shoulder on fire where an iron had grazed him. The weapon did not tear. It wrote. A count-iron branded a running tally into what it struck, little notches that itched like debt, and the tallyman who had marked him was already calling his count to the others: running, eastward branch, wounded, slowing.

Ilse had taught him to run wounded, back in the weeks when he had still been a recruit worth correcting. Run bent. Lead with the hurt side; they aim at your strength. He ran bent, and the notches on his shoulder itched their itch, and behind him the pursuit kept its spacing like something that had drilled, because the Court's vanguard drilled the way water drills stone: patiently, and for centuries.

Twelve molted knights. Eight of Crane's tallymen, the ones whose irons had come up level without a wobble. Behind all of it, far back, the battle on the rim went on without him, and he could not let himself hear it.

He could hear the Lacuna.

The organ behind his sternum turned its attention toward the hunt the way a man turns in bed, and opened, and asked. It had asked before, quietly, at every tier, and each time he had paid on the installment plan: a sip of spilled shadow here, a consumed creature there, a memory set aside like a coin he would get back later. The dark around him was rich with the essence of the tide that hunted him. He could pay everything at once, and go up a tier in the middle of a battle, and let the Predator genome write itself the last of the way into his flesh.

Or he could keep running at a wounded man's pace down a road full of glass-limbed knights, and lose.

"I was always going to die of work," Soren said out loud, to the road, to the tick at his hip, and stopped running, and paid.

The growth fever hit like the harbor coming down. Heat first, then light, then the sense of being a page on which something enormous was writing quickly. He stayed on his feet because a mortuary assistant learns to stay on his feet around things that are not his business to fall over. The new gland opened behind his eyes like a third eyelid, and the world resolved into counts.

The True Tally, the old texts would have called it, if the old texts had dared. Every knight wore its count like a smell: ninth molting, third service, unhurt, certain. Each tallyman: fourth iron, second oath, wavering. And the tide itself, the whole ascension host pouring down the root-road, resolved into a single sum, and the sum was not free-floating. It was moored.

He saw it with the new sense as plainly as he had ever seen a wound: a chain of mooring-spires driven along the road at intervals, living root struck through with lantern-glass, each one singing one held note, and all the notes converging into the herald's voice. The tide was not an army. It was a ledger, and the herald was the hand that held the page open. Break the count, and the sum went to zero everywhere at once.

The first thing he read with the new sense, because you read yourself first, was his own line. One mortician, sixth essence, Predator to the root of him, frightened, bleeding, alone, and still counting. He noted the line, filed it, and moved on, and the filing was the strength of it. A count you keep is an anchor. A count that keeps you is a god.

Then the Fade came for him, the way it always came: politely, taking the far things first.

He felt it lift the far shelf of himself. The sea, long gone, was only the absence where a sea had been. His mother's face, long gone, was a doorway he had learned not to look at. Now it went deeper. Ilse's voice began to thin, the exact pitch of her "Forty-one" going gray at the edges, and Dax's grin, that quick crooked brightness, started coming apart like wet paper.

Soren held on the way men hold on in floods, to whatever is nearest and does not float.

The counter ticked at his hip, and the tick came in twos, because Vann counted twice.

He pulled the cord at his neck and the wedding ring came up, Dax's mother's ring, and inside the band, scratched tiny and worn nearly smooth, the words he had never shown anyone: for my boy.

"Vann counts twice," he said, to the dark, to himself, to the things unwinding him. "Merrow's mother wrote 'for my boy.' The rest can burn. Not these."

He chose the burn list himself, then, deliberately, the way you sign an amputation paper: the color of the mortuary's morning light, the name of the publican on Sallow Court, the taste of the good tea. Names, faces, prices. The Fade took what was offered and left him the two things he refused to offer, and the refusal was the whole of the man, and the whole of the man held.

The Fade receded from those two small objects the way a hand recedes from a stove, and Soren stood up out of the fever with the Sixth Essence moving in him like a second tide, and turned to face the road he had come down.

He went through the vanguard the way he went through a shift at the mortuary: by the count, in order, nothing wasted. The kill-sense told him which heartbeat would stop first and his body went there; Fold-step put him between one blink of a knight's glass eye and the next; the long knife found joints because joints are where the count of a body can be divided. Tallymen tried to mark him and marked the air. A knight took his left forearm to the glass and he repaid it by counting the creature's own leg out of true at the knee. He did not fight like a soldier, because soldiers are trying to win. He was trying to finish.

One of Crane's tallymen he caught alive, disarmed, young enough that his oath still showed. Soren put the long knife away and left him his life and an errand. "Run home," Soren said. "Tell the Primate the count is broken. And tell him Vann counted twice, and one of the twos was him." The boy ran, glad to be sent anywhere at all, and Soren let the mercy cost what it cost.

The herald stood at the first mooring-spire with its lantern face tipped toward him, patient as a cancelled appointment.

They stood a moment like two clerks at the same window, one of them holding the ledger and the other holding the knife, and the mooring-spire between them sang its held note down into both their bones.

"The Warden asked for you by face," it said.

"It can have my face," Soren said. "I know where it lives."

The True Tally read the creature whole, and he hated how well it read: one herald, ninth molting, holder of the count, alone in its office, tired. He killed it anyway, once, cleanly, and then counted twice, because a woman with a maimed hand had taught him that the second count is where certainty lives.

The note the herald had held went out of the world like a finger lifted from a harp string. Down the miles of root-road the mooring-spires went dark one after another, and the sum of the tide came apart, and the ascension host stopped mid-stride, masterless. Knights stood like furniture. Some knelt. The hunt was over because the arithmetic was over, and there was nothing left on the road but Soren and his own breath and the tick at his hip.

He took inventory of himself, because inventory was the man. No sea. No face. Her voice, barely, a gray thread. His own name, for now. The counter and the ring, and the fever's new eyes, and the old knife.

And warmth. Ahead, down the last of the root-roads, the woven dark of the nest stood open the way a door stands open when the house within has heard you come up the path. He had not asked. He had not knocked. He was being let in.

The woven wall came apart in front of him without being touched, coil by soft coil, the way a hand opens when it means to give something rather than take it.

The Warden opened the nest for him. Gently.
