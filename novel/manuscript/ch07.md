## Chapter 7 — The Missing Sixth Floor

Nine days after the card, Soren went back to work.

The Concord had wanted him moved into the tally-house dormitory, permanent, with his meals counted and his sleep counted, and he had refused in writing, twice, and then once more aloud, and Ilse had backed him with an argument he would remember: "A gravid who loses his daylight trade loses his alibi for being human. Let him keep the mortuary. Morticians have the best excuse in the city for smelling of the dead." So he kept Salt Row, and kept the ledger voice, and Greech, who missed nothing and mentioned less, said only, at the end of the first week back: "You've stopped smelling of the workshop. Whatever soap that is, don't bring it near the parish dead."

"It's new," Soren said.

"New things always are," Greech said, and went back to yelling at an invoice. Then, without looking up: "Gray coats came by Tuesday. Asking about the anniversary delivery. Asked whether the assistant was the same assistant." The pen scratched. "Told them the assistant was the same assistant, and that the assistant files like a bailiff, and they left. They didn't like the second part. Nobody likes the second part."

On the ninth night of his return, the fog came in low and close, with that faint sweetness in it that the whole city had agreed not to discuss, and the mortuary was quiet, and Soren went up the back stair to the drying loft with a lamp in one hand and a bundle of ledgers under his arm, the way he had gone up that stair four hundred times since he was seventeen.

The back stair had sixty-four steps. He had counted them on his first night in the house, when counting was all the family he had brought with him, and he had counted them a hundred times since, because stairs are honest and don't move.

Seventy-six.

He stopped on the landing that had never been there, twelve steps above the loft, and made himself do the arithmetic again, aloud, in the dead room's silence. Sixty-four steps to the loft. He had climbed seventy-six. Somewhere behind him in the dark, twelve steps had been added to a stair that had stood for ninety years, and they had been added without a sound, the way the Court put cards in pockets.

There was a door on the landing.

It was a good door, mortuary stock, black-grained, with a brass knob gone dull in the way brass goes dull only where hands have not touched it for years. There was no number on it. There was nothing on it. And through the crack under it, the light of his own lamp was bending, leaning toward the gap the way the fog leaned toward his window on the morning he woke up with two heartbeats, and the smell coming through the gap was crushed flowers and cold wax.

He knew what a Fold was now. He had walked into one through his own bedroom wall with a fire-iron and a bad attitude. He stood on the impossible landing, being careful, being mortal, being the man who counts, and he thought about the wall of the missing, and about who in Greyharbor kept gardens in impossible places.

He opened the door, because in the end he was always going to open the door.

* * *

The sixth floor was a garden.

It was loam underfoot, black and soft and cold as the inside of a grave, and it went back much further than the building went, much further than the block went. Overhead hung a canopy of pale caps, tiers of them, gentle as the underside of a mushroom, sifting a light that did not come from anywhere. And in rows that a gardener's pride had laid out, in beds that a gardener's knee had weeded, teeth were growing.

Hundreds of them. Human teeth, and some not, planted upright in the loam like bulbs, root up, crown up, furred with a moss so fine it might have been velvet. They gave off a hum. Not a sound, exactly: a word-shaped warmth, each tooth humming with something that pressed on the ear the way a word presses on the mind when it is said too softly to be heard. Last words, he thought. He had a professional instinct for last words, and the whole field of them was humming at once, and his kill-sense, for the first time since he had grown it, told him nothing at all, because nothing in the garden was owed. Everything in it had already been paid.

Soren stood among the beds and counted, because that was what he had instead of screaming. Three hundred and thirty-one. Each root carried a tally mark filed into the tooth itself, neat as a clerk's stroke. He did not know the count they referred to. He wrote down the number in his notebook, because some counts keep you sane by agreeing to be kept.

Once, only once, he crouched at the edge of the third bed, where a tooth smaller than the rest hummed warm against his palm, and let himself begin to listen. The hum sharpened as he leaned, gathering itself toward a word the way fog gathers toward a lamp, and the word was almost a shape he knew, was about to be a voice, was a heartbeat away from being somebody's last breath said out loud into a stranger's hand, and Soren sat back on his heels and stopped listening, because he understood, in the marrow the trade had built into him, that a last word is a thing you are given or a thing you take, and he had no right to take this one. The tooth hummed on, patient, unspent.

The wallpaper was on the stairwell behind him. That was the wrong thing to say and it was the true thing: the wall he had come in through was papered, and the paper ran up the wall and along the wall, climbing vine-work, flower after flower after flower, and the flowers were spelled. He saw it the way you see the second image in a puzzle picture. The vines were letters. The garden was a book, and the book was reading him.

At the height of a standing man's eyes, in letters of vine and tendril, in a hand older than the house and younger than the fog, the wallpaper said:

SOREN VALE.

And beneath the name, in the same patient growth:

THE SPOREFALL WAS PLANTED. ASK THE CONCORD WHO SOWED.

He read it four times. He copied it twice, once in his notebook and once by saying it aloud in the dead room's silence, because a thing said out loud is harder for a quiet world to change. When he spoke, every tooth in the garden chimed, faintly, together, like glasses at a funeral breakfast, and the hum of them rearranged into a single held note that he felt in his new organ like a hand laid flat on his sternum, and then it let go.

In the paper, in among the vines, there was a face. He had missed it because it was only a face the way the walker had been only rigging: eyes closed, mouth a folded leaf, breathing. He was sure it was breathing, because the vine-work around it swelled and settled, swelled and settled, with the patience of something that had been standing in that wall since before the Sporefall, waiting for a man who could see Folds to climb a stair that did not exist and read its name in the paper.

He did not ask it anything. He thought about it. He stood in the garden of the dead's discarded teeth with the question in his mouth like a hot coin, and he thought about the word sowed, and he thought about the Concord's wall, and about Ilse's counter, and about how much he did not yet know about who counted for whom.

"Who sowed?" he said at last.

The face did not wake. The teeth hummed. The light sifted down like flour through cloth, and the garden smelled of crushed flowers and cold wax, of anniversary fog, of perfume, and Soren Vale, novice of the Veridian Concord, stood alone in the sixth floor of a five-floor building and understood that he had just been handed the bill for something his city had been paying on since the sky came down.

He backed out. He closed the good door on brass that no hand had touched. He counted his way down: sixty-four steps to the loft, and the count came out honest, and he stood in the drying loft among the hanging linens with his lamp up and his heart going like a pressman's wheel.

In the loft's dark window, his reflection turned away from the stair.

He did not. Not for one heartbeat, and then not for two, and the reflection stood there in the glass with its lamp raised, looking up at a landing that was no longer there, patient as arithmetic, keeping its own count, and it let him walk off alone into the house below.
