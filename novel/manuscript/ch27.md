## Chapter 27 — The Warden's Succession

Inside, there was no dark to speak of. There was light the way there is light in deep water, sourceless and everywhere, gray-gold, and it came from the walls, and the walls were faces.

Soren stood in the core of the Warden and understood, with the calm of a man finally shown the back of the clock he had been repairing for a year, what the god was made of. Not stone. Not flesh, exactly. The core was a cathedral whose stones were faces, every size from a coin to a cartwheel, set in living root by living root, and every face was asleep and singing, and the singing was the architecture. The Warden was not a creature wearing a city of the dead. The Warden was a congregation. Every memory the Fade had ever taken was in the walls, wearing its face, and the god was the space their singing made.

He looked for the faces he knew, because that is what people do in crowds. A bride, mid-vow, young enough that her ribbons had been fashionable twice over. A lamplighter with his ladder. A child caught in the exact middle of a laugh, the laugh kept like a fly in amber. And the singing beneath all of them was not mournful, which was the worst of it. It was patient, and full, and horribly like a city at evening.

And toward him, out of that congregation, came a face he had sold to the dark without meaning to.

He could not have described his mother to save his life. That was the Fade's oldest theft, taken in a corridor of House Blackwing while he slept. So there was something unbearable in the way the face arrived and fit itself into the exact shape of the hole he had, a woman of forty-some years with tired eyes, and he knew it was her the way you know a room you were born in: not by the furniture. By the fit.

"You show me her face," Soren said, "and I can't even tell if you're flattering me."

"I do not wear it to wound," the Warden said. It spoke with her mouth and with a thousand others, a chorus in one throat, and it did not sound like a trick. It sounded like a library. "I wear it because she is here. She is the newest of my great holdings and the oldest of my griefs. You asked once if the canopy had a king. It has a shelf, and she is on it, and she has been waiting to be read."

The throne stood at the crown of the core, grown up out of the floor like a thing that had been rehearsing for an audience that never arrived: a seat of root and glass, high-backed, patient. The succession, offered now in the open, with the tide broken and the herald dead and no one left to speak for the canopy but the canopy.

"Sit," the Warden said. "Be the mind of it. Keep the city as a garden, fed on schedule, ruled by count. You will never be hungry again, and nothing you love will be taken unless you take it. This was the offer I made you in dreams. I make it now in person, and I will not make it again."

"I'm a mortician," Soren said. "Hunger is my whole business. And I've seen what your garden does to the flowers." He put his hand on the seat once. It was warm. He took his hand back. "I didn't come for your chair. I came for her last."

"You will dream for the city," the Warden said. "They will bring you their forgetting like the sick, and you will be kind, and it will cost you nothing, because nothing costs a god."

"I've buried paupers," Soren said. "Nothing costs a god the way nothing costs a debt. Somebody always holds the paper."

The Warden was quiet for the length of one full turn of the great pulse beneath them.

"Then walk my shelves," it said.

The shelves were the walls. The Hollow was built, literally, from everything the Fade had ever taken, and it was stored, and it was sorted, and it was worn. Soren walked a gallery of strangers' Tuesdays. He passed a shelf that hummed and breathed salt, and his chest ached on a hinge he had no memory of owning, and he understood that somewhere on it was a sea he had sold for a vial, and he kept walking, because if he stopped he would open every jar in the building and there would be nothing left of him to carry the next one.

Here and there a shelf was marked in a hand rather than a glyph, inked onto the root with a stylograph: catalog numbers, dates, and once, small, in a tired lowercase, the word mine. He did not stop for that either. Stopping was a country he might never have walked out of.

He found her at the center, because he was a Seed, and Seeds are keys.

It was a case of gray glass the size of a heart, planted in the wall by hand, waxed shut and marked with a glyph: a seven-lobed bloom drawn in one unbroken stroke. The mark from the old gene-script. The mark from the sealed corridor. His mother's sign.

"Read her," the Warden said. "She wrote it to be read by her children. I have waited forty years to hear it read."

The case opened like a book, and the memory began, and Soren stood inside his mother's last clear hour.

A laboratory. Younger faces than the Concord wore now, and among them a woman with ink on her fingers, writing gene-scripts in an alphabet he had been taught at House Blackwing and forbidden to use. The Seeds, seven designed lineages, and the intent, plain as a scalpel on a tray: the Lacuna was never meant to be a mouth. It was meant to be a bridge. The giving-script was written to grow a Hollow that fed on memory offered, not memory taken; to re-seed the canopy with human remembering until the hunger turned inward and died of fullness. Gravid as gardeners. Symbiosis, not feeding. She had designed the cure before the city knew it was a patient.

And then the two readings, unfolding like hands dealing a deck he could not stop. The Court had found the work first and read the Seeds as feed-stock, and taught the Warden appetite, feeding it on the forgotten for a generation until taking was the only grammar it knew. The Concord had found the Court's interest and read the Seeds as a bomb, sealed the corridor, buried the giving-script under doctrine and fear, and called the burial caution. The Court fed it. The Concord locked it. Both starved it, and both, in their different churches, prayed it would stay small.

"Did it hurt you," Soren asked, when he could speak, "keeping her?"

"Keeping is all I know," the Warden said. "It is the hurting I am learning to stop."

Her voice, near the end, recorded in stages, because she had known she was being eaten while she worked:

"If you are hearing this, you counted true, and the count was long. I am sorry for the length of it. They tell me I have a son. I have written this before, in versions of me that no longer exist, and each of them remembered less and meant it more. Build the bridge. Do not feed it. Do not lock it. Teach it to ask."

The memory folded itself closed. The case sat open and empty and warm.

Soren put his hand flat on the wall beside it, which was the closest he could come to holding anyone. Somewhere in the cathedral of faces, a version of his mother that the dark had half-eaten had spent her last clear hour writing instructions for a child she was told she had. He decided, with the part of himself that was still a mortician, that he would finish her sentence for her. That was what assistants were for.

Around him the singing changed. Every face in the cathedral turned toward him, the way they had turned once before across a horizon of dream, and the Warden came out of the congregation and, wearing a dead woman's face, knelt.

For the first time in an age, it asked instead of took.

"Teach me," it said, in a chorus of everyone it had ever eaten. "Teach me to ask."
