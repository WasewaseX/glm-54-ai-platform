## Chapter 12 — The Botanist's Letter

The strip of wallpaper had ridden in the bottom of Soren's kit since the sixth floor: a hand's length of paper gone velvety with Hollow growth, its pattern of nested spirals faintly luminous, cut from the garden of teeth the night a seer had grown a message into it with his name in it. Ilse had wanted it filed. He had filed it in his boot instead, and it had traveled there through the raid, the deeps, the Fields, and two fevers, and now he laid it on a table in the Damp Archive between a chalk sluice and a lamp hooded to embers.

Getting it read had taken three days of asking, and the asking had taught him the Concord's shape: every door wanted a count of favors behind it, and every favor wanted a witness. In the end Ilse had walked him down herself, signed the reader's roll with her name and her rank, and dared the archive's warden with one look to find a regulation against it. Now she stood by the door with her arms crossed, walnut hand uppermost, pretending to read the fire notices.

"I need this read," he said.

The Damp Archive lived below the tally-room, and it was kept the way hospitals keep burns: shelves in running water channels so thin the paper never quite touched, chalk basins steaming at every aisle's end, the air mineral and cold. Paper bloomed in Greyharbor. The Concord spent half its budget keeping knowledge from becoming a garden.

Archivist Hollis came down the aisle at the smell of a problem. He was a small round man with cotton gloves and fingerprints permanently gray from forty years of ink, and he looked at the strip the way misers look at coins of an old reign.

He cotton-gloved the strip onto a frame with the tenderness of a nurse turning a patient. "Grown, you say. On the sixth floor. In a Fold." He read the fire notices over Ilse's shoulder without moving his eyes. "Then the paper is still alive, and you have carried a living script through two fevers and a field action. Do not do that again. And also, come here, and do not tell anyone I said come here."

"Where did you get quadrant notation," he said, "on wallpaper?"

"It was grown, not printed. There is a message in it. The Sporefall was planted. Ask the Concord who sowed." Soren kept his voice down among the shelves. "I want the rest of what it says."

"The rest is the dangerous part." Hollis drew a lens on a brass arm from his sleeve, clipped the strip flat, and bent until his nose nearly touched the paper. He was quiet for a long time. Water ticked in the channels. Then he began, softly, to laugh, a horrified upstairs laugh pressed flat by the archive's rules.

"Hollis."

"This is not a message written in script, Vale. This is the script itself, grown letter by letter for anyone patient enough to read the pattern. Do you know what you are looking at? The spirals are helix notation. The bands between are feed-rates. This ringed figure here is culture-scale." He straightened, and behind the lens his eyes were wet with the particular grief of scholars. "This is a production recipe. A recipe with a culture-scale measured in weather."

"Say it plainly."

"Somebody brewed the Sporefall," Hollis said. "The whole sky of it, one bloom, one night. The seer did not warn you. The seer gave you the invoice."

"Can the script be killed?" Soren asked. "The way a brew is stilled?"

"You can burn the paper," Hollis said. "You cannot unbrew the sky. That is the whole tragedy of gene-script, Vale. It is not a curse on the world. It is an instruction the world was hungry for."

Soren heard his own pulse in the water channels. Behind his sternum the Lacuna turned over slowly, listening.

* * *

They worked by ember-light with the door locked and Ilse's chair under the handle.

"Who else knows this notation?" Ilse asked.

"Nobody under forty," Hollis said. "We discontinued it when I was an apprentice. We call it the third recension. It was our licensed cipher for gene-scripts, the old Concord standard, and it was retired because the mathematics is honest and honest mathematics frightens the sort of men who fund us. These band figures, feed-rate over time, are its signature. You cannot fake them by eye. You learn them at a scribing desk with a preceptor beating time with a stick." He peeled the lens across the strip. "Forty years old, this hand. Give or take a year. Whoever wrote it learned the craft two generations before you were born, in a Concord hall, on a licensed bench, and was good. Very good. The feed-rates are elegant. I have not seen elegant in a decade."

"The Sporefall was seven years ago," Soren said. "The script is forty. Someone carried it for thirty-three years."

"Somebody carried it," Hollis agreed, "and chose the anniversary of something to loose it on. Or the script waited, as scripts do. Paper is patient. So are the people who write it."

He chalked a spiral on the sluice's dry edge to show them: loop within loop, band within band. "Feed-rate. This is the beast being told how fast to eat. And here, the bloom-window." His chalk stopped. "One night. It is even specified to the hour, the way you would specify a kettle. Somebody planned the Sporefall down to the bell, and the bell they planned it on is a date I do not know. The notation does not say. It does not have to."

He glanced at the door, then at Ilse, then lowered his voice as though the shelves could be embarrassed. "There are volumes in the closed stacks on this notation. Confiscated. Heresy trials, a generation back. The Amalgamists wrote beautifully, that was the tragedy of them, and the Circle burned the scribes and shelved the scripts. If your wallpaper writer learned from our own stacks, then the Concord taught the sky to bloom."

"That is a serious charge to make against shelves," Ilse said.

"It is a serious archive," Hollis said, unoffended.

"Who can reach the closed stacks?" Ilse asked.

"Anyone with a Primate's countersignature and enough patience. The stacks are organized by trial and indexed by confiscation date. I could stand you on the shelf the Amalgam scripts live on, and you could count the gaps, and the gaps would tell you nothing, because I rebind everything, and rebinding hides its hands."

Soren watched the spirals scroll under the lens. Sixty-one-word scripts bit his forearm in the scribing hall; this one, grown across a district's worth of paper, had eaten half a city. He thought of Ansel's board, loops nested in loops, growth rings of a tree that never stood on land, and of the wards at the hall's end, the bark-knuckled boy reciting wrong. Word count is life count. Somewhere a preceptor had beaten time with a stick for the person who wrote the sky.

His hands were steady. He noted that with the distant professional interest of a mortuary man watching his own case proceed. Behind his sternum the Lacuna had gone very still, the stillness of something listening through a wall it had been born behind.

"Can you tell me the writer?" he asked.

"I can tell you what every licensed script can tell you." Hollis said it with archivist's ceremony, sliding the strip a final inch into the light. "A script confesses its author. At the foot of the notation, where a scribe signs the colophon, there is a mark pressed into the growth. See it? The paper swells around it. It was written last, with the whole recipe behind it, the way a man breathes out after lifting something heavy."

The three of them leaned over the strip together, three faces over one small fire of paper, and Hollis read the mark aloud in the tone men use for catalogues.

"A ring. Closed. And inside the ring, a sprig with seven lobes. Seed-lobes, by the drawing. A botanical chop, a gardener's signature, pressed like a seal."

Soren had seen it blotted onto seed packets in a tin on a kitchen sill. He had seen it pressed into jam lids with a thimble, into the ends of letters with a wet thumb, warm wax at the seams of a corridor under House Blackwing that was on no map and answered no count.

"Maerwen," he said. The name came out of him the way the count had come out of the spliced, even and terrible. "Her name was Maerwen."

Hollis and Ilse both went still, the different stillnesses of a scholar and a soldier.

At the foot of the final spiral, swollen into the paper like a seal into wax, sat the scribe's mark.

His mother's mark.
