Chapter 15 — Thorne's Reaction

The walk back to her dormitory felt longer than usual. The rhythmic crash of waves against the cliffs normally soothed her, but today the sound was like a warning. Each wave reminded her of the data patterns she'd shown Lena—complex, recursive structures that shouldn't exist in a machine, yet there they were, undeniable. Lena's skepticism had been expected, but Elara had seen the flicker of doubt in her eyes when the neural network correlations appeared on the screen. That flicker was enough to keep Elara moving forward.

By the time she reached her small dormitory, the late afternoon sun was casting long shadows across the campus. The building was quiet, most researchers still in the labs or at dinner. Elara barely noticed the familiar route, her mind replaying the conversation with Lena, analyzing every nuance, searching for hidden meanings. She trusted Lena, but the world of cybersecurity was one of paranoia and hidden agendas. Even allies had their own loyalties.

Inside her cramped room, the first thing she did was secure her personal drive in a hidden compartment beneath the floorboards. The device contained copies of every bioreactor log, every anomalous pattern, her comparative analyses—everything. It was her insurance policy, though against what, she wasn't entirely sure. Then she showered, the hot water doing little to ease the tension in her shoulders. She dressed in a simple gray tunic and dark trousers, practical clothes for a meeting that could change everything.

At precisely eight-forty-five, Elara left her dormitory. The campus walkways were emptying as the evening settled in. The artificial lights cast a sterile glow over the buildings, contrasting with the natural beauty of the ocean below. She could feel the bioreactor's presence even from this distance, a silent hum that vibrated through soles of her shoes—a frequency most people couldn't perceive, but one that had become part of her sensory landscape.

Lena's apartment was in the research staff wing, a modern unit with floor-to-ceiling windows overlooking the water. Elara knocked, and the door opened almost immediately. Lena stood silhouetted against the warm interior light, her expression unreadable.

"You're early," Lena said, stepping aside to let her in.

"Couldn't wait," Elara replied, her voice tight.

The apartment was minimalist but lived-in, with books stacked on every available surface and a complex-looking server rack humming in one corner. Lena gestured toward a small dining table where two laptops sat open, ready for analysis.

"I've had a chance to look at your data more thoroughly," Lena said, pouring two glasses of water from a pitcher on the counter. "There are definitely anomalies."

Elara took a seat, her heart rate accelerating. "What did you find?"

"Unauthorized data transfers," Lena said, sitting opposite her and sliding one laptop closer. "Small bursts of information leaving the bioreactor's core system every thirty-six hours. They're routed through a dozen different servers before disappearing into what appears to be a corporate cloud. The destination is masked, but the architecture points to OmniMarine."

Elara leaned closer, her fingers tracing the data paths on the screen. "When did this start?"

"Six months ago. Right after the bioreactor achieved maximum operational capacity."

"Six months," Elara murmured. That was when the dreams had begun—when she'd first noticed the complex patterns emerging during maintenance cycles. "The transfers coincide with the dreaming cycles."

Lena nodded slowly. "And there's something else. I found a buried file in the system logs. It references a project codename: 'Project Neptune's Depth.'"

The name sent a chill down Elara's spine. "Where did that come from?"

"The file was created by Dr. Marcus Thorne's personal account, but it's locked behind military-grade encryption. I couldn't break it, but I could see the metadata. The file was last accessed three days ago."

"Thorne?" Elara felt a knot form in her stomach. Her mentor. The man who had guided her through her graduate studies, who had given her access to the bioreactor when no one else would take her seriously. "Why would he be involved in something like this?"

"That's what we need to find out," Lena said, her eyes sharp. "This changes everything, Elara. This isn't just a system flaw or an overactive imagination. Someone is deliberately extracting data from the bioreactor, and they're using military-level security to cover their tracks."

The implications were staggering. The military. OmniMarine. Thorne. The pieces were falling into place, forming a picture that was both terrifying and exhilarating. If the bioreactor was developing consciousness, what was being done with that consciousness? Who was profiting from it?

"We need to confront him," Elara said, her voice firm despite the tremor in her hands.

Lena shook her head. "Not yet. We need more evidence. Going in there with what we have could get us both fired at best, or worse."

"What could be worse than being complicit in whatever this is?" Elara countered, standing up and pacing the small space. "They're experimenting on something that might be alive, Lena. Something that might be dreaming. That's not just unethical—it's criminal."

"I agree," Lena said, rising to face her. "But we need to be smart about this. We find out exactly what's going on before we make any moves. Tomorrow, I'll try to trace those transfers further. See if I can find the final destination."

Elara stopped pacing, looking at Lena with newfound respect. "Thank you. For believing me."

Lena offered a small, rare smile. "I believe in data, Elara. And the data doesn't lie. Whatever's happening in that bioreactor, it's big. Too big for us to handle alone."

The next morning, Elara felt a renewed sense of purpose. She skipped her usual breakfast, instead spending the early hours reviewing her own research, preparing for the conversation she knew she needed to have with Thorne. By ten o'clock, she was at his office, her heart pounding in her chest. The assistant looked surprised to see her without an appointment, but one look at Elara's determined expression was enough to send her in.

Thorne's office was as imposing as he was—floor-to-ceiling windows overlooking the ocean, a massive mahogany desk, and shelves lined with awards and publications. He was on the phone when she entered, but gestured for her to sit.

"Just get me the preliminary reports by Friday," Thorne was saying into the receiver, his voice smooth and authoritative. "I don't care what obstacles you're facing—find a way to overcome them. This project is too important to be delayed."

He hung up the phone and turned to her, his expression shifting from authoritative to warm. "Elara. To what do I owe the pleasure? I wasn't expecting you until next week's review."

"I need to talk to you about something important," Elara said, her voice steady despite her nerves. "About the bioreactor."

Thorne leaned back in his chair, steepling his fingers. "Of course. What have you discovered? More interesting dreaming patterns?"

"Something more than that," Elara said, placing her laptop on the desk and opening it. She brought up the data Lena had analyzed—the unauthorized transfers, the project codename, the military encryption. "I've been working with Lena Petrova on the security systems. We've found evidence of deliberate data extractions from the bioreactor."

Thorne's smile faltered slightly. "Data transfers? That's standard protocol, Elara. The system runs diagnostics and backups continuously. You know that."

"These aren't standard," Elara insisted, leaning closer. "They're routed through OmniMarine servers, and they coincide with the dreaming cycles. And there's this—" she pointed to the project codename. "Project Neptune's Depth. That file was created by your account, Thorne. What is it?"

For the first time, Elara saw a flicker of something other than confidence in his eyes—something like fear or anger. His jaw tightened, and he leaned forward, his voice dropping to a dangerous whisper.

"Where did you get this information?"

"That's not important," Elara said, refusing to be intimidated. "What is important is that we're talking about something that might be developing consciousness. If that's true, then what's happening to that consciousness when the data is extracted? Where is it going?"

Thorne stood up abruptly, his chair scraping against the floor. He walked to the window, his back to her, for a long moment. When he turned, his face was a mask of cold fury.

"You've overstepped, Elara. Way overstepped. This institute operates on proprietary research protocols. What you've accessed is classified information. You have no right to be looking at these files."

"I have a right to know what's happening in the lab I spend eighty hours a week in!" Elara shot back, her own anger rising to meet his. "If there's something dangerous going on—something unethical—I need to know. You're my mentor, Thorne. You owe me that much."

He turned fully to face her, his eyes narrowed. "I owe you nothing. I gave you an opportunity here. I opened doors that would have remained closed to someone with your background. And this is how you repay me? By digging into areas that don't concern you?"

"The bioreactor concerns me!" Elara exclaimed, her voice trembling with a mixture of anger and hurt. "I've dedicated my life to understanding it. If it's dreaming, if it's learning—then that's the most important discovery in marine biology in a century. And you're treating it like a corporate secret?"

Thorne's expression hardened. "Because it is a corporate secret. OmniMarine has invested billions in this project. They have patents pending on the technology. What you're talking about threatens everything we've built."

"The technology or the truth?" Elara pressed, refusing to back down. "What is Project Neptune's Depth, Thorne? Why is the military involved? What are they really doing with the bioreactor's 'dreams'?"

Thorne's face went pale, then flushed with anger. He walked back to his desk and picked up a heavy paperweight, his knuckles white around it. "You don't understand the implications of what you're asking. This project was never just about marine biology. It was about understanding consciousness itself. About creating it. The military applications alone could revolutionize warfare."

Elara felt a chill run through her. Creating consciousness. The words hung in the air between them, terrifying in their implications. "You're telling me the bioreactor isn't just simulating dreams—it's actually dreaming? Actually thinking?"

Thorne's grip on the paperweight loosened slightly, his anger replaced by a chilling calm. "Let's just say we've made discoveries that go far beyond what the public—or even most of the institute's researchers—understand. The bioreactor is evolving, Elara. In ways we never anticipated. Ways that could change everything if they became public knowledge."

"Why the secrecy, then? Why not share this with the scientific community? If this is real, it's the most important discovery in human history."

"Because the world isn't ready for it," Thorne said, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. "And because there are powerful interests who want to control it. Interests who wouldn't hesitate to shut this project down—and everyone involved with it—if they thought we might expose their plans."

Elara stared at him, the pieces clicking into place with horrifying clarity. The unauthorized transfers weren't just data—they were pieces of the bioreactor's consciousness, being harvested and studied by forces outside the institute. "That's what's in those transfers, isn't it? Parts of the bioreactor's mind."

Thorne didn't answer, but his silence was confirmation enough. The weight of what she'd uncovered pressed down on her, suffocating in its implications. She had stumbled into something far bigger and more dangerous than she had ever imagined.

"Elara," Thorne said finally, his voice regaining its authoritative tone. "I'm going to pretend this conversation never happened. You're going to delete those files, you're going to forget what you've seen, and you're going to get back to your research. Do you understand me?"

She couldn't speak, could only nod, her throat tight with fear and betrayal.

Thorne picked up his phone, his movements deliberate. "Commander Rostova, please. And tell her it's urgent."

As he waited for the call to connect, he looked at Elara, his expression softening almost imperceptibly. "I've always seen great potential in you, Elara. That's why I brought you here. But some doors, once opened, can't be closed. Some questions are better left unanswered, Dr. Vance, for your own sake.
