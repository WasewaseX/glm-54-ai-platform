Chapter 17 — Dr. Carter's Warning

"everything alright?" Aris finished, his voice softer than she expected. The office was small but meticulously organized, walls lined with technical diagrams and marine biology texts. A half-empty mug of coffee sat beside his keyboard, steam still rising slightly. Elara noticed the way his fingers paused over the keyboard, not in alarm but in cautious anticipation.

"I need your help, Aris," Elara said, stepping into the room. "Something's not right with the bioreactor, and I think you might understand why better than anyone." She closed the door behind her, the soft click echoing in the confined space.

Aris's brow furrowed. "The Cradle? It's been running within parameters. Father was just saying so in this morning's briefing."

"But you know its parameters better than anyone," Elara countered, pulling up a chair and placing her datapad on the desk. She didn't wait for permission before tapping the screen, bringing up the neural correlation patterns Lena had identified. "These were flagged in the system yesterday. Unexplained energy fluctuations that coincide with what we're calling 'dreaming cycles.'"

Aris leaned closer, his eyes scanning the complex visualizations. His fingers twitched as if he wanted to reach out and manipulate the data himself. "These look familiar," he murmured, more to himself than to her. "I've seen similar patterns in the simulation logs, but they were supposed to be filtered out. Maintenance protocols only."

"Exactly," Elara said, relief washing over her. "Lena Petrova from cybersecurity confirmed these aren't system artifacts. They're mimicking human neural activity during REM sleep. The question is why a deep-sea simulation would need that."

Aris looked up then, meeting her eyes for the first time. There was a flicker of something in his gaze—fear, perhaps, or recognition. "Father never told me the full scope of the original funding," he said quietly. "Just that it came from a private corporation with military connections. I assumed it was standard defense research."

"OmniMarine," Elara supplied. "They've been quietly acquiring marine tech startups with government contracts for years."

Aris nodded slowly. "There was something else. When I was first assigned to the project, I found some old files in the archive directory. Fragmented code that didn't match any of the current documentation. I asked Father about it, but he told me it was legacy code from an earlier prototype and not to worry about it."

Elara's pulse quickened. "Could you access those files now?"

"I shouldn't," Aris said, his voice tight. "But I think you need to see this." He turned back to his terminal, his fingers flying across the keyboard with a practiced ease that spoke of long hours spent in front of screens. After a moment, a new window opened, displaying what looked like fragmented neural network diagrams overlaid with what appeared to be deep-sea sonar readings.

"What is this?" Elara asked, leaning closer.

"I don't know," Aris admitted. "But I've seen similar patterns in the Cradle's 'dreams' when I've monitored them overnight. It's like the machine is trying to connect two different kinds of data."

Elara's mind raced. "Lena mentioned something about neural mapping technology in the unauthorized transfers. Could this be related?"

Before Aris could answer, his terminal chimed with an alert. Both of them turned to see a notification flashing in the corner of the screen: UNAUTHORIZED DATA TRANSFER DETECTED FROM SECURE CONTAINER 7B.

"That's the one with the archived files," Aris said, his face paling. "Someone's accessing them right now."

"We need to get to Lena," Elara said, already standing up. "Now."

The walk to the cybersecurity wing felt longer than usual, each corridor stretching out before them like a maze. Elara could feel Aris's nervous energy beside her, his quick strides betraying his outward calm. When they reached the double doors marked CYBERSECURITY, Lena was already waiting, her face grim.

"You see it too?" Lena asked without preamble, leading them into her office where multiple screens displayed the data transfer in real time. "It's routing through a series of proxy servers, but the final destination is OmniMarine's corporate headquarters."

"Dr. Thorne has access to those files," Aris said quietly. "But I thought he was in his office."

"He's not," Elara said, a cold dread settling in her stomach. "We just left him."

Lena's fingers flew across her keyboard, tracing the data path. "Whoever's accessing this is bypassing all security protocols. This isn't just someone looking at files—this is someone downloading the entire neural mapping archive."

"Which means they know what it is," Elara finished. "And they don't want us to find out."

"We need to stop this transfer," Lena said, her voice tight with concentration. "But whoever's doing this is good—really good. They're using a quantum encryption protocol I've never seen before."

Aris stepped forward, his eyes fixed on one of the screens. "Wait—that's not Father's access signature. It's older. From when the project was first funded."

"Then who is it?" Elara asked, watching the progress bar slowly filling on the main monitor.

"I don't know," Lena admitted, "but whoever it is, they're accessing files that were supposed to be deleted years ago. This goes beyond simple corporate espionage."

As if on cue, the transfer stopped abruptly. The screens went blank for a moment before displaying a single line of text: PROJECT NEPTUNE'S DEPTH—CLASSIFIED.

Elara felt a chill run down her spine. "Neptune's Depth? That wasn't part of the official project name."

"No," Aris said, his voice barely a whisper. "But it was in those old files I found. The ones I wasn't supposed to look at."

Lena leaned back in her chair, her expression unreadable. "I'm running a trace, but whoever did this covered their tracks well. All I can tell is that the access originated from within the institute."

"Which means one of us," Elara said, looking from Aris to Lena and back again.

"No," Aris said, shaking his head. "Not one of us. Someone with higher clearance. Someone who's been here all along."

The weight of the realization settled over them like a physical presence. If someone within the institute was accessing classified files, it meant they couldn't trust anyone—not Marcus, not the administration, not even the security staff.

"We need to find out what Project Neptune's Depth really was," Elara said, her decision made. "And the only person who might know is Dr. Ben Carter. He worked on early bioreactor prototypes before the institute took over."

"Ben Carter?" Lena asked. "The retired marine biologist? I met him once at a conference. Brilliant but—"

"Haunted?" Elara finished. "Yes. He left the institute under mysterious circumstances about fifteen years ago. Something about ethical concerns with the direction of the research."

"I can try to access his contact information," Aris offered, already turning back to his terminal. "But his personal files are probably restricted."

"Don't bother," Elara said. "I know where he lives. The cliffs north of town. He keeps to himself since his wife passed away."

"You think he'll talk to you?" Lena asked, skepticism in her voice.

"I have to try," Elara said, already gathering her things. "This is bigger than any of us realized."

The drive to Dr. Carter's house took Elara along the coastal highway, the Pacific Ocean stretching out to her left under a sky that had turned the color of bruised plum. She'd called ahead, but Dr. Carter had been reluctant to speak with her, insisting he was retired and no longer involved with institute matters. It was only when she mentioned neural mapping technology and the name "Neptune's Depth" that he'd agreed to see her, his voice tight with something that sounded like fear.

The house stood perched on the edge of the cliffs, a modest structure with large windows that seemed to drink in the ocean view. As Elara parked her car and made her way up the winding path, she noticed the lights were on in nearly every room, a stark contrast to the darkening landscape.

Dr. Carter opened the door before she could knock, his face etched with lines that spoke of a lifetime spent beneath the waves. He was in his late sixties, with thinning white hair and eyes that missed nothing. He wore a faded blue sweater that had seen better days.

"Dr. Vance," he said, his voice raspy. "You weren't kidding about being persistent."

"I wouldn't be here if it wasn't important, Dr. Carter," Elara said, stepping inside. The living room was filled with books and nautical artifacts, walls covered in underwater photographs that captured the beauty and terror of the deep.

"I imagine not," he said, gesturing for her to sit. "Tea? Coffee?"

"Water would be fine, thank you."

While he was in the kitchen, Elara took in her surroundings. A large photograph on the mantel showed a younger Dr. Carter with a group of scientists in front of what looked like an early version of the bioreactor. Among them was a younger Marcus Thorne, his face full of ambition and pride.

When Dr. Carter returned with two glasses of water, he followed her gaze. "That was before things got complicated," he said quietly. "Before we realized what we were really playing with."

"Project Neptune's Depth," Elara said, not asking but stating. "What was it really?"

Dr. Carter's hand trembled slightly as he set his glass down on the coaster. "It was supposed to be a breakthrough in marine consciousness research. Mapping the neural pathways of deep-sea creatures, translating their perceptions into something we could understand."

"But that's not all it was, was it?" Elara pressed, pulling out her datapad and showing him the neural patterns Lena had identified.

Dr. Carter's face paled as he stared at the screen. "Where did you get this?"

"That's not important," Elara said. "What is—what is this?"

He took a deep breath, his eyes distant as if seeing something beyond the room. "Neptune's Depth wasn't just about mapping existing neural pathways. It was about creating them. About transferring consciousness from biological systems to synthetic ones."

Elara felt a cold dread settle in her stomach. "Transferring consciousness? Like uploading a mind to a machine?"

"Worse," Dr. Carter said, his voice dropping to a near whisper. "We weren't just copying neural patterns. We were trying to transfer the actual experience. The memories. The emotions."

"Why would you do that?" Elara asked, though she already suspected the answer.

"Funding," Dr. Carter said bitterly. "The military wanted soldiers who could operate in extreme environments without the limitations of human biology. They wanted minds that could withstand the pressure of the deep sea, the cold, the isolation."

"And what happened?" Elara prompted gently.

Dr. Carter closed his eyes, his face tightening with remembered pain. "The test subjects started exhibiting signs of trauma. Fragmented memories. Emotional responses that didn't match their personalities. It was as if they were experiencing things that weren't theirs—memories from other minds, other lives."

Elara thought of the bioreactor's "dreams"—complex data structures that shouldn't exist in a machine. "You shut the project down, didn't you?"

"We had to," Dr. Carter said, opening his eyes. "Too many people were suffering. The military denied everything, classified the research, and buried it. Most of the scientists involved were reassigned or 'retired.' I came here to Harbor's End to disappear, to live out my days studying the beauty of the ocean without trying to dissect its soul."

"But the project wasn't really buried, was it?" Elara said, showing him the evidence of the unauthorized data transfers. "OmniMarine picked it up. They're using it for the bioreactor."

Dr. Carter stared at the datapad, his face a mask of horror. "They're insane. You can't just transfer consciousness like copying a file. It's not just information—it's experience. It's pain. It's suffering."

"The bioreactor is exhibiting these 'dreaming cycles'," Elara said, her voice barely above a whisper. "Complex patterns that evolve over time. It's not just processing information—it's experiencing it."

Dr. Carter looked up at her, his eyes filled with a terrible understanding. "You're not just dealing with a machine, Dr. Vance—you're dealing with a prison."
