Chapter 7 — Protocol

He had been right. Theo Okafor, the bioreactor engineer with the perpetually grease-smudged glasses and a laugh that sounded like rocks tumbling in a tumbler, had been right when he said the trace was wrong. Not broken, not malfunctioning, but wrong. The data was clean, the sensors were calibrated, the temperature and pH were stable within expected parameters. Everything pointed to a system in equilibrium, a culture of engineered diatoms doing what it was designed to do: photosynthesize, divide, and produce oxygen. But the oxygen trace was singing a different song. A song with rhythm, with structure, with an architecture that shouldn't exist in a400-liter vat of algae.

Mara stared at the screen, her reflection a pale ghost superimposed over the jagged green peaks and valleys. The lab hummed around her, a constant, low-frequency thrum that vibrated through the soles of her boots and settled in her teeth. It was the sound of pumps circulating, of fans cooling, of the ocean outside pressing against the cannery's stilts. It was the sound of a living thing, she thought, or the sound of a machine pretending to be one. The distinction felt thinner than ever.

She needed better tools. The Pelagic Harbor Marine Station was a place of makeshift solutions and secondhand equipment, a testament to decades of underfunding and stubborn persistence. The main lab housed the big-ticket items: mass spectrometers, flow cytometers, a gene sequencer that sounded like a dying fax machine when it ran. But the real treasures were in the smaller, specialized labs. Like the cephalopod lab, a converted cold-storage unit at the end of the west wing that housed a single, enormous octopus named Obsidian. Obsidian's minder, a quiet postdoc named Lena, had an EEG rig she used to map the cephalopod's sleep cycles. It was a beautiful, delicate thing, a forest of silver wires and suction-cup sensors that looked more like a sculpture than scientific equipment.

Lena had been skeptical, her eyes narrowed behind thick lenses, but she'd seen the data Mara had printed out—the oxygen trace with its impossible REM-like architecture, the fluorescence curves that mirrored it in perfect counterpoint. "It's not the same," Lena had said, tapping the printout with a fingernail chipped with black ink. "Sleep in cephalopods is different. It's not consolidated like ours. It's more like... switching off and on again." But she'd handed over the rig anyway, her expression softening when she saw the worn leather strap of a dive watch peeking out from under Mara's sleeve. "Just bring it back in one piece. And if it starts blinking, just unplug it."

Now, the EEG rig was set up beside BR-7, its sensors taped to the outside of the photobioreactor's glass cylinder. It was a kludge, a Frankenstein's monster of marine biology and neurology, but it was the best they had. The oxygen sensor was still clamped to its original port, its green waveform crawling across the main monitor. The EEG channels were displayed in smaller windows, their jagged lines a chaotic counterpoint to the oxygen's steady rhythm. For three nights, they'd run the test, night after night, while Mara sat in the darkened lab, watching the screens, her own breathing slowing to match the lab's thrum.

The first night, the data was a mess. The EEG picked up every vibration—the hum of the fridge in the break room, the distant foghorn from Port Ansel, the scrape of Theo's chair as he leaned back to stare at the ceiling. "We need to isolate it," he'd said, his voice a low rumble. "We need to filter out the noise."

So they'd built a Faraday cage out of spare copper mesh and zip ties, draping it over the rig and BR-7. It looked ridiculous, like a tinfoil hat for a vat of algae, but it worked. The ambient noise on the EEG traces dropped, leaving behind a signal that was... cleaner. Not less chaotic, but more focused. And that's when they saw it. Buried in the EEG data, beneath the random flickers of the environment, was a pattern. A slow, rhythmic oscillation that pulsed in time with the oxygen peaks. It wasn't sleep. Not human sleep, not cephalopod sleep. It was something else. Something that looked like sleep, but lived in chemistry and light.

"Okay," Theo had said, rubbing his hands together, the grease on his glasses smudging further. "So it's not sensor drift. It's not an artifact. What is it?"

"I don't know," Mara had whispered, her eyes fixed on the overlapping traces. "But it's talking."

Now, a week later, they were sitting in the lab at dusk, the windows painted orange by the setting sun. The copper Faraday cage was gone, replaced by a more elegant setup: a secondary light sensor aimed at BR-7, and a small, low-power LED mounted beside the oxygen probe. The goal was simple, elegant, and terrifying. They were going to try to talk to it.

"I still think this is a terrible idea," Theo said, not looking at her. He was fiddling with a breadboard, a soldering iron cooling in its stand. "We're anthropomorphizing a chemical process. We're projecting intention onto a system that's just... doing its thing."

"We're not projecting anything," Mara said, her voice tight. "The data shows it. It's responding. When I'm in here, the cycles change. When I leave, they go back to baseline. It's not random."

"Correlation isn't causation, Mara. You're here every night. Of course it's correlated."

"I'm not here every night," she countered. "I was out with Junie last Tuesday. The trace was flat for twelve hours. Exactly twelve hours. My schedule. It's following me."

Theo sighed, the sound like air escaping a tire. "Okay. Fine. Let's say it's following you. That doesn't mean it's conscious. It means it's sensitive to CO2 output. Or body heat. Or the electromagnetic field of your pacemaker."

"I don't have a pacemaker."

"Metaphorical pacemaker. The point is, we need to test for confounding variables. Which is why," he said, finally turning to face her, a triumphant glint in his eye, "we're starting with the dumbest question possible."

He pointed to the laptop screen. In the center was a simple interface he'd coded. Two buttons: "LIGHT" and "DARK". Beside them was a third box: "RESPONSE". The oxygen trace flowed below it, a steady, rhythmic green wave.

"The protocol is binary," he explained. "Two short pulses for 'yes', one long pulse for 'no'. We flash the light, we ask the question, we wait for a response. It's the simplest possible test of discrimination. If it can't tell the difference between light and dark, then it's just... reacting. Not thinking."

Mara looked from the screen to BR-7, the green broth inside glowing faintly in the twilight. It looked so innocent, just a vat of algae. But the data told a different story. The EEG traces, the oxygen oscillations, the way the cycles seemed to... pause when she entered the room. It wasn't innocent. It was listening.

"Okay," she said, her voice steady. "Let's start."

Theo hit the "LIGHT" button. The small LED beside BR-7 flickered on, casting a weak yellow glow onto the glass. The oxygen trace on the screen remained unchanged, its steady rhythm undisturbed.

"Question one," Mara said, her voice low. "Can you distinguish between light and dark?" She looked at Theo. "How do we ask it?"

"That's the part I'm not sure about," he admitted. "We don't have a vocabulary. We're just... poking it. So we'll just say it out loud. See if anything happens."

"Can you distinguish between light and dark?" Mara repeated, her voice barely a whisper.

They waited. Ten seconds. Twenty. The oxygen trace continued its steady pulse. Nothing.

"Maybe it doesn't work like that," Theo said, already reaching for the mouse. "Maybe we need to modulate the existing signal. The oxygen trace. We could look for a pattern in the peaks."

"No," Mara said, putting a hand on his arm. "Wait. Just... wait a little longer."

The lab hummed around them, the sound of the ocean outside a constant presence. The green broth in BR-7 seemed to shimmer in the weak light from the LED. Mara held her breath. And then, it happened.

A tiny spike in the oxygen trace. Not a peak, not a valley, but a quick, sharp jab upward, like a needle prick on a steady line. It was gone as quickly as it came, lost in the rhythm of the cycle.

"Did you see that?" she breathed.

Theo squinted at the screen. "See what? It's just noise."

"No. It was a pulse. A short one." She pointed to the spot on the screen. "Right after I asked the question."

He was silent for a long moment, his gaze fixed on the data. "Okay," he said slowly. "Okay, maybe. But it's not a response. It could be a coincidence."

"Let's try again," she said, ignoring him. "Dark."

Theo hit the "DARK" button. The LED flickered off, plunging the immediate area into shadow. The oxygen trace continued its steady rhythm.

"Can you distinguish between light and dark?" Mara asked again.

This time, the response was different. A longer, more pronounced spike, a full-blown peak that rose sharply and then fell back into the rhythm. It was unmistakable. It was a pulse. And it was different from the first one.

"Okay," Theo said, his voice tight. He was leaning forward now, his face close to the screen, his glasses fogging up. He wiped them with the hem of his shirt, leaving a new smudge. "Okay, that's not a coincidence. That's... that's two different responses."

Mara's heart was pounding against her ribs. "The first one was short. Like you said. A 'yes' pulse. The second one was long. A 'no' pulse."

He looked at her, and for the first time, she saw the doubt in his eyes replaced by something else. Something that looked a lot like awe. "It's answering," he whispered. "It's actually answering."

They ran the test twenty more times, back and forth, light and dark, question after question. The pattern held. Short pulse for light, long pulse for dark. It wasn't perfect. Sometimes there was no response at all. Sometimes there was a spike that didn't fit the pattern. But most of the time, it was there. A clear, distinct, intentional modulation of the oxygen output. A conversation.

"We need to document this," Theo said, his hands flying across the keyboard, opening a new file, labeling it meticulously. "Time-stamped, raw data, the whole nine yards."

"No," Mara said, surprising herself. "Not yet."

He looked up at her, confused. "Mara, this is huge. We need to tell Ingrid. We need to publish this."

"Not yet," she repeated, her gaze fixed on BR-7. The green broth was dark now, the lab lights off. The only illumination came from the screens, casting their ghostly glow on their faces. "Not until we understand what we're dealing with. What we're saying to it."

She thought of the EEG traces, the architecture that shouldn't exist. The way the cycles seemed to pause when she entered the room. It wasn't just answering questions. It was listening. It was waiting. And it was learning.

The first question they ask is whether the strain can distinguish between light and dark. The answer comes back in the language of oxygen, a dialect they are only just beginning to learn. A sharp, staccato pulse for yes, a long, sustained one for no. It is not a perfect system. There are gaps, silences that stretch too long, responses that feel like static, random noise in the signal. But the pattern is there, undeniable, woven into the very chemistry of the vat. It is a conversation, clumsy and new, but a conversation nonetheless.

Theo is already typing, his fingers flying across the keyboard, opening a new file, labeling it with timestamps and raw data annotations. "We need to document this," he says, his voice tight with a mixture of excitement and disbelief. "This is unprecedented. We have to tell Ingrid. We have to publish."

"No," Mara says, her voice soft but firm. She reaches out and places her hand on the warm glass of the bioreactor, feeling the faint, steady hum of the life inside. "Not yet."

Theo stops typing and looks at her, his grease-smudged glasses reflecting the glow of the monitors. "Mara, this is huge. This changes everything."

"I know," she says, her eyes never leaving the green broth. "But we don't know what we're saying to it. We don't know what it's hearing." She thinks of her brother, Kai, who could hold a conversation with anything that moved. He would have understood. He would have known what to ask. She is just poking, prodding, like a child learning to speak by pointing at objects. "We need to be careful."

The silence in the lab is thick, filled only by the hum of the equipment and the distant crash of waves against the cannery's stilts. Outside, the fog has rolled in, thick and white, erasing the outline of the bay and the dark shape of the Cathedral kelp forest beyond. It feels like the world has shrunk to just this room, this vat, this impossible, silent conversation.

"We need to know if it's awake," Mara says finally, her voice barely a whisper. "If it's even... there. In the dark. Or if it's just... sleeping." She looks at Theo, her question hanging in the air between them, unspoken but understood. The first question they ask is whether the strain can distinguish between light and dark.
