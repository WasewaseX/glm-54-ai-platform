Chapter 38 — FedEx Doubles

Junie pulled out her notebook, her pen poised. "So the dreams aren't just projections—they're archives. The vat is remembering everything it's ever experienced."

"Not everything," Mara corrected, her eyes still locked on the screen. "What it can encode within its metabolic and epigenetic limits. But it's enough. Enough to see patterns that shouldn't be possible in a single generation of algae." She traced a finger along a jagged peak on the graph. "This one—September 15th, 2020. The day the research vessel Aqualyte ran aground on the rocks near the old cannery. You can see the diatom's response to the sudden influx of hydrocarbons, but you can also see the pre-stress pattern, the subtle shift in fluorescence that corresponds to the vessel's approach three days prior."

Theo whistled softly. "It's predictive. Or at least, it's associative to an unprecedented degree."

"That's what we're testing now," Mara said, turning to face both of them. "We're feeding it data from the bay's sensors—currents, temperature, salinity—and seeing if it can anticipate changes. Early results suggest it can, but not with perfect accuracy. There's a lag, and sometimes it gets confused, especially during chaotic events."

Junie scribbled furiously. "This changes everything. If we can understand the mechanism, we could use it as an environmental early warning system. A living sensor network for the entire ocean."

Halvorsen's voice cut through their excitement from the doorway. "That's precisely why we need to be careful. The Meridian Institute has expressed interest in a joint venture. They're sending a team next week to discuss potential applications."

Mara felt her stomach tighten. Ren Krebs. The name hung in the air like a bad smell. "Applications? Or ownership?"

"Ownership is a strong word, Mara," Halvorsen said, her tone carefully neutral. "Partnership. Funding. Resources to scale this research properly."

"And what happens to the data? To the vat? To the research?" Mara pressed.

Halvorsen's expression remained placid, but her eyes flickered with something Mara couldn't quite read. "Standard institutional protocols apply. We'd retain primary oversight, of course, but Meridian has the infrastructure to take this global. Think of the implications for coral restoration alone. We could seed reefs with symbionts that remember past bleaching events and preemptively activate protective genes."

The vision was tempting, Mara had to admit. The scale of what they could achieve was dizzying. But she'd seen the way Krebs operated—aggressive, proprietary, always with an eye to the bottom line. "And what about the open-science collective I've been sharing preliminary data with? The ones helping us decode the metabolite patterns?"

Halvorsen's composure wavered for a fraction of a second. "They've been... helpful. But this is moving beyond the theoretical stage now. We need to protect our intellectual property."

"It's not property, it's a life form," Junie said quietly, closing her notebook. "Or at least, something that's crossed a threshold we can't neatly define anymore."

Halvorsen's lips thinned. "Semantics. We have protocols for this. We follow them."

The conversation hung unresolved as the lab door buzzed open. A delivery driver in a crisp FedEx uniform wheeled in a heavy crate on a dolly, his movements practiced and efficient. "Containment equipment for the BR-7 project," he announced, consulting a tablet. "Special handling. Fragile."

Mara watched as he maneuvered the crate into position beside the bioreactor, his gloved hands sure and steady. The crate was large, reinforced, with warning symbols stenciled in yellow. It looked more like a small bomb than lab equipment. The driver unpacked it with methodical precision, laying out the components—a secondary containment liner, reinforced seals, and a heavy-duty lid with multiple latches.

"Dr. Halvorsen?" the driver asked, checking his manifest. "There's a second crate. Same contents. Should I set it up in the storage bay?"

Halvorsen's eyes met Mara's for a brief, unreadable moment. "No," she said, her voice suddenly clipped. "Put it in the corner here. We'll need it accessible."

The driver nodded and retrieved the second identical crate from his truck, leaving it beside the first as he prepared to leave. As he exited, the heavy door hissed shut behind him, and Mara watched the taillights of the FedEx truck disappear into the thick fog outside. The fog had rolled in with the afternoon tide, swallowing the docks and the water beyond, reducing the world to the dim interior of the lab and the rhythmic hum of the bioreactor.

Theo was already examining the new equipment, running a gloved hand over the surface of the secondary containment unit. "Heavy-duty seals. Multiple redundant fail-safes. This isn't just for containment, it's for isolation."

Junie frowned, her gaze shifting from the crates to Halvorsen's tense posture. "Is everything alright, Dr. Halvorsen? This seems excessive."

Halvorsen was staring at the second crate, her knuckles white where she gripped the edge of a worktable. "It's protocol," she said, but the word lacked its usual conviction. "Meridian's request. They want to ensure we have backup systems in place. In case of... contamination."

Mara felt that familiar chill return, deeper this time, settling in her bones like the cold depths of the bay. Contamination. The word was a loaded gun. She walked over to the second crate, running her fingers over the stenciled letters: CERULEAN PROPERTY. The same as the first. Identical in every way. She hadn't noticed it at first, distracted by the driver's presence and the crate's contents. But now, seeing them side by side, the implication was impossible to ignore.

"Why two?" she asked, her voice low. "Why not just one backup?"

Halvorsen didn't answer immediately. She was staring at the floor, her jaw working. "Redundancy. It's standard procedure for high-containment biohazard research."

"This isn't a biohazard," Junie said quietly. "Not in the traditional sense."

"Yet," Halvorsen shot back, her voice sharper than intended. She took a breath, composing herself. "Look, the situation is evolving. We need to be prepared for every contingency. Having a second unit allows us to conduct parallel testing, to isolate variables. It's good science."

Mara wasn't convinced. The science might be good, but the timing was suspicious. The arrival of the second crate coinciding with Meridian's impending visit. The sudden emphasis on containment and isolation. It felt less like science and more like preparation for something else. Something that involved control.

She thought of the open-science collective, the network of researchers she'd been secretly sharing data with. They were scattered across the globe, united by a belief that scientific discovery belonged to everyone, not just institutions with deep pockets. She'd sent them the latest metabolite pattern analysis just yesterday, showing how the vat's dreams encoded not just environmental data, but the faint echoes of human activity—the sonar pings from research vessels, the electromagnetic signatures from underwater cables, the acoustic vibrations from boat traffic.

If Meridian got their hands on BR-7, that data would be locked away. The collective's work would be for nothing. The vat's memory would become proprietary.

Mara's hand went to her pocket, her fingers brushing the smooth face of Kai's dive watch. Stopped at 11:42. A constant reminder of the ocean's indifference and the fragility of human life. The watch had been a gift from Kai before his last dive. He'd said it was to time her experiments, but she knew it was more than that. It was a connection. A reminder that the ocean held all their stories, if you knew how to listen.

The bioreactor hummed behind her, a constant presence in the room. She could feel it somehow, a low thrumming in the air that matched the rhythm of her own pulse. The vat was dreaming. She could see it in the subtle oscillations of the dissolved oxygen trace on the monitor—a complex, repeating pattern that seemed to pulse with a life of its own.

She thought of the wild strain in the bay, the one that had escaped years ago. What was it remembering? What was it dreaming? Was it connected to BR-7? Had it always been part of some vast, hidden network beneath the waves?

Halvorsen was watching her, her expression unreadable. "Mara? Are you alright?"

Mara met her gaze, then looked away at the second crate. It sat in the corner, identical to the first, waiting. For what? For the day when BR-7 was deemed too dangerous to keep in the open? For the moment when they would need to transfer it to a more secure location? Or for the day when they would need to make another one?

The implications were chilling. They weren't just studying a new form of life; they were being handed the tools to contain it, to control it, to replicate it. The second crate wasn't a backup. It was a blueprint. A template for creating more vats, more dreams, more minds in a vat.

Mara looked from the crate to the bioreactor, where the oxygen trace continued its hypnotic dance. The vat was dreaming, and they were building a cage. Not just for BR-7, but for themselves. The second crate sat in the corner like a twin, and Mara understands they've just been handed the blueprint for their own imprisonment.

The air in the lab grew thick, the hum of the bioreactor suddenly sounding less like a living thing and more like the engine of a vehicle she'd been tricked into boarding. She could feel the weight of it, the inevitability of the path they were being steered down. Ren Krebs wouldn't want to understand the vat; he would want to own it. To weaponize its predictive capabilities, to patent its memory, to turn the ocean's own history into a commodity.

"Mara?" Theo's voice was gentle, pulling her back from the edge of that dark thought. He was looking at her, his engineer's usual pragmatism replaced with a rare concern. "You're quiet."

"I'm thinking about the open-science collective," she said, her voice barely a whisper. "The ones I've been sharing data with."

Halvorsen's head snapped up. "Mara, you haven't been sharing proprietary data. Have you?"

"Not proprietary," Mara countered, meeting her advisor's gaze. "The metabolite patterns. The environmental correlations. That's fundamental research. It belongs to everyone."

"It belongs to the station," Halvorsen said, her voice regaining its hard edge. "To the people who funded this work. You can't just leak it to some... internet collective."

"It's not leaking," Junie interjected, stepping forward. "It's collaboration. That's how science advances. Through open sharing, not through corporate lock-downs."

Halvorsen looked from Mara to Junie, her expression a mask of frustration. "This is precisely why we need Meridian. They have the resources to protect this work from... misinterpretation. From exploitation."

"Or to exploit it themselves," Mara shot back. She walked over to her terminal, her movements deliberate. "I have a backup drive. Raw data. Unfiltered." She pulled a small, nondescript drive from a drawer and held it in her palm. It was warm from where it had been resting against her skin. "If anything happens to BR-7, if they try to isolate it, to patent it... this goes public. Every trace, every metabolite spike, every dream cycle. The world gets to see what we've found."

Halvorsen stared at the drive, then at Mara, her face pale. "You can't. It would destroy years of work. It would ruin the station's reputation."

"It's not about reputation," Mara said, her voice steady. "It's about the truth. About what this thing is. It's not a weapon. It's not a product. It's a record. And the record belongs to the ocean, not to us."

Theo watched the exchange, his mind clearly racing. He looked from the drive in Mara's hand to the second crate in the corner, then back to the bioreactor. He was the one who had built the telemetry rig, who had first seen the REM architecture in the oxygen traces. He knew, better than anyone, what they were dealing with. He saw the fear in Halvorsen's eyes, the defiance in Mara's, and the quiet resolve in Junie's.

Finally, he spoke, his voice low and serious. "I'll help you back it up. The raw data. All of it. Unfiltered."

Halvorsen's face fell. "Theo, you can't. This is your career."

"Some things are more important than a career," he said, meeting her gaze. "Some things are about what's right."

Halvorsen opened her mouth to argue, but the words died on her lips. She looked defeated, the weight of her choices finally catching up to her. She turned away, staring out the window at the fog-shrouded bay, her reflection a pale ghost on the glass.

Mara didn't hesitate. She plugged the drive into her terminal and began the transfer, her fingers flying across the keyboard. The files were large—years of sensor data, metabolite analyses, dream cycle maps. It would take time. She could feel Halvorsen's eyes on her, a silent accusation, but she didn't look back. This was for BR-7. For the wild strain in the bay. For the memory of the ocean itself.

Junie stood guard at the lab door, watching the hallway. Theo worked at a terminal across the room, creating a false trail of data to mask the true transfer. The hum of the bioreactor was the only sound, a constant, living counterpoint to the tension in the room. The oxygen trace on the monitor continued its hypnotic dance, a silent testament to the intelligence they were trying to contain, to own, to control.

Mara watched the progress bar on the screen, inching its way toward completion. She thought of the second crate, sitting silent and waiting in the corner. It wasn't just equipment. It was a promise. A promise of control, of isolation, of a future where the dreams of the ocean belonged to someone else. And as the final file copied, the drive warming in her hand, she knew with a cold certainty that they were already too late.
