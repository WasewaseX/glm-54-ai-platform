Mara accepted the coffee, the warmth seeping into her cold fingers. "We think we've mapped the dream cycles to environmental triggers. It's not just random oscillations; it's a record of the bay."

Junie leaned closer, her professional skepticism momentarily replaced by genuine curiosity. "A record? Like a diary?"

Junie traced a finger along the metabolite wave, her brow furrowed. "So it's not just dreaming about the bay. It's dreaming the bay."

"Precisely," Mara said, a surge of pride warming her chest. "The strain is holding a chemical memory of its environment. Every tide, every boat, every season in the kelp forest—it's all in there."

The implications settled over the room like fog. They weren't just studying an organism; they were studying a living archive, a chemical echo of the world outside the glass walls. The weight of it was staggering.

Junie straightened, her expression shifting back to her usual analytical mode. "This changes things. Ethically, I mean. If it's a memory, then what are our responsibilities? Are we witnesses? Curators? Or are we still just scientists studying a phenomenon?"

The question hung in the air, unanswered. The door hissed open again, and Dr. Halvorsen entered, her presence filling the small room. She took in the scene—the three of them clustered around the monitors, the graphs still glowing on the screens.

"Progress, I hope?" she asked, her voice crisp, but her eyes lingered on Junie for a fraction of a second too long. The bioethicist had a way of making people feel scrutinized, and Halvorsen was no exception.

"We believe we've established a direct correlation between the dream cycles and environmental data," Mara said, choosing her words carefully. "BR-7 is encoding the bay's history in its metabolites."

Halvorsen's gaze shifted to the screen, her professional mask firmly in place. "Remarkable. But correlation isn't causation. Have you accounted for all variables? Water temperature, nutrient influx, atmospheric pressure?"

"We've controlled for those," Theo said, a hint of defensiveness in his tone. "The patterns are too specific, too consistent to be coincidence. The alignment with the tide tables alone is statistically significant."

Halvorsen nodded slowly, her fingers tapping a silent rhythm on the edge of the console. "Publishable, then? A paper in Marine Biotechnology?"

Mara hesitated. The idea of publishing felt premature, almost disrespectful. They had only begun to scratch the surface of what BR-7 was doing. "We need more data. To understand the extent of the memory, the depth of it."

"Understandable," Halvorsen said, but her smile didn't reach her eyes. "The pressure to produce is always there, Mara. Remember that." She turned to leave, then paused at the doorway. "And Junie? My office. When you have a moment."

Junie gave a curt nod, her expression unreadable as she watched Halvorsen leave. The bioethicist took a slow sip of her coffee, her eyes fixed on the door. "She seems... eager for publication."

"She's always eager for publication," Theo muttered. "It's how the game is played. Grants, papers, citations."

"Not this," Junie said, her voice low. "This is different. This feels bigger than the game." She set her coffee cup down and turned to face them. "I've been going through the archived maintenance logs for BR-7. Standard procedure for an ethics review, you understand."

Mara felt a knot form in her stomach. "And?"

"And there are discrepancies," Junie said, her gaze shifting between Mara and Theo. "Several entries regarding the autoclave system from five years ago. The maintenance reports indicate a full decontamination cycle was completed on schedule. But the raw data logs show something different."

"What difference?" Theo asked, leaning forward.

"The temperature readings," Junie said. "They dipped below the required sterilization threshold for a period of forty-two minutes. Long enough for certain heat-resistant spores to survive."

The air in the room grew thick. Mara felt a cold dread seep into her bones. "The autoclave failure. The one they said never happened."

"It seems there was a failure," Junie confirmed. "And it was logged, then subsequently amended in the official report to show a successful cycle."

Halvorsen. It had to be her. She had been the project lead then, the one responsible for oversight. The knowledge settled over Mara like a shroud of cold seawater. Her advisor, the woman who had guided her academic career, had covered up a breach of protocol that could have catastrophic consequences.

"Why would she do that?" Theo whispered, his face pale.

"Pressure," Junie said grimly. "Funding. The project was on the brink of being shut down. A major failure like that would have been the final nail in the coffin."

Mara stared at the door Halvorsen had exited through, her mind racing. The woman who had always preached rigorous science, the guardian of protocol, had herself violated it. The hypocrisy was staggering, a bitter pill to swallow.

"We have to confront her," Mara said, her voice tight with a mixture of anger and betrayal. "We can't let this stand."

Junie shook her head. "Not yet. We need more. A pattern. Something undeniable. Accusations like this can destroy a career, and if we're wrong, the damage is irreparable."

"We're not wrong," Theo insisted. "The data is there."

"Data can be misinterpreted," Junie countered. "We need to understand her motivation, the context. There might be more to the story."

The conversation was cut short by the arrival of a man in a rumpled suit, clutching a leather briefcase. He introduced himself as Ben Carter, a science journalist from the *Ansel Bay Chronicle*.

"Dr. Halvorsen cleared me for an embed," he said, his eyes scanning the room with the practiced gaze of someone looking for a story. "Thought I'd start with the team making the breakthrough."

Mara's stomach clenched. A journalist. Now. With Junie's revelation hanging in the air. It felt like a trap.

"Breakthrough is a strong word," Theo said, stepping into the role of cautious spokesperson. "We're still in the early stages."

"Early stages of what, exactly?" Carter pressed, his pen poised over a notepad. "The whispers around the station are... interesting. Something about a dreaming vat of algae."

Mara exchanged a look with Junie, who gave a subtle, almost imperceptible shake of her head. The message was clear: say nothing. Not yet.

"We're studying diatom behavior," Mara said, her voice carefully neutral. "Standard research."

Carter smiled, a thin, knowing expression. "Right. Standard. That's why Dr. Halvorsen called it the most significant discovery in marine biology since the discovery of thermophilic archaea. Standard stuff."

Mara felt a flush of heat creep up her neck. The man was a predator, and they were cornered prey. "She's enthusiastic," she conceded. "As all good scientists should be."

"Enthusiastic, or protective?" Carter's question was casual, but his eyes were sharp. "There are whispers about data access. About proprietary rights."

The mention of proprietary rights sent a jolt of alarm through Mara. If Ren Krebs got wind of this, if he moved to secure the patents, BR-7's dreams would become corporate property. The thought was unbearable.

"We're a publicly funded institution," Theo said, his tone firm. "Our data is, by definition, public."

"In theory," Carter said with a shrug. "In practice, well, I've seen how these things work." He glanced at his watch. "Well, I'll let you get back to your standard research. I'll be around." He gave them a final, appraising look and left, the door hissing shut behind him.

The silence that followed was heavy with unspoken fears. The journalist was a loose cannon, and Halvorsen's secret was a powder keg waiting for a spark.

"We have to move faster," Mara said, her voice low and urgent. "Before Carter finds out about Junie's findings. Before Krebs gets his hands on this."

Junie nodded. "Agreed. But we need a plan. Going to Halvorsen now is too risky. We need leverage."

"What kind of leverage?" Theo asked.

"Proof," Junie said. "More than just the amended logs. We need to find the original, unedited data. The raw files that were overwritten."

Mara thought of the server room, the labyrinth of hard drives and backup tapes that stored decades of station data. It was a needle in a haystack, but it was their only hope.

"I know where the old tapes are stored," she said. "In the sub-basement. The archive room."

"Then that's where we go," Junie said, her expression resolute. "Tonight."

The decision made, the three of them fell into a tense, focused silence. The weight of their secret was a physical presence in the small room, a shared burden that bonded them in a way nothing else could. They were no longer just a student, an engineer, and an ethicist. They were conspirators, guardians of a truth that could shatter the foundations of the station.

Later that evening, long after the station had fallen quiet, Mara slipped out of her quarters. The corridors were dimly lit, the hum of the station's systems a constant, low thrum in the background. She moved with practiced quiet, her footsteps muffled on the worn linoleum. The sub-basement was a place few visited, a forgotten archive of the station's history.

The door to the archive room was heavy and cold, a solid barrier against the world. Mara keyed in the access code she'd stolen from Halvorsen's desk during a faculty meeting weeks ago, her heart pounding against her ribs. The lock clicked, and she pushed the door open, the smell of dust and old paper washing over her.

The room was vast, filled floor to ceiling with metal shelves stacked with boxes of files, tapes, and journals. The air was thick and still, the only light the beam of her flashlight cutting through the gloom. It was a graveyard of data, and they were grave robbers.

Junie and Theo were already there, their flash beams probing the darkness. "Over here," Theo whispered, leading them to a section labeled "BR-7 Project Archives, Pre-2018."

They spent hours poring over boxes, their fingers growing dusty and their backs aching. The search was methodical but grueling, a slow excavation of the past. Then, Junie let out a soft curse.

"What is it?" Mara asked, moving to her side.

Junie was holding a dusty tape case, the label faded but still legible: "BR-7 Autoclave Cycle Log, 08/15/2018. Raw Data."

Her hand trembled slightly as she slid the tape from its case. "This is it," she whispered. "The original."

Theo took the tape and examined the drive they'd brought, a relic from an earlier era of data storage. "It should still be readable. The format is old, but standard."

With a soft whir, the drive accepted the tape. A progress bar appeared on the small screen, crawling slowly forward. Each tick of the counter was a hammer blow against the carefully constructed lie Halvorsens had built.

Finally, the data loaded. A cascade of characters and symbols filled the screen, a raw, unedited stream of information from five years ago. Junie began scrolling through the log, her eyes scanning the lines of code.

"There," she said, pointing to a section of the text. "Here's the anomaly. The temperature spike, then the drop. Look at the timestamp. Forty-two minutes below threshold."

Mara leaned closer, her breath catching in her throat. It was there, in black and white. The undeniable proof of the failure. The lie was exposed, raw and ugly.

They copied the files to a portable drive, the silence in the archive room broken only by the soft whir of the equipment and the sound of their own breathing. The evidence was in their hands, cold and hard and irrefutable.

As they prepared to leave, Mara's flashlight beam caught something on a nearby shelf. It was a framed photograph, partially obscured by a stack of boxes. She moved the boxes aside and picked it up. It was a photograph of Dr. Halvorsen, her hair streaked with gray, standing beside a much younger version of herself, smiling as she held up a petri dish. The dish was filled with a milky substance, the SY-7 strain in its earliest stages. The caption on the back read: "First successful culture inoculation. The future of coral restoration."

Mara stared at the photo, a wave of conflicting emotions washing over her. The pride in Halvorsen's eyes, the hope in her smile. It was a different woman from the one who had covered up a critical failure, the one who was now desperately trying to control the narrative around BR-7.

The image was a snapshot of a moment frozen in time, a promise of a future that had taken a different path. The woman in the photo had been a true believer, a scientist driven by the desire to heal the oceans. The woman Mara knew was a product of a different reality, one shaped by pressure, ambition, and the weight of a terrible secret.

As Mara looked from the photo to the portable drive in her hand, the connection between the two became painfully clear. The lie and the dream were not separate things. They were two halves of the same story, the story of a woman who had sacrificed her principles for a greater good, only to find that the cost was her own integrity.

Halvorsen's coffee cup trembles as she stares at a photo of herself with the young SY-7 strain, and the distance between then and now becomes impossible to measure.
