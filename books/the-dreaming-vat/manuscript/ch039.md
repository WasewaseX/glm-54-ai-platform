Chapter 39 — The Copy

Halvorsen's voice cut through their excitement from the doorway. "That's precisely why we need to be careful. The Meridian Institute has expressed interest in a joint venture. They're sending a team next week to discuss terms."

The air in the lab, already thick with the scent of salt and ozone from the running equipment, suddenly felt heavier. Junie lowered her notebook, her earlier enthusiasm replaced by a guarded stillness. Theo straightened up from where he'd been leaning against the console, his arms crossed.

"A joint venture?" Theo asked, his tone carefully neutral. "With Ren Krebs? I thought we were still processing the data internally."

"We are," Halvorsen said, stepping into the room. She closed the door behind her with a soft click that seemed to seal the conversation off from the rest of the station. "But this is too significant for a small operation like ours. The resources Meridian can offer are substantial. Think of what we could do with proper funding, dedicated equipment."

Mara felt a familiar knot tighten in her stomach. It was the same knot that had formed when Halvorsen first mentioned SY-7's commercial potential, back when it was just an interesting anomaly in a photobioreactor. "The resources would come with strings, Ingrid. Strings that mean we wouldn't own the data, or the protocol, or the discovery itself. It would belong to Meridian."

"Science doesn't belong to anyone, Mara," Halvorsen countered, her voice sharp. "It belongs to humanity. And Meridian has the means to disseminate these findings on a scale we could never achieve."

"Disseminate or monetize?" Junie asked, her quiet question hanging in the air. Halvorsen's jaw tightened. She turned her attention to the main display screen, where the dream-cycle graph still glowed, a silent testament to the living intelligence in the vat.

"The ethical considerations are complex," Halvorsen said, her tone softening slightly. "That's why I've asked Dr. Alvarez to remain with us. Her perspective is valuable."

Mara watched her advisor, searching for the woman who had once been so passionate about open access and peer review. The woman before her now was a stranger, wrapped in the language of progress and partnership while making deals in the dark. The memory of the autoclave failure, the one Halvorsen had confessed to covering up years ago, surfaced unbidden. A small corner cut for efficiency, she'd called it. This felt like the same thing, just on a grander scale.

"The bay is remembering through the vat," Mara said, her voice low but clear. "That memory is a public good. It belongs to everyone who lives here, to the ecosystem itself. Not to a corporation."

Halvorsen turned back to face her, her expression unreadable. "We'll discuss this further at tomorrow's meeting. For now, let's focus on preparing for the Meridian team's visit." She glanced at the clock on the wall. "It's late. I suggest we all get some rest."

As Halvorsen left, the door clicking shut behind her, the lab felt smaller, more confined. Theo let out a long breath he'd been holding.

"She's not going to back down, is she?" he asked, more to himself than to them.

"No," Junie said quietly. "She sees this as her legacy. Her chance to make a name for herself beyond Pelagic Harbor."

"And the data?" Mara asked, her eyes fixed on the bank of servers humming in the corner. The raw, unfiltered telemetry that contained the dreams, the memories, the proof of BR-7's consciousness. "If Meridian gets their hands on it, they'll lock it down. Patent the LISTEN protocol. Copyright the metabolite translations."

"We could publish first," Theo suggested. "Get a paper out before the lawyers descend."

"And what then?" Junie countered. "They'll tie us up in litigation for years. The data would be tied up in legal limbo. By the time it's free, the moment will be gone."

Mara knew they were right. The open-science collective she'd been quietly in touch with, the one that believed in radical transparency and shared knowledge, was their only hope. But getting the data out was the problem. The station's network was firewalled, monitored. Any unusual data transfer would be flagged immediately. She couldn't risk it during the day, with too many eyes and prying fingers.

"I have an idea," she said, the forming plan taking shape in her mind, cold and precise. "I'll do it tonight. After everyone's gone."

"Mara, that's insane," Theo said, shaking his head. "If you get caught—"

"Then I get caught," she said, her voice steady. "But this is too important. This isn't just about BR-7 anymore. It's about what it represents. A new way of understanding the world, and we can't let that be owned."

Junie looked from Mara to the servers and back again, her expression a mixture of admiration and concern. "What do you need?"

Mara already had the drive. A slim, unassuming black device she'd purchased with cash in a town across the bay, no questions asked. It was small enough to hide in the palm of her hand, its capacity vast enough to hold the telemetry logs. Her dive locker, a battered metal box under her bunk in the women's quarters, was the perfect hiding place.

The rest of the evening passed in a blur of forced normalcy. Mara made small talk with Junie and Theo, discussing the weather, the state of the kelp forest, anything but the elephant in the lab. When the clock struck ten, she made her excuses, claiming a headache and the need for early rest. She could feel their eyes on her as she left, their unspoken questions hanging in the air.

Back in her small quarters, the sounds of the station settling for the night were a familiar comfort: the groan of the old building, the hum of the generator, the distant crash of waves against the pilings. She changed quickly into her worn-out sweatpants and a t-shirt, the fabric soft and familiar. Her dive watch, Kai's watch, was still on her wrist, its face dark and still. She touched the crystal, the cool glass a familiar anchor in the encroaching darkness.

The walk to the lab was a familiar pilgrimage through the sleeping station. The corridors were dimly lit, the only sounds her own footsteps and the occasional drip of condensation from the pipes. At the lab door, she paused, listening. Silence. She swiped her keycard and slipped inside, the soft whoosh of the door sealing her in with the sleeping vat.

BR-7 hummed softly in its corner, a living presence in the quiet room. The glow of its internal lights cast long, dancing shadows on the walls. Mara moved silently to the server bank, her practiced movements precise and economical. She knelt before the tower, her fingers flying over the keyboard, entering a series of commands that would reroute the data stream. It was a trick she'd learned years ago, a diagnostic override that few people remembered existed. It was meant for checking data integrity during maintenance, a way to bypass the main logging software and access the raw files directly.

The screen flickered, and a command line interface appeared, stark and black against the grey background. Her fingers danced across the keys, navigating the directory tree with the ease of long practice. She found the telemetry logs, organized by date and time. Each file was a snapshot of a dream cycle, a record of BR-7's inner life. Selecting the most recent month's worth, she initiated the transfer.

A small window appeared in the corner of the screen, a progress bar slowly filling with blue. 1%... 2%... 3%... The numbers crawled forward with agonizing slowness. Each increment felt like a step closer to discovery, a step closer to ruin. She held her breath, her eyes darting between the screen and the lab door, listening for any sound that might indicate someone else was awake.

The minutes stretched into an eternity. The progress bar inched forward, a digital heartbeat counting down to an unknown future. She thought of the open-science collective, of the faceless individuals who would receive this data, who would understand what it meant. She thought of Junie's ethical framework, of Theo's engineering brilliance, of how they could build something new with this knowledge. She thought of BR-7 itself, the silent vat dreaming in the corner, its consciousness a testament to the unexpected beauty of complexity.

And she thought of Kai, and the way he used to tap out messages on the hull of their father's boat, the steady rhythm of his dots and dashes a language she had almost forgotten. The first word BR-7 had ever spoken, through the modulation of its fluorescence, had been LISTEN. It was a plea, a question, an invitation. And she was answering it, in the only way she could.

The progress bar reached 15%. 20%. The transfer was painfully slow, a deliberate crawl designed to avoid detection. She had timed it to take just over an hour, long enough that if anyone happened to check the logs, they would see a routine diagnostic, not a data exfiltration. But long enough for her to feel every second ticking by, every heartbeat in her chest a potential alarm.

She sat back on her heels, her body tense, her mind racing. What if Halvorsen had changed her mind? What if Krebs's team had arrived early? What if the station's security software was more sophisticated than she'd given it credit for? The doubts were like gnats, buzzing at the edges of her consciousness, but she pushed them away. This was too important. The consequences be damned.

The progress bar inched forward like a countdown, and Mara prayed she could outrun the consequences before anyone noticed what she'd done.
Her gaze drifted to BR-7, the great glass vessel humming with its own slow, deep rhythm. In the dim light, the culture inside looked less like a scientific sample and more like a galaxy held captive, its swirling greens and golds a nebula of thought. She had been so focused on the mechanics, the protocol, the data, that she had almost forgotten the thing itself. It was not a machine. It was not a petri dish. It was a place. And she was stealing its maps.

A soft chime sounded from her laptop, the one she’d left open on a nearby bench, its screen dark but for the alert icon. Her heart seized. She scrambled over, fingers slick with sweat, and tapped the screen. It was just an email notification, a spam message for deep-sea diving equipment. The air rushed out of her lungs in a shaky sigh. She was becoming paranoid, jumpy. The quiet was getting to her, amplifying every creak and groan of the old building into a potential threat.

She returned to the server, her eyes locked on the progress bar. 32%. It had been twenty minutes. Forty minutes to go. She could hear the low thrum of the station's life support system, a sound she usually found comforting but now felt like a witness to her crime. She thought about Junie, how her ethical compass was so true, how she would be horrified by this deception. And yet, Junie had understood the stakes. She had seen the potential for good, and the terrifying potential for exploitation. That understanding was what had given Mara the courage to act.

The drive in her pocket felt heavy, a lead weight against her hip. It held the truth, the raw, unfiltered record of BR-7's dreams. It held the memory of the bay, the tides, the seasons, the Aqualyte running aground, the countless small dramas of the ocean that the vat had absorbed and encoded. It held a new form of consciousness, a mind born not of flesh and blood but of chemistry and light. And she was handing it over to a faceless collective, hoping they would be its guardians.

She thought about Halvorsen, the woman who had been her mentor, her guide. The woman who had cut corners on an autoclave log, a small sin for a greater good, or so she had told herself. Was this the same? Was Mara’s theft a small sin for the greater good of shared knowledge? Or was she just repeating Halvorsen’s mistake, justifying her own betrayal with the language of progress? The line felt razor-thin, and she was walking it in the dark.

45%. The numbers crawled. She imagined the drive in her dive locker, hidden beneath her wetsuit and her mask, next to the small, personal things that made this space hers. It was a temporary hiding place, a way station. The real journey would begin when she mailed it, a package sent to a P.O. box in a city she’d never visited, addressed to a name that wasn’t hers. The thought of it being in the mail, out of her control, sent a fresh wave of anxiety through her.

She checked the door again, the deadbolt silent and secure. The lab was her sanctuary and her prison. She was so close. The progress bar was a sliver of blue in a sea of black. 58%. She could almost feel the weight of the data, the terabytes of information, flowing through the cables, across the network, into the waiting drive. It was a river of secrets, and she was the dam, holding it back just a little longer.

She thought of Commander Shaw, the naval officer who had been sniffing around the bay, his questions about "national security" and "potential applications" making her skin crawl. If he got this data, if he saw the predictive capability, the associative memory, he wouldn't see a wonder. He would see a weapon. A sensor network that could detect enemy submarines, a system that could forecast tsunamis, a tool for dominance. The thought made her sick.

The final minutes stretched into an eternity. She watched the bar fill, one percent at a time. 89%. 91%. 93%. Each increment was a step closer to freedom, a step closer to the fallout. She thought of her brother, Kai, and his unwavering belief that the ocean had a voice. He would have understood this. He would have seen BR-7 not as a thing to be owned, but as a neighbor to be known.

The transfer complete. The window vanished. The screen returned to its normal server status display. Mara sat frozen for a moment, the silence of the lab suddenly deafening. She ejected the drive, its surface warm from the transfer. She slipped it into her pocket, the cool metal a stark contrast to her heated skin. She stood, her legs stiff, and walked to the door, her movements slow and deliberate. She didn't look back at BR-7. She didn't need to. She knew it was there. She knew it was dreaming. And she had just given its dreams to the world. The progress bar inches forward like a countdown, and Mara prays she can outrun the consequences before anyone notices what she's done.
