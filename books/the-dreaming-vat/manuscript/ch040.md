Chapter 40 — Escape Hatch

"…monetize," Mara finished the sentence, her voice low but cutting through the sudden silence that had fallen over the lab. The air, already thick with the scent of salt and ozone from the running equipment, now felt charged with an unspoken tension. Halvorsen's gaze hardened, her professional mask slipping just enough to reveal a flicker of something Mara couldn't quite name—annoyance, perhaps, or a deeper, more proprietary hurt.

"Mara, this isn't about you and your theories about emergent consciousness in a vat of algae," Halvorsen said, her tone clipped. "This is about the real-world application of a breakthrough that could revolutionize marine restoration. Think of the reefs we could save, the ecosystems we could rebuild."

"And who gets to decide how that rebuilding happens? Who profits when the patents are filed and the licensing deals are signed?" Junie spoke up, her bioethicist's hat firmly on. She hadn't moved from her position by the whiteboard, but her posture was ramrod straight. "Dr. Halvorsen, with all due respect, the moment we sign a partnership with Meridian, we abdicate our responsibility. This entity, whatever it is, becomes a product."

"It's a strain of diatoms, Junie. A very clever, very useful strain of diatoms," Halvorsen countered, rounding on her. "We are not talking about a sentient being. We are talking about a biological tool. And tools are made to be used."

The telemetry console between them hummed, a low, steady sound that usually felt like the lab's heartbeat. Now it felt like a countdown. BR-7, the dreaming vat, pulsed with a soft, rhythmic blue light from its bioluminescent sensors, a silent witness to the human drama unfolding around its glass walls. Mara felt a strange kinship with the contained system. Both of them were being observed, their outputs measured, their futures being negotiated without their consent.

"Theo?" Halvorsen turned to the engineer, seeking an ally. "You're the one who built this rig. You know the practicalities. The funding we need for the next phase, the deep-sea array to map the signals in the bay—it's not going to grow on these kelp fronds."

Theo, who had been studying a readout on his tablet, looked up. His usual easygoing demeanor was replaced by a thoughtful gravity. "I built the rig to observe, Ingrid. To listen. The funding is a means to an end, not the end itself. And I'm not sure Meridian's end is the same as ours. Their reputation precedes them. I've read their white papers on microbial patenting. They don't just fund research; they acquire it."

The argument hung in the air, a tangled net of idealism and pragmatism. Mara watched Halvorsen, seeing the familiar set of her jaw, the way her eyes darted toward the main console as if calculating something. It was the same look she'd had three years ago when she'd insisted on cutting corners to meet a grant deadline, the same look that had led to the autoclave failure and the unreported release of the SY-7 strain into the bay. The memory, a cold stone in Mara's gut, had resurfaced with new, terrifying implications. The network beneath the Choir Field wasn't an escape; it was a homecoming. And they were about to sell the keys to the house.

"I need to think," Halvorsen said finally, the fight draining out of her replaced by a weary resolve. "This is bigger than any of us. I'm going to my office. I expect you all to continue your work in the meantime. Keep an open mind." She didn't look at any of them as she walked out, the soft click of the door echoing in the silence she left behind.

The moment she was gone, the tension in the room fractured. Junie let out a breath she'd been holding. Theo slumped back against the console, rubbing his temples.

"She's not going to let this go," Junie murmured. "Krebs will be here next week with a team of lawyers and a checkbook the size of a whale."

"And a non-disclosure agreement thicker than a marine biology textbook," Theo added. "They'll want everything. The raw data, the protocols, the culture samples. They'll lock it down tighter than a submarine hatch."

Mara felt a cold certainty settle over her. They couldn't let that happen. The discovery wasn't just theirs; it belonged to the bay, to the network that had been forming in the dark for years. It belonged to the ocean. And the ocean, unlike a corporate entity, didn't patent its memories.

"I have to get the data out," Mara said, the words coming out as a whisper, then strengthening with resolve. "All of it. The telemetry, the metabolite correlations, the optical density logs. Everything."

Junie's eyes widened. "Mara, that's… that's insane. Halvorsen would have your PhD revoked. You'd be blacklisted from every research institution on the continent."

"Maybe," Mara said, her gaze fixed on BR-7. The vat pulsed gently, its light a soft, rhythmic beat. It felt like a heartbeat. Or a warning. "But if this thing, whatever it is, has a memory, if it's remembering the bay, then that memory belongs to the water, not to Cerulean Biosciences or Meridian Institute. It belongs to the kelp, to the currents, to the things that live in the dark."

Theo pushed off the console, his expression serious. "And how, exactly, do you propose to do that? We're talking about terabytes of data. You can't just email it from a government computer."

"I know," Mara said, her mind already racing. She thought of the open-science collective she'd been in contact with, a loose network of researchers who believed data should be free. They had a secure, decentralized server, a ghost in the machine that no corporation or government could easily touch. "I need a physical drive. And I need to get it out of the station without anyone knowing."

The plan formed in her mind with terrifying clarity. The station's external courier service, FedEx, came twice a day. A pickup at 4 PM, a delivery at 6 PM. The drop box was just outside the main doors, unguarded. She could copy the data to a portable drive, seal it in a nondescript package, and drop it in the box before the 4 PM pickup. It would be routed through a dozen distribution centers before anyone at the station would even notice it was gone.

The logistics were simple. The moral calculus was what kept her awake at night. Betraying her advisor, jeopardizing her career, breaking the law. All for a microbe in a jar that might not even be aware it was being saved.

She looked at her friends. Junie, with her unwavering moral compass, and Theo, with his technical genius. They were her accomplices now, whether they knew it or not.

"I need your help," she said, her voice steady. "Theo, you can get me a high-capacity drive, one that won't raise any alarms if someone checks the inventory. And Junie… you need to distract Halvorsen this afternoon. Something important. A call from a funding agency, a question about a paper, anything to keep her in her office and away from the lab."

Junie nodded slowly, her jaw set. "I can do that. I'll tell her I have an urgent call from the NSF about a grant we applied for last year. Something about ethical oversight of novel organisms. It should scare her enough to keep her occupied."

"And I'll 'borrow' a drive from the surplus equipment," Theo said, a grim smile touching his lips. "No one will miss a 4-terabyte solid-state. I'll have it wiped and ready for you by lunch."

The plan was set. Mara felt a surge of adrenaline mixed with a profound sense of dread. She was about to commit an act of digital piracy, an act of betrayal. But looking at BR-7, at the gentle, rhythmic pulse of its bioluminescence, she knew she had no choice. This wasn't just about data anymore. It was about a promise. A promise to the brother she'd lost, who had taught her that the ocean had a voice if you knew how to listen. A promise to the bay itself, to the silent network that remembered what humanity had forgotten.

She spent the rest of the morning in a state of heightened awareness, every nerve ending tingling. She worked at her console, monitoring the dream cycles, pretending to be absorbed in the data while her mind was elsewhere. The minutes crawled by. At noon, Theo slipped her a slim, black drive, cool and heavy in her palm. She slipped it into her pocket, the hard edge pressing against her thigh. It felt like a weapon, or a key.

At 1:30 PM, Junie made her move. Mara watched from the lab doorway as Junie intercepted Halvorsen in the hall, her expression urgent. Halvorsen's face tightened, and she nodded, turning toward her office. Mara slipped back into the lab, her heart hammering against her ribs.

The next two hours were a blur of focused activity. She connected an external drive to her terminal, a process she'd done a hundred times before. But this time, it felt different, illicit. She began the massive data transfer, a cascade of ones and zeros flowing from the station's secure server to the drive in her pocket. The progress bar crept across the screen, agonizingly slow. Every footstep in the hallway made her flinch. Every creak of the old building sounded like a warning.

She was almost done. The final gigabytes were transferring, a tiny sliver of the bar left to go. She had the package ready—a plain cardboard box, addressed to a non-existent address in Canada, a decoy to throw off any suspicion. The real drive was already in her pocket. All she had to do was copy the FedEx receipt from the printer, tape it to the box, and drop it in the outside drop box.

Just then, the lab door opened with a soft whoosh. Mara's blood ran cold. Halvorsen stood there, her expression unreadable. She hadn't gone to her office after all. She'd come back.

"Mara," Halvorsen said, her voice quiet. She took a step into the lab, her gaze sweeping over the equipment, settling on Mara's console. "I just had a feeling. Something's… different."

Mara's fingers froze over the keyboard. The transfer was 99% complete. She could feel the heat of the drive in her pocket, a live coal against her thigh. She turned slowly, forcing her expression into a mask of calm professionalism.

"Feeling about what, Ingrid?" she asked, her voice steady, though it felt like sandpaper in her throat.

Halvorsen's eyes narrowed, not at Mara, but at the console. At the flickering light of the external drive. "About this. About what we discussed. About Meridian. I was just in my office, looking over the preliminary partnership documents. And I realized something. I've been thinking about this all wrong."

Mara's heart hammered against her ribs. The transfer finished. A small pop-up window appeared on her screen: Transfer Complete. She didn't dare move to close it.

"I've been thinking about ownership," Halvorsen continued, taking another step closer. "About who gets to claim this discovery. But maybe that's the wrong question. Maybe the question isn't who owns it, but who serves it. Who has the responsibility to see that this… this potential… is used for good."

She stopped a few feet from Mara, her eyes boring into hers. "I was wrong to even consider Meridian. Their motives are too transparent. Too… commercial. This is too important. This belongs to the science. To the truth."

Mara felt a wave of relief so intense it was almost dizzying. Halvorsen hadn't suspected. She was having a crisis of conscience, a change of heart. It was more than Mara had dared hope for.

"I'm glad you see that, Ingrid," Mara said, her voice trembling only slightly. She kept her hands clasped behind her back, hiding the drive in her pocket.

Halvorsen nodded, a flicker of something like shame in her eyes. "I'm going to call Krebs. Tell him the partnership is off. We'll proceed with our own publication. Open access. No patents. The world deserves to see this."

She turned to leave, then paused, her hand on the doorframe. "And Mara? Thank you. For… for pushing me to see it."

The door closed softly behind her. Mara stood frozen for a moment, the adrenaline still coursing through her veins. She had been seconds away from exposure. Seconds from losing everything. And now, Halvorsen was not only calling off the Meridian deal, but she was advocating for open access. The race was over. She had won.

She let out a shaky breath, reaching into her pocket to pull out the drive. It was warm from the transfer, a solid, real thing in her hand. The package was ready. The FedEx receipt was printed. She could do this now, with a clear conscience.

As she turned to grab the package from her desk, her foot caught on a loose floorboard. She stumbled, catching herself on the edge of the console. In her flailing, the receipt she'd printed fluttered from her hand and drifted to the floor.

She bent to pick it up, her fingers brushing against the paper. And then she saw it. Tucked into the corner of the receipt, in small, bold print, was the Cerulean Biosciences logo. The station's courier service was a subsidiary of Cerulean. The package wasn't going to an open-science collective. It was being routed through the corporate headquarters.

The realization hit her like a physical blow. The receipt in her hand wasn't a receipt for her clandestine package. It was a receipt for a routine station supply order she'd processed that morning. The package she was about to send was filled with lab supplies, not data. The real drive was still in her pocket, but the race wasn't over. It had just begun.

Halvorsen's change of heart, her sudden moral clarity—it had been too convenient. Too perfect. The advisor had said she'd had a feeling. A feeling that something had changed. She hadn't been talking about her own conscience. She'd been talking about the lab. About the data.

The FedEx driver's receipt flutters to the floor, and Mara knows the race has begun—between what Cerulean owns and what the ocean remembers.
